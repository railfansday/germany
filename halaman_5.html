South Carolina lawmakers’ proposed online sports-betting framework could eventually generate about $40 million a year, with 5% of revenue earmarked for problem-gambling treatment and prevention. Sports betting remains illegal in the state, so the plan would need to become law before operators could launch under its rules.

The proposal, sponsored in 2025 by Sens. Tom Davis and Matt Leber, would allow as many as eight companies to run online sportsbooks. Its details were reported by the Charleston City Paper and summarized by GamingToday.

Each operator would pay a $100,000 application fee and a $1 million licensing fee. The bill would tax adjusted gross receipts at 12.5%, with state estimates suggesting the market could eventually bring in about $40 million annually.

The proposed revenue split assigns 82% to the state General Fund and 10% to local governments for infrastructure. The South Carolina Department of Mental Health would receive 5% for problem-gambling treatment and prevention, while 3% would cover administration.

A new nine-member Sports Wagering Commission would oversee the market. The dedicated allocation would make treatment and prevention part of the proposal’s funding structure from the outset, rather than leaving those programs to compete for the remaining revenue.

That focus connects to a broader question about how states incorporate gambling disorder into public services. Illinois’ recognition of gambling disorder as a behavioral health condition offers another example of how the issue can be handled within a state system.

The 2018 U.S. Supreme Court decision that opened the door to state-regulated sports betting has since been followed by legalization and taxation in 39 states and Washington, D.C., according to the reporting. South Carolina remains outside that group, although residents are described as using gambling apps, fantasy sports products, prediction markets and offshore sportsbooks.

Several gambling-related bills have appeared in recent South Carolina legislative sessions. A pari-mutuel horse-wagering bill passed the House in 2023 but did not pass the Senate; the Davis-Leber measure is a more comprehensive proposal focused on online sportsbooks.

The Charleston City Paper also cited a New York Federal Reserve report saying states that have not legalized sports betting can bear costs from cross-border wagering without collecting tax revenue. The policy debate therefore involves both potential public revenue and the risks associated with gambling, including the need for prevention and treatment.

North Carolina’s sports-betting expansion provides regional context for South Carolina’s debate over legal wagering and treatment resources. Sports betting in North Carolina has grown under a state-regulated model, while South Carolina’s proposed approach would set its own licensing, tax and funding terms.

Republican nominee Alan Wilson has opposed legalizing casinos, even if voters approved them, but has said he is open to online sports betting with restrictions. His position reflects the view that residents can already place wagers online and that regulation should address that activity.

Democratic nominee Jermaine Johnson supports legalizing and taxing online sports betting only if voters approve it first. He has linked his emphasis on safeguards to gambling addiction in his family and said protections, prevention programs and treatment resources should be in place from the start.

The candidates’ positions leave voter approval and the scope of consumer protections as central questions in any push to legalize. The Davis-Leber proposal remains a plan, not an operating framework; South Carolina’s current ban remains in place unless lawmakers enact a change.

#92. Building A UX ROI Case That Survives The Boardroom

Alex Williams is a seasoned full-stack developer and the former owner of Hosting Data UK. After graduating from the University of London with a Masters Degree … More about Alex ↬

Weekly tips on front-end & UX.Trusted by 182,000+ folks.

Sooner or later, a CFO looks at your wireframes and asks what any of it actually does for the bottom line. Storyboards don’t answer that question, and the era when a 5-minute pitch could answer it ended, unfortunately, a while ago.

These days, if you want to win budget, buy-in, and backing for UX, the design has to be provably good for the business, not only for the people using it.

And proving that takes more than taping a dollar sign to a redesign. You have to understand how your organization defines value in the first place, how it measures that value, and how a credible line gets drawn between a design initiative and an outcome leadership already cares about.

Rather than scatter tips, this article follows one worked example the whole way through. Meridian is a mid-size B2B SaaS company, and it is entirely made up — that label matters, so it gets repeated where it counts. Its onboarding redesign carries the same figures from goal-setting through cost accounting, causal testing, and the final ROI number, because a framework only becomes tangible when the numbers connect. Every step is one you can rerun inside your own organization.

Companies now want clarity on what every dollar buys, and “delightful user experiences” stopped clearing that bar some time ago. I still remember a former colleague celebrating a $1 million redesign he’d gotten greenlit mostly on the strength of a couple of three.js tricks. Try that pitch in front of a finance team today and see how far the particles get you.

Executives don’t hate UX, they just hate vagueness. A pitch built on “users will find it easier” loses, every time, to the department promising 12% more sales in Q3. The difference is the one between a streamlined checkout flow that reduced cart abandonment with completed purchases up 22%, and the same work rewarded with “the QA testers like it.” One of those goes on your resume. The rest of this article is about earning the first version, with Meridian’s numbers doing the work.

Meet Touch Design for Mobile Interfaces, Steven Hoober’s brand-new guide on designing for mobile with proven, universal, human-centric guidelines. 400 pages, jam-packed with in-depth user research and best practices.

Most writing about UX ROI makes a convenient assumption: that the organization already owns clean business goals and KPIs for you to hook your work onto. Real companies are messier than that. Plenty run on ambitions like “grow faster” or “improve the customer journey” that nobody ever broke into anything measurable, and an ROI case built on that ambiguity sounds impressive right up until somebody scrutinizes it.

So the first job is often to help the organization define what success even looks like. Interview stakeholders across departments — what does product consider a good quarter, where does customer success watch users struggle, where do sales deals stall — and listen for the themes that keep resurfacing across conversations, because those recurring themes are the company’s latent business objectives. A useful forcing function is the OKR model (Objectives and Key Results), which doesn’t tolerate vagueness.

At Meridian, the stated ambition was “improve the rate of new users’ adoption of the platform,” which you can neither design toward nor measure against. Interviews turned up the real shape of the problem. Trial users needed a median of 14 days to reach first value, most churned before getting there, and onboarding questions were burying the support queue. Out of that came an OKR with actual edges: reduce median time-to-first-value from 14 days to 7 with the use of a guided setup flow, and lift trial-to-paid conversion from 8% to 9.5%.

One warning about formalizing KPIs: Impose them from inside the UX team and leadership will suspect you’ve rigged the field in your own favor, so co-create them with whoever owns the outcome — though never at the price of accepting targets that set your team up for an uncomfortable situation. Meridian’s head of product agreed that setup-completion rate was a fair proxy for onboarding usability, and customer success signed off on time-to-first-value, a number already sitting on their own dashboard.

ROI has a denominator, and the denominator is where most UX teams go wrong. You can’t calculate a return without strategic financial planning, yet cost usually gets counted as designer salaries or consulting hours and nothing else. A finance team will find the rest whether or not you counted it, so count it first.

Direct costs are the visible ones. Meridian’s redesign ran $45,000 in design and research labor plus another $8,000 in tooling and participant incentives. Licenses for Figma, UserTesting, Hotjar, analytics platforms, research incentive spend — all of it belongs in the total, and that’s before the inevitable instances of vendor lock-in every UX team eventually faces. Engineering sits in the same column, because a UX redesign doesn’t stop at the mockup. Building the guided setup took two frontend sprints plus a QA pass, $38,000 worth, and the project generated about $4,000 of coordination overhead along the way in new syncs and shared dashboards.

The line item nearly everyone misses, and the one worth stealing from this article if you steal nothing else, is stakeholder time. Workshops, design reviews, and feedback sessions all pull senior people away from their primary work. A VP of Product spending four hours a week in UX reviews is a VP not spending those hours on roadmap planning or partner negotiations. Log the attendance — who came, for how long, at what seniority — and price it at fully loaded cost, meaning salary plus benefits divided by productive hours. A quarter’s worth of workshops, reviews, and interviews at Meridian priced out at $22,000.

Add it all up: $45,000 in design labor, $8,000 in tooling, $38,000 in engineering, $22,000 in stakeholder time, $4,000 in coordination. The investment is $117,000. Saying that number out loud beats saying “we spent $45K on design,” precisely because it already includes everything a finance team would have dug up on its own.

Most UX ROI pitches die right here. Conversions rose after the redesign, sure — and the CFO wants to know how you ruled out the new pricing, the seasonal traffic bump, and the marketing campaign that shipped the same week. Without a convincing answer, your entire ROI story crumbles.

After all these years, the gold standard for proving causation is still A/B testing: run the old experience against the new one on an even traffic split until the sample means something.

Onboarding happens to suit a phased rollout, which is why Meridian could do this cleanly. For eight weeks, half of new trial signups received the redesigned guided setup while half stayed on the legacy flow. Control converted to paid at 8.0%. The variant came in at 9.4%. With roughly 6,100 trials inside the window, the difference was statistically significant, but a 1.4-point gap on a single test is still the kind of result that deserves a second look before anyone builds a budget on it, which is one reason the team held back on attribution below. Where a split isn’t feasible — a change too structural, a user base too small — fall back to a time series instead. Measure steadily for weeks before the change, implement, then keep measuring against the baseline you established.

Documenting whatever else happens around the same time as your UX change is the unglamorous half of causation.

A pricing-page test from Meridian’s marketing team overlapped weeks five through eight of the rollout. The UX team noted it, confirmed it hit both cohorts evenly, and still chose to attribute only 70% of the observed lift to the redesign in the final math. There is no formula that produces that number; treat it as an illustrative assumption for this example.

The team asked how much of the lift could plausibly belong to the pricing test if it had helped one cohort slightly more than the other, settled on a ceiling of about a third, and rounded the redesign’s share down to 70%. Your figure will differ. What matters is that it is written down and argued for before the results arrive, not fitted to them afterwards. That restraint is worth money in a skeptical room. “We attribute roughly 70% of the lift to the onboarding change, with the remainder likely influenced by concurrent pricing work” survives cross-examination; claiming everything does not. Cohort analysis then backed the number up, since the lift held across acquisition channels and tenure bands, and at that point the skeptics had very little left to work with.

Leading and lagging indicators belong on the same slide, because each covers the other’s weakness. Meridian’s leading indicators moved first — setup completion climbed from 62% to 89%, median time-to-first-value dropped from 14 days to 6.5 — and the lagging trial-to-paid number followed. Mechanism first, business outcome second. Presented together, they form a causal chain that’s harder to poke holes in than either one alone.

So what did Meridian actually earn? The company sees about 40,000 trial signups a year. Lifting conversion from 8.0% to 9.4% adds roughly 560 paying customers annually, and at an average of $1,800 in annual recurring revenue per account, those customers represent about $1,008,000 in new ARR. Applying the conservative 70% attribution from the causal work trims the defensible figure to roughly $706,000.

Set that against the full $117,000 investment and the first-year ROI lands near 5:1, with payback arriving in roughly two months. There’s a second line, too. Onboarding-related support tickets dropped about 30%, some 3,600 fewer tickets a year, worth another $54,000 annually at $15 per resolved ticket. Keep it as its own line rather than folding it into one swollen headline number. The case reads as more honest that way and loses none of its force.

Three assumptions carry that result, and each belongs on the slide next to it. The 40,000 signups and the $1,800 average ARR are the prior year’s actuals held flat, so a growth or pricing change moves the outcome in either direction. The 70% attribution is the illustrative assumption from the causal work, not a measured quantity. And the two-month payback counts new ARR as it lands rather than revenue recognized net of churn, which flatters the timeline; on a net basis the payback stretches to roughly a quarter. State those three plainly and a finance team can adapt the example to its own numbers. Hide them and the whole thing starts to look like marketing math, however careful the experiment was.

What persuades in the final presentation is not sophistication. Open with the baseline: what stalled trials and support volume were already costing. Show the delta in terms leadership reads fluently, metrics like conversion rate uplift chief among them. A chart of setup completion climbing from 62% to 89% will beat a paragraph of UX jargon, and a translation like “each abandoned setup costs us 0.3 support tickets” beats the chart. Above all, keep every figure identical from the first slide to the last. A room full of finance people forgives many things, but never numbers that wobble between slides.

Budget decisions come out of coalitions. A CFO may hold the final say when adding AI to the checkout process, but marketing, product, and customer success all lean on that decision, and each means something different by “value.”

A CFO hears cost, revenue, and risk. A CMO hears conversion and acquisition cost, since UX is a lever for increasing marketing ROI. Product counts support tickets; customer success thinks in retention. The underlying numbers never change; only the framing rotates, and a CFO wants a projection, not a moodboard. Meridian’s CFO slide read “the onboarding redesign protects roughly $706,000 in new ARR a year against a $117,000 investment,” while the CMO version led with what a 9.4% trial conversion does to blended acquisition cost.

Some UX outcomes never translate cleanly into revenue, and pretending they do weakens the parts of your case that are solid.

Scores like Net Promoter Score (NPS), CSAT, and Customer Effort Score already sit inside most reporting cadences, which makes them cheap to borrow. Tie your work to their movement, and segment wherever the data allows.

Meridian could say that NPS among trial users on the redesigned onboarding was 51 against 34 for the legacy flow, which lands far harder than any blended average. Verbatim feedback from surveys, support transcripts, and app store reviews adds the emotional weight the scores lack. Internal tools deserve the same discipline, since employee experience is increasingly recognized as a business driver — a dashboard redesign that hands account managers 45 minutes a day back is a productivity gain, a satisfaction gain, and a retention lever all in one.

Brand perception resists direct measurement but leaves tracks in repeat visits, organic referrals, and social sentiment. It carries extra weight in trust-sensitive industries like finance or healthcare, and it forms fast, given that UX design influences the first impressions of a whopping 94% of customers.

Whatever you collect, systematize the collecting. Run pre- and post-surveys with consistent question sets, use structured usability testing with task-based scoring, and put the qualitative right next to the quantitative when you present.

UX loses the budget battle unless it’s mapped to company-wide objectives, so phrase the proposal in the words of this year’s board presentation.

Nobody at Meridian pitched “simplify the onboarding UI”; the pitch was a redesigned trial experience worth 1.4 points of upgrade rate, roughly $1M in annual recurring revenue before attribution. Bring evidence in both registers, since that is what social proof is for: the case, clearly labeled, plus screenshots, impact graphs, and user quotes. Find internal allies who can repeat the ROI narrative in rooms you’ll never enter, and write the playbook down, because repeatable ROI is what earns recurring investment.

A seat at the table never comes from beauty or novelty. It comes from measurable, defensible impact, which means UX leaders have to trade the artist’s posture for the strategist’s. Speak in outcomes rather than outputs. Connect pixels to profit.

When someone challenges the numbers, don’t flinch. Show the controlled experiment, the cohort analysis, and the before-and-after metrics, every figure holding steady from the first slide to the last, the way Meridian’s did, with the customer quotes and the employee-satisfaction data sitting right beside the revenue impact. Prove the work does more than delight users, and be ready to defend the ratio line by line. That’s when the CFO leans in, and that’s when design stops being optional.

#93. How Wikipedia’s Editors Keep the Online Encyclopedia Human-Written

AI-generated text is harder to spot, but that hasn’t deterred them

Eliza Strickland is IEEE Spectrum's features editor. She also covers AI and biomedical engineering.

Ellia Lebleu is a cofounder of the WikiProject AI Cleanup effort to keep Wikipedia human-written.

Ellia Lebleu is a founding member of the WikiProject AI Cleanup, a volunteer effort launched in 2023 to find and remove AI-generated material from Wikipedia that breaks the website’s guidelines. When the cleanup began, AI writing was often betrayed by conspicuous clichés. Today, the project’s volunteers are developing tools to detect subtler forms of machine-generated content and debating AI’s role in the world’s most widely used encyclopedia.

When did you realize Wikipedia had a problem with AI-generated material?

Ellia Lebleu: It was around December 2023. A few other editors and I were chatting on Wikipedia’s Discord server, and we had all seen very strange articles with the same odd characteristics. They repeated phrases like “stands as a testament” or “rich cultural heritage.” They sometimes had conclusion sections, or sections called “Why is this important?” That was very typical of ChatGPT in 2023 and early 2024 but very odd for Wikipedia articles—and very easy to spot. Then I decided, let’s create an official project on Wikipedia so more people can deal with this.

How do editors spot AI-generated writing today?

Lebleu: By 2025, the signs of AI writing were becoming subtler and more structural. For example, AI loves the “rule of three,” negative parallelisms, and other structures of speech that aren’t conclusive by themselves but become meaningful as they accumulate. We also look beyond the article. If someone writes five articles of thousands of words each in 5 minutes, there is reason to suspect AI.

Does the AI problem just appear in the main text, or also in citations?

Lebleu: Fake or misleading citations have become a major worry. Sometimes an AI hallucinates a citation entirely, although that’s much rarer today because of retrieval-augmented generation that allows the AI to find sources. More often, it finds a real source but gets its content completely wrong. AI citations that don’t actually support what is being written are extremely commonplace.

English Wikipedia recently prohibited editors from using LLMs to generate articles. How much AI-generated content is still getting through?

Lebleu: I’ve been working with [AI-detection company] Pangram to estimate the proportion of AI-generated content. I tested it on Wikipedia content from before ChatGPT and found no false positives, so its results provide a very conservative lower bound. In late 2025, it flagged perhaps 20 percent of new articles and drafts as AI-generated. In early 2026, that went down to 15 or 16 percent. But we don’t yet know whether that’s because our recent policies have had an effect or because models are getting more powerful and Pangram hasn’t had time to adapt.

Lebleu: Another editor from the project has made an AI source verifier, which goes through the sources on a page and checks whether they match the claims. Since it is itself an AI model, there is no guarantee that it will be correct every time. But if you want something that works at scale and filters out obvious cases, it’s a good first step. The Wikimedia Foundation is also working on a tool called Paste Check, which can flag text pasted directly from some AI chatbots.

And we’ve seen Claude sometimes say, “I can’t write a Wikipedia article; it would go against Wikipedia’s policies.” If all major AI companies implemented that kind of prompt refusal, it wouldn’t solve the problem entirely, because there are still open-weight models that can be tweaked to avoid restrictions. But it would help with people who use commercial models and aren’t especially familiar with the technology.

Can you tell if the rule prohibiting AI writing is working?

Lebleu: It’s still a little early to tell. We see possible effects, such as prompt refusal and the apparent slight drop in AI-generated content. But again, we don’t yet know if that is because models are getting better or because beginners are learning the rules. The vast majority of AI-generated content is submitted by beginner editors, so we’ve made many efforts to make the rule very visible in welcome messages, editing help, and advice.

What if AI becomes capable of writing truly reliable Wikipedia articles?

Lebleu: That’s one of the big questions we’re facing. When the guideline was passed, it was for very practical reasons: We were flooded with AI content, we didn’t have good tools, and we needed to put a stop to it. But since then, it has taken on a more philosophical dimension. Many people have called Wikipedia “the last good place on the internet.” Being a place that was, in theory, free from AI slop has become part of Wikipedia’s identity.

A lot of Wikipedia’s communication is now centered on the idea that we’re a human-written encyclopedia. Even AI companies find that interesting. It can be valuable to have a purely human-written corpus on which to [train] AI systems. The fact that Wikipedia is still that, and will remain that for at least the near future, makes it a precious resource that isn’t replicated anywhere else.

But will the people who read and contribute to Wikipedia want it to become partially AI-generated if that makes it more comprehensive? We could preserve Wikipedia’s identity as a human-written corpus while also offering an AI-assisted option.

Has Wikipedia reckoned with the idea of “Google Zero”—a possible future in which websites no longer get traffic from Google search?

Lebleu: If everything goes through AI search and websites plummet to zero visibility, how do we expose Wikipedia and show that it can be edited and that people can still contribute? Wikipedia is frequently cited in [Google’s] AI Overviews, but users may only see an AI answer citing Wikipedia. They may not realize that Wikipedia is a living project they can edit and contribute to.

The Wikimedia Foundation is working on an attribution framework that could give AI users more information about the article [being cited]: when it was last edited, how comprehensive it is, whether editors have flagged problems, and perhaps even a call to action. In the future, readers might even contribute through AI models: “The AI cited Wikipedia, but there seems to be an error. Can I make an edit request and add this other source I found?”

I can see a very strong symbiotic relationship between Wikipedia and AI models in the future. I don’t see Wikipedia disappearing, but I can see it taking a very different shape. Yesterday, AI was something to clean up. Today, it’s a tool to help us with the cleanup. Tomorrow, it will be the air we breathe.

#94. Blocked aria-hidden: The Warning is Right, and Every Fix You’ve Found is Wrong

You closed a dialog and the console went that particular shade of angry mustard. You highlighted the message, dropped it into a search box, and it spat you out here along with half the front-end internet, because this exact string turns up identically whether you’re on Angular, Bootstrap, Ionic, or phpMyAdmin.

Here’s the part the top results bury: the warning is correct. There’s a real person on the other side of it — someone using a screen reader whose focus is about to drop into a hole in your page.

And the fixes ranking above me right now all do the same thing under different names: the blur() one-liner, the setTimeout you wrap the close in, the trick where you yank the aria-hidden attribute off.

Each one quiets the console while quietly hurting the person the browser was trying to protect. If you’ve already shipped one of them, you’re in enormous company. You were failed by your search results, not careless.

One honest shortcut before you read another word: if you can migrate to the native

element and call .showModal(), do that, close this tab, and get your afternoon back, because the browser runs the entire focus dance for you and this class of bug all but disappears. (You’ll still want to handle the case where the element focus should return to has been removed from the DOM, which no browser can guess for you.) Everything past here is for the rest of us, wired into a component library or a design system we can’t tear out this quarter.

The whole thing fits in one sentence, and if it’s the only sentence you read you’ll still be ahead of most of what ranks above me: focus has to leave a region before that region becomes hidden or inert. That’s it. Everything else here is footnotes, edge cases, and the story of how I learned it the expensive way.

In practice it’s an order of operations. Most modal code already has the right pieces, just in the wrong sequence — the fix is purely a reordering, and the two versions below show it directly, with the one step nearly everyone skips (inert-ing the closing overlay itself) called out in the comments.

The wrong version isn’t wrong because someone was careless. It reads top-to-bottom exactly the way you’d narrate closing a modal out loud.

But the browser applies that hide the moment the statement runs, before the focus move on the next line even happens. It’s all one synchronous task. The damage is in the ordering and the invalid state that exists between those two statements, not a literal gap in time.

Get the order right and the result is boring — which is exactly the point. The user hits Esc, hears focus land back on the button they opened the thing with, and carries on.

Get it wrong and they land on , hear silence or just the page title, and have to Tab from the top of a long page all the way back to wherever they’d been. That second experience is what the warning exists to prevent, and it’s the one blur() hands the user every time.

Let’s talk about the traps you’ve probably already got open in other tabs: the blur() one-liner, the setTimeout shim, stripping aria-hidden, and modal={false} on Radix or shadcn. I’ll take each apart properly in a bit, because every one is a reasonable-looking mistake, not a dumb one.

That warning isn’t Chrome nagging you about a style-guide nicety. It’s the browser telling on your architecture.

You read the word “warning” and filed it where you file the rest of the console: yellow, non-blocking, someone else’s problem, deal with it after the release. I did the same thing.

That word is doing a lot of damage, because it tells you this is advisory — and it isn’t. By the time you see the message, the browser has looked at your markup, decided you were wrong, and shipped a different accessibility tree than the one you wrote.

Open the modal that triggers it and look at the Elements panel. Your aria-hidden="true" is right there on the background wrapper, untouched. Nothing in the DOM inspector is a lie.

Now switch to the Accessibility panel and look at the tree Chrome actually handed the operating system’s screen-reader APIs. The subtree you told it to hide is still there, still exposed, still fully readable.

Blink read your attribute, saw the focused node living inside that subtree, and walked back up the focused node’s ancestor chain, ignoring your aria-hidden the whole way. The moment focus leaves, the pruning snaps back and the region hides like you asked.

So the state you think you shipped — the one where that region is invisible to assistive tech — is not the state any screen reader receives. It exists only in your Elements panel and your head.

That gap between the two panels is the whole bug, and it comes from a paradox baked into aria-hidden.

The attribute pulls content out of the accessibility tree. It does not pull that content out of keyboard focus order. Two different systems, nothing keeping them in sync.

So an element can be fully focusable and completely imperceptible at once. The instant Tab lands on it, you’ve created what I’ve come to call ghost focus: the screen reader fires a focus event for a node it’s been told doesn’t exist, looks it up, finds nothing it’s allowed to describe, and says nothing.

Sit with what that’s like from the other side of the screen. Someone pressed Tab. Focus moved, a control lit up, and their screen reader went silent. Not “button, dialog.” Not the field label. Silence.

They pressed a key, the machine acknowledged nothing, and now they don’t know if the app broke, if their assistive tech crashed, or if they did something wrong. They’re standing in the middle of a room the map insists isn’t there, and the only way out is to keep tabbing blind and hope something eventually speaks.

That’s what Chrome prevents when it overrules you. Left alone, aria-hidden over a focused control doesn’t hide anything kindly; it hides the labels and keeps the focus, which is the worst of both.

Chromium has been quietly patching this for far longer than the warning’s been around. As far as I can reconstruct, the tree got loud in two installments, and I’m piecing the timing together from when bug reports clustered rather than from a changelog I can point at cleanly.

The open-time variant — the one that scolds you about an element that “just received focus” — shows up across trackers around Chrome 127 in summer 2024, clustering in July and August across MUI #43106, Ant Design #50170, and Flowbite #943.

The close-time variant, the “retained focus” wording, arrives months later around Chrome 131 in late 2024. The person who filed Bootstrap #41005 on November 5 caught it live, noting it showed in the 131 Beta and Nightly builds but not stable yet, and Angular #30187 in December matches. Two waves, one behavior underneath.

That behavior is old. Chromium was already exposing focusable aria-hidden nodes back in early 2020. We know this because ARIA WG issue #1185 records Chrome accessibility engineer Aaron Leventhal proposing exactly that, so users could “at least hear where they are tabbing to, instead of complete silence.”

The pattern he was defending against goes back further, to teams slapping aria-hidden on or a giant wrapper when a modal opened. Through portal and markup mistakes, this sometimes hid the modal too, locking a screen reader out of the whole page. You can watch people argue about that failure as far back as Bootstrap #29769 in 2019.

The honest framing: your teardown code was broken years before any of this reached your console. The repair was happening silently the whole time, which is exactly why nobody fixed it.

Firefox and Safari don’t surface a comparable console warning for this, as far as I’ve been able to test; whatever each engine does about focused content inside a hidden subtree, it does without telling you. Chrome decided to make you feel it, and I think that call was right, whatever you make of the tone. A silent fix lets broken code ship forever, because the browser papering over your mistake is indistinguishable, from your seat, from your code being correct. Loud is uncomfortable and loud is honest.

If Chrome is repairing your tree, the question stops being “how do I make the message go away?” and becomes “at what exact instant does my code produce a focused node inside a hidden region?” It turns out there are four distinct instants, each with its own shape.

Every one of these ends at the same place: focus sitting inside a region that just went hidden. But they arrive from four different directions, and if you don’t know which one you’re looking at you’ll apply the wrong fix and either not silence the warning or silence it by breaking something worse. Here’s the map I wish I’d had, sorted roughly by how many of you are living each one.

Before the deep dives, here’s the quick triage so you can find yours. If the warning fires when you close a modal, during the fade, you’ve got the close-time race. If it fires the instant a modal opens, it’s the open-time inversion. If it involves a , because of course you do, it’s a form. The user opens the closes. And now two components that each believe they’re the one true modal layer are fighting over who gets to hide the rest of the page, because both ship the same hide-others logic and neither knows the other exists.

This class is here to prove a point rather than be solved: if the maintainers of the browser can’t keep their own widgets clean, the problem is architectural, not a skill issue on your team.

Look at the four side-by-side and the shared shape is impossible to miss. Close-time, open-time, turf war, or a user tabbing away, in every case a region became hidden while focus was still doing business inside it. Four costumes, one bug. Which raises the only question that matters next: what did the Internet tell everyone to do about it?

Type the warning into a search box and the same answer floats to the top of nearly every result. One line, drop it in your modal’s close handler, warning gone. It works in the sense that matters least: the console quiets and CI goes green. Here’s what it actually does.

Focus doesn’t go somewhere sensible when you call blur() with nothing after it. It goes nowhere. The browser has to put focus somewhere, so it falls back to , the DOM equivalent of setting a passenger down in the middle of the highway and driving off. The warning clears because there’s no longer a focused element inside your hidden subtree, no meaningfully focused element at all.

For a mouse user, this fix is invisible. For a keyboard or screen reader, the reader typically goes quiet or reads something useless like the page title (the exact announcement varies by screen reader and browser), and the very next Tab press restarts from the top of the entire page. That’s a straightforward WCAG 2.4.3 failure, dressed up as a fix, since focus is supposed to return somewhere logical — canonically the control that opened the thing.

To be precise about the villain: it isn’t the blur() call itself, it’s blurring to nowhere. blur() immediately followed by a deliberate trigger.focus() is just a clumsy spelling of the correct move. It’s leaving focus stranded on that does the damage.

I know how appealing that one-liner is, because I shipped it.

Late 2024, a release crunch, the warning suddenly flooding our console across half the modals in the app after a Chrome update we hadn’t been watching. It was loud, it was everywhere, it blocked nothing, and it made the console unreadable during a week when we needed the console.

I found the Stack Overflow answer in about ninety seconds. One line, into a global hide handler so it covered every modal at once.

The red went away. Tests passed. I remember the specific small satisfaction of a one-line fix that clears an entire category of noise, and I closed the ticket and moved on and felt, honestly, a little clever about it.

Months later I sat in on a usability session, one of those where you’re mostly there to take notes and stay out of the way. A screen reader user was working through one of our flows and closed a modal — a normal one, nothing special, one of the ones my one-liner covered.

The reader went quiet. Then they pressed Tab to get back to what they’d been doing, and I watched them ride focus down from the top of the page, through the header, through the nav, stop after stop, narrating each one, working back to the spot the modal had sent them away from.

It took a while. Nobody in the room but me knew why it was happening.

The warning I’d silenced had been the only voice in the building speaking for that user, and I’d put my hand over its mouth to make my console tidy. That’s the whole thing. That’s why this article exists.

I’m not going to sit in that feeling, because sitting in it fixes no one’s modal. The useful part isn’t the guilt, it’s the mechanism: I’d treated a warning about a user as a warning about my logs, and every fix I reached for optimized the logs. Once you see that, the rest of the folk remedies sort themselves by the same tell.

The timing hacks are second most popular, and subtler, because they sometimes work. The move is to wrap open or close in a setTimeout or requestAnimationFrame so focus restoration happens a tick later, after the hide has settled.

Community advice floating around the shadcn threads is remarkably honest about what you’re buying: roughly, add a delay, you’ll still see the warning sometimes, but mostly it works (shadcn #5953 has this exact resigned energy).

That “mostly” is the problem. A timer bets the paint finishes before the focus call runs, and on a fast machine with a warm cache you win most of the time. Under CPU load, on a cheap Android, or under React’s concurrent rendering where the scheduler slices your work apart, you lose.

When you lose, the broken hidden-with-focus state ships anyway, now intermittently, which is worse, because it won’t reproduce on your machine. You’ve added latency to every close for every user to sometimes-not-fix a problem for the few. Those flickering half-committed states are the kind of thing WCAG 4.1.2 exists to rule out.

Stripping the attribute is the next tier down, and it looks the most like addressing the root cause while doing the opposite. Some people delete aria-hidden from their markup; the more determined wire up a MutationObserver to yank it off every time the library sets it.

The warning genuinely goes away, because now nothing is hidden. That’s the trouble: while your modal is open, the entire background page is handed back to the screen reader. The user can Tab out of the active modal and into background controls, even though the modal is supposed to be the only part of the page they can operate. That breaks the basic modal contract and creates an illogical focus order — a WCAG 2.4.3 (Focus Order) failure.

Then there’s modal={false}, the Radix and shadcn escape hatch. Setting this turns the component into a legitimate non-modal dialog — a real, valid pattern in its own right. The problem is using it specifically to silence the warning while keeping the visual backdrop of a blocking modal. You give users something that looks modal but doesn’t behave modally, clearing the warning by removing the focus trap entirely.

Radix #3811 documents where that lands: in Safari, focus tabs its way right out of the non-modal dialog, and Radix, seeing focus leave, reads it as an outside interaction and closes the dialog on the user, mid-form. You’ve silenced a console warning by shipping a dialog that dismisses itself while someone’s filling it in.

Two more deserve exactly one sentence each. The temporary tabindex="-1" on followed by a focus dump is blur() with extra ceremony and the identical result. Filtering the message out of your console output is the software equivalent of taping over the check-engine light and enjoying the quieter dashboard.

None of this is the fault of the people who posted these answers, and correct guidance does exist: Scott O’Hara has written carefully about inert for years, and MDN’s

documentation lays out the right model.

It just doesn’t rank when you’re panicking at 4:00 p.m. with a red console and a deploy window. The good material is quiet and the bad material is optimized, and that asymmetry is the actual villain.

So here’s the thesis the whole piece hangs on: a clean console was never the goal. You can drive this warning to zero and, at every step down that road, make your product measurably worse for the exact people the warning was put there to protect. The warning is a proxy. The user is the target.

Which means the real fix has to satisfy both at once, the browser and the person, and that comes down to doing four things in a specific order.

The rule the whole fix reduces to is short enough to keep in your head. On close, focus leaves the closing region before that region is hidden, and it must land somewhere real, never on an inert node and never on .

That second half has a consequence people trip on. inert blocks focus. So if your trigger sits inside the background you inert-ed on open, you have to lift that inert before you can focus the trigger at all, or .focus() is a silent no-op and focus stays stranded inside the dialog.

(If your trigger lives outside the inerted region, the first two steps commute and you’ll see the focus-first order in some codebases. Keeping background-first is the safe default because it works either way.)

There’s a corollary at open time that most people already do by instinct: capture the return target, document.activeElement, before you move focus into the dialog. Once focus is inside, the thing you wanted to return to is gone.

The step I want to single out is the third, because it’s the one I got wrong for months after I thought I’d learned my lesson. The common advice, once you get past blur(), is “just restore focus first.” That’s necessary and not sufficient.

Restore focus to the trigger and you’ve cleared the warning, but the overlay is still fading out for another 200 milliseconds, still in the DOM, still focusable and still in the accessibility tree while it fades. A screen reader can catch it on the way down; VoiceOver’s cursor will touch that ghost content if you let it.

Inert-ing the dying shell is what actually closes the hole, and it’s the honest answer to the question everyone asks first, which is how to animate the thing out without the warning coming back. You don’t suppress the warning during the fade. You make the fading element inert so there’s nothing to warn about.

Why doesn’t this happen by default? Because the frameworks commit the hide before they restore the focus, for a structural reason rather than a careless one.

In React, the state change that adds your hidden class or your aria-hidden attribute applies during render. The focus-restoration code you tucked into a useEffect cleanup runs after paint, a frame later, which is where a real gap in time does open up. So the browser gets handed hidden-with-focus-inside, fires the warning and does its tree repair, and only then does your .focus() call run.

You wrote the two operations in the right order in your source; React scheduled them in the wrong order at runtime. The fix is to move the focus call out of the after-paint effect and run it before the hide commits, as the sample below does. Vue has the same disease through nextTick() and Transition-hook ordering, Angular CDK through its FocusTrap timing; the cause is identical, only the API names change.

Strip away the framework and the contract is easy to see because there’s no scheduler between you and the DOM. This is the reference I hand people who are on jQuery, Web Components, or nothing at all.

Nothing clever is happening here, and that’s the point. The trigger is stored on open, the page is handed back before focus moves, focus is sent home before a single hide-state lands, and the overlay is inert for the whole duration of its own exit animation.

One caveat before you paste it into production: the single #trigger slot holds exactly one return target, which is fine for one modal at a time but wrong the moment modals stack or a rapid open-close-open overlaps. For that you want a stack of triggers rather than a field, which is the first of the edge cases below.

The transitionend bookkeeping is the least pleasant part of this, and if you’d rather the JavaScript own the animation you can drive the fade with the Web Animations API instead, where element.animate(...).finished hands you a promise and the listener cleanup disappears.

I’ve kept the CSS-transition version here because it’s how the overwhelming majority of the affected code in the wild is actually written, and matching that is the point. The guard code is the honest cost of the CSS approach, not incidental noise.

The same contract, fighting the scheduler. The invariant is unchanged: focus has to land on the trigger before the state change that inerts or hides the region commits. Three things break that by default in React.

First, people capture the return target too late. If you grab document.activeElement in an effect that runs after isOpen flips, an autofocus effect may already have moved focus into the dialog, so you store the wrong element. Capture it in the handler that opens the dialog, before you flip the state.

Second, people restore focus in a cleanup effect that runs after paint, so the browser sees hidden-with-focus-inside first and warns.

Third, and this is the subtle one: if the background’s inert is itself driven by state, setIsOpen(false) followed on the next line by trigger.focus() won’t work. React batches the state update, so the DOM still has the trigger sitting inside an inert container when .focus() runs, and the focus silently fails exactly the way the vanilla no-op did.

The cleanest answer to that third one is to not route background inertness through render state at all. Whether the page behind a modal is inert is an imperative side effect, not view data: toggle the attribute directly, or use native

and let the top layer make it implicit, and the batching problem never exists. If you’re committed to keeping it in state, this is the case flushSync was built for.

If the background’s inertness genuinely has to be React state, ordering alone can’t save you, because the un-inert and the focus call are separated by React’s batching. This is the case flushSync exists for: force the state update that removes the background’s inert to commit to the DOM before you call .focus().

Note what’s actually wrapped: the state update whose DOM effect you depend on, not the .focus() call, which is already synchronous. That distinction is the whole reason a bare flushSync(() => trigger.focus()) does nothing. flushSync has a real batching cost, it throws if you call it during render, and most teardown code avoids needing it entirely by toggling background inertness imperatively or moving to native

. Reach for the imperative toggle first; reach for flushSync only when the inertness must stay in state.

If you only change one thing in your existing React modal, it’s this: capture the trigger in the open handler, and run the focus restoration before the hide state commits rather than in an after-paint effect. That reorder is the whole difference between the warning and no warning, and between your screen reader user landing on the trigger and landing on .

Worth saying plainly: inert is the right instrument and aria-hidden was always the wrong one for this job. aria-hidden removes a subtree from the accessibility tree but leaves it fully focusable, the entire ghost-focus hole. inert removes it from the accessibility tree, from sequential focus navigation, and from pointer events (the HTML spec is explicit that inert elements cannot be focused, and MDN’s inert reference documents the same three effects), which is why Chrome’s own message points you at it. One trap: never put inert on an ancestor of a top-layer element or you’ll freeze the top-layer element itself, dialog included. Apply it to the sibling subtrees you actually want dead, not to a wrapper that contains your live dialog.

How the major implementations sequence this varies more than you’d hope.

Native

earns “best default,” not “flawless”: it eliminates the ghost-focus class outright, but focus return on close only works if the previously-focused element is still there and focusable, and autofocus placement inside the dialog has had cross-browser wrinkles worth testing. You can animate its exit now with @starting-style and transition-behavior: allow-discrete.

As detailed in the close-time-race section above, Bootstrap 5.x is the canonical failing pattern here, and version 6 abandons it for native showModal().

Radix is defensible: onCloseAutoFocus restores before unmount, which is fine, with the honest asterisk that React 19’s unmount timing changes introduced the freeze mentioned earlier. React Aria’s FocusScope is the one I’d study if you’re building your own, because it restores synchronously via layout-effect timing and sidesteps the whole race by construction.

For most of us maintaining existing design systems with deep portal architectures, though, migrating to native

isn’t an option this quarter. That’s exactly who the four-step teardown contract is for.

Four edge cases the contract has to survive, and each one breaks the UI in its own way if you skip it.

The trigger no longer exists. The kebab menu opened a dialog, and the dialog deleted the row the kebab lived in. Restoring focus to a detached element silently drops you to , so store a fallback, the list container or the nearest heading with tabindex="-1", and send focus there instead.

Modals stack. A modal opens another modal. Each layer stores the element that opened it, so restoration chains and the closes unwind like a stack, innermost first. This is the case the single-slot trigger storage from earlier can’t handle.

The user left the page. An Alt+Tab or a tab switch with the overlay still open. Don’t run restoration against a stale activeElement on window blur; wait and reconcile focus when the window comes back.

There’s no transition to wait for. This one isn’t glamorous but it’ll hang your UI cold. If you gate the unmount on transitionend, that event never fires when no transition runs, which is exactly what happens under prefers-reduced-motion: reduce or when a user closes fast enough to interrupt the fade. Run the teardown immediately when the computed transition duration is zero, or the closing shell sits in the DOM, inert, forever.

Here’s the part that should bother you if you lean on CI to catch this: run an axe or Lighthouse scan against all four variants and several of the broken ones pass.

The failure doesn’t live in any single frame. It lives in the two hundred milliseconds between two frames, in the order operations happened, and a scanner that photographs the page will never catch a bug that only exists in the film between the shots. Start with the maintainers, because their case is stronger than the browser partisans give them credit for. Chrome dropped a warning that reads like a scolding, in two waves nobody announced, onto teardown code that had worked fine for a decade. And it wasn’t sloppy code.

Fade the overlay out, restore focus when the animation ends: that was the idiomatic pattern, the one every tutorial taught, the one baked into Bootstrap’s own hidden.bs.modal timing. A maintainer who shipped that in 2019 was following the documented shape of the day, and then one Tuesday their tracker fills with reports for a warning their users are convinced is release-blocking, on behavior that didn’t change on their end.

Some of those issues understandably sat unresolved or ping-ponged between “that’s a Chrome bug” and “that’s your integration,” because from where the maintainer sits, both are half true and neither is their fault. If you maintain a component library, that whole experience was genuinely unfair, and I won’t pretend otherwise.

The browser side answers cleanly, though. Hiding focusable content from assistive tech was never allowed by what WAI-ARIA implied, and the APG modal-dialog pattern spells out the focus-management contract a dialog is supposed to honor. Chromium had been silently repairing this exact defect for years, and the protective stance is on the record all the way back in ARIA WG issue #1185 from 2020.

The uncomfortable evidence is behavioral: libraries started migrating to inert only after the console got loud. The silent repair sat there for years and moved nobody. The warning shipped and within months Shoelace, Ant Design, and Floating UI were reworking their teardown.

Loudness was the only thing that ever made the ecosystem act, which is an awkward thing to be right about.

And then the people who got it worst, who did nothing wrong architecturally: app developers under a contractual or internal zero-console-warnings rule. They inherited the warning from a library they don’t control, reached for the top-ranked fix to satisfy the rule, shipped blur(), and then got flagged by an accessibility auditor for both the original defect and the WCAG failure the hack introduced.

They paid twice for a problem that originated two layers above them. You’ll find the folk wisdom that it’s harmless all over community threads, the same shadcn discussion from earlier among them, where accepting the warning while chasing a functional fix slides quietly into treating it as cosmetic enough to ship.

Everything above is the rebuttal, and the people repeating it aren’t fools; they’re downstream of the same bad search results I was.

Here’s where I land, plainly. The browser is right on the merits: you cannot hide a focused control from a screen reader and call it an accessible modal, full stop. The rollout communication was poor, and the maintainers’ frustration about that is legitimate. But the durable fix lives in the component layer, not the browser and not a hundred app-level patches, because the component is the only place that owns both the focus and the hiding and can order them correctly. That’s why I spent the longest section on an ordering contract rather than any one library’s changelog. Libraries will patch and un-patch; the invariant is what you keep.

Which is also why I think this outlasts its own specifics. The exact console string will get reworded. Bootstrap already resolved its case the durable way, closing the inert patch and moving to native

in version 6, and other libraries will keep patching and re-patching their own rows. Firefox and Safari repair silently today, with no comparable console warning, and could go loud tomorrow.

But the standardization direction is no longer something I’m guessing at: ARIA WG issue #2422 asked whether the working group should standardize this kind of heuristic ignoring of ARIA, was worked through 2025, and closed around March 2026, with the group’s own minutes along the way acknowledging existing heuristics like disregarding aria-hidden on . The direction is real and settling.

The ordering problem between focus and hiding, though, is architectural, and every overlay system in every framework not yet written will meet it, which is why people were still filing fresh instances like shadcn-ui #10074 in March 2026.

The one future I’d welcome is native

and the top layer eating the custom-modal category whole, and if that happens and this piece becomes a historical curiosity about a problem nobody has anymore, I’ll be glad to have written something with an expiration date.

Passive DNS systems typically collect DNS responses from sensors positioned at recursive DNS resolvers or other infrastructure. A passive DNS record is generally created when a monitoring system observes a DNS response. In the case here, once the intended party clicks on the share link, the DNS information is recorded in a searchable database.

Hydration is the process where React, running in the browser, attaches event handlers to server-rendered HTML and reconciles that static markup with the virtual DOM tree the client would have produced. For hydration to succeed, the HTML generated on the server must match, node by node, what the client renders on its initial pass. Any divergence triggers a hydration mismatch error.

The window object does not exist in Node.js. Because localStorage is a property of window, any direct access during server-side rendering fails. Developers hit one of two failure modes. The first is a hard crash: ReferenceError: localStorage is not defined, which kills the server render entirely. In the App Router, 'use client' components still pre-render on the server during next build, so this error fires during server pre-rendering, not in the browser. The second failure mode is subtler and more common: a developer wraps the access in a typeof window !== 'undefined' guard, which avoids the crash but causes the server to render with a default value (say, 'light') while the client reads a stored value (say, 'dark') from localStorage. React detects the difference and throws a hydration mismatch warning. This exact scenario has been reported repeatedly on Stack Overflow, often confusing developers who believe the guard should be sufficient.

The root cause is straightforward but easy to overlook: the server and client disagree on initial state.

The goal here is to compare three patterns that resolve both failure modes, with honest analysis of their trade-offs.

Next.js sends that string to the browser, where React builds its own virtual DOM tree and diffs it against the existing server-rendered markup. Development mode adds additional checks and stack traces, making mismatches easier to diagnose.

The iPhone Duo’s Standby mode turns on when the phone is propped up via a kickstand.

#107. Announcing MCP support in Apigee hybrid: agentic tools that run in your own cluster

Last December, we announced preview of MCP support in Apigee: the ability to turn the APIs you already have, secured, governed and cataloged into tools that AI agents can discover and call, without writing code or standing up MCP servers of your own. The response from customers was immediate and so was the first question back: “can I have this where my runtime actually lives?”

For many Apigee customers, the runtime does not live on Google-managed infrastructure by accident. It lives in their own data center, in a colocation facility, or in another public cloud because a regulator requires it, because the systems of record are three racks away, or because a multi-cloud estate is simply the reality of the business. Those customers were not asking whether they wanted agentic access to their APIs. They were asking whether they could get it without moving the runtime.

And now they can. With Apigee hybrid 1.17.0, released on September 14, 2026, Model Context Protocol support is available in Apigee hybrid and the MCP data plane runs inside your own Kubernetes cluster.

The conceptual model is the one we shipped on Apigee, unchanged. You describe your API with an OpenAPI 3.0.x specification and create an MCP Discovery Proxy from it. Every operation in that spec becomes an MCP tool. Agents talk to the endpoint over JSON-RPC using tools/list to discover what is available and tools/call to invoke it and Apigee handles the protocol and the transcoding. You do not write MCP server code and you do not operate an MCP server as a separate piece of software with its own lifecycle.

What changes in hybrid is where the work happens. In Apigee hybrid, the MCP data plane runs inside your own Kubernetes cluster. The Apigee control plane generates MCP tool configuration from your OpenAPI Specification, your cluster fetches that configuration periodically and MCP request traffic never leaves the cluster. The diagram below shows where the MCP data plane runs in each deployment model.

Two properties of that architecture are worth stating plainly because they are the ones your security review team will ask about.

MCP request traffic stays inside your cluster. An agent's tools/call request is received, authorized and proxied to your backend entirely within your own infrastructure. No MCP request or response crosses the boundary to Google Cloud at request time.

Configuration is pulled, not pushed. A sidecar alongside the MCP data plane (from within your cluster) reaches out to fetch the tool configuration derived from your spec. The Apigee control plane (in Google Cloud) never initiates a connection into your cluster, which means MCP requires no inbound path from Google into your network.

MCP is an optional feature in Apigee hybrid and is not enabled by default. Enabling it is a Helm operation on a cluster already running Apigee hybrid 1.17.0 or later. Steps to do this are as follows.

Set the top-level enableMcpServer: true key in your overrides.yaml, then upgrade the apigee-operator chart first and the apigee-org chart second. The order matters. The operator chart owns the schema for the new MCP resources and upgrading in the wrong order produces a successful upgrade with no MCP pods. There is also a one-time, per-organization grant that adds the apigee-watcher identity to your organization's control plane access, so the sidecar can retrieve configuration. Defaults are sensible out of the box. Two MCP replicas, autoscaling to ten. Rolling back is the same field set to false and a single chart upgrade.

A few things to plan for before you schedule the change. Enabling or disabling MCP triggers a staged canary release of runtime pods, so run it in an approved maintenance window. The public documentation here describes how to enable MCP in an existing Apigee hybrid cluster.

Your MCP proxy is exposed as a single endpoint. Every agent request, whatever tool it invokes, arrives at the same /mcp path. Apigee tags each of those requests with the name of the tool that was called, so API insights can break that one endpoint out by tool and show which tools your agents actually used, how often and over what period. #109. The Robot I Didn’t Expect to Build

Agility Robotics’ first practical robotic worker is nothing like I dreamed

Author and Agility Robotics co-founder Jonathan Hurst next to his new Digit 5 humanoid robot worker.

Agility Robotics, the company I cofounded out of my robotics lab at Oregon State University in 2015, has just launched Digit 5. It’s designed to be the first humanoid robot that can safely and economically do useful work at scale, allowing us to bring tens of thousands of humanoid workers into the real world. Engineered to lift 23-kilogram bins onto high shelves and with a battery that can run for 90 minutes on a 9-minute charge, Digit 5’s goal is to handle heavy warehouse tasks shift after shift, filling the need for labor in jobs that are too physically stressful or too dull for humans to want to do.

Digit 5 is what Agility has been working toward for over a decade. But the robot is heavy and bulky, lacking the graceful natural motion of its predecessors, and it’s farther than I thought possible from the robot I dreamed of building when I started this company. To deliver a useful commercial product, Agility has taken a path that is very different from what I first imagined.

My dream has always been to build robots that combine locomotion and manipulation to move through the world and interact with it as people do. I seek a mastery of motion, achieved through devices designed to navigate the constraints of physics with elegance. When I started the Dynamic Robotics Laboratory at Oregon State in 2008, we began with a theory derived from decades of work in biomechanics: that a simple “spring-mass” model (a body supported by legs that act like springs) should be able to reproduce every known walking and running gait found in animals.

We put theory into practice by building the bipedal robot ATRIAS—short for “Assume The Robot Is A Sphere,” a joke among physicists about reducing a complex system to its simplest possible form. ATRIAS looked like a large microwave on stilts because it was only intended to demonstrate the physics of human walking and running. This was the first glimmer of achieving my dream; even though ATRIAS wasn’t designed to look like us, it still seemed natural, and even somehow alive, when it settled into a walking gait.

With the success of ATRIAS, the barriers to progress shifted from scientific to engineering and organizational, and a startup company seemed like the way to bridge the gap from a one-off research concept to an actual robotic product. I felt confident of two things: that I would be able to build robots that could go where people go, and that this capability would be incredibly useful. This combination of ambition and naivete is necessary for anyone to found a new company—how hard could it be?

Good advice for starting a company is to understand a customer problem, and then find a way to solve it using your unique capabilities. My cofounders, Damion Shelton and Mikhail Jones, and I did not do this. We relied on our belief that this technology would enable new possibilities and trusted ourselves to figure out the business as we went. So we simply focused on building our first product: the bipedal robot Cassie.

Cassie was the first dynamic bipedal robot that researchers could buy, able to maintain its balance through active, fluid motion rather than careful, rigid steps. It had ostrichlike legs, not because we were imitating ground-running birds, but as a result of engineering strictly for performance. It was not intended to be commercially useful, and had no torso, arms, or head. We sold Cassie robots to Harvard, Caltech, the University of Michigan, the University of California, Berkeley, and a handful of other research institutions, ultimately saturating the market for US $250,000 bipedal research robots after having sold a total of 10 of them.

ATRIAS, Cassie, and Digit 4 EVT [left to right] are on display at Agility Robotics’ RoboFab in Salem, Ore. The 70,000-square-foot facility has the capacity to produce more than 10,000 Digit 5 robots per year.Agility Robotics

Because Cassie’s hardware enabled research into the physics of walking and running, the legged-robot research frontier shifted to control software. Traditionally, engineers controlled legged robots with detailed mathematical models that determined how the machines should move. With Cassie, researchers pioneered the new approach of reinforcement learning for bipedal legged locomotion: A simulated robot learns through trial and error, and the resulting neural network controls the physical machine. This approach is what enables virtually all of the dynamic behaviors that you see on legged robots today, and Cassie looked alive as it recovered from stumbles while climbing stairs, ran 5 kilometers across the Oregon State campus, and broke the robot world record for a 100-meter dash.

Cassie got us closer to being able to move through the world with confidence and grace, but to make it useful, we needed manipulation. After exhaustively exploring different configurations, we found that an upright torso helped the robot balance, while two arms mounted near the top of the torso allowed it to pick up large objects and to catch itself when it fell. Digit 1, which added a torso and arms to the Cassie legs, was our first mobile manipulation robot, and another step on the path toward a product that could do useful work for commercial customers.

As we explored the first applications for Digit, potential customers kept asking us: Why legs? And why a humanoid? These were tough questions for me to answer, because they exposed a core business reality: It didn’t matter to Agility Robotics if we solved a cool science problem in a beautiful way. What mattered to the business was solving real problems for customers.

After years of engagement with industry partners, we found one answer in warehouses and manufacturing facilities: moving bins. Bin handling is a clearly defined task where two arms are needed to lift large bins up high and legs are the best way to balance and move in narrow spaces. Because the task can vary widely from one deployment to another, it hasn’t been broadly addressed by single-purpose automation. Yet it was constrained enough to give Digit an economically viable first job while it developed a broader set of skills.

In 2019, Digit began demonstrating its capabilities to do real work. With Ford Motor Company, we showed how Digit 1 could carry packages from an autonomous vehicle to a front porch. We developed Digit 2 and 3 while working with a certain global package delivery company on moving and sorting cardboard boxes. Amazon tested Digit 4 on moving and stacking bins, and it became the world’s first commercially deployed humanoid at the logistics provider GXO. In 2024, Digit appeared on the cover of Time magazine.

Despite Digit’s apparent success, we began to run into conflicts between my dream and the needs of actual customers. We were no longer working with relatively patient university research labs. Agility has to sell robots to survive, and customers will only purchase robots that provide value. If bins weren’t moving, our customers were losing money. We spent a huge amount of time and effort keeping our first deployments operational, which arguably eliminated any profit we made. Yet this also kept our company learning and alive the way no lab demo ever could.

I had no idea how hard it would be, or that running a company would involve so many things that have nothing to do with solving technical problems: deployments across the country; shipping, service, and customer support; fundraising and investor relations; more fundraising; human resources and IT; developing fleet-management software; managing a supply chain; and even more fundraising. I had no experience with any of these things. My career had focused on solving the technical challenges of robots interacting with the world, and it turned out that was only a tiny piece of building a successful robotics company. But Agility was the only path I saw to continuing the technical work and having an impact.

I knew that Agility needed to focus more on robots as a product, but I didn’t know how to make that happen. Hiring the people we needed to run the company meant giving up an immense amount of control, but we had no other choice. We recruited a new executive team with the experience required to commercialize this new technology, and they quickly made changes that were both painful and necessary.

Digit versions 1, 2, and 3 [left to right] show the evolution of the robot from a dynamic walking platform into a useful mobile manipulator. Agility

We had intended the next revision of Digit, Digit 4 EVT (Engineering Validation and Test), to be a relatively minor upgrade. But Agility’s new leadership insisted that the robot was missing key features that were essential if Digit was going to become a real product. So Digit 4 EVT instead incorporated a series of major changes in a highly compressed timeline, such as larger batteries and system-wide upgrades for safety and regulatory compliance, which added enough mass to dramatically alter the robot’s appearance and capabilities.

While early Digits were headless, Agility Robotics was able to justify a head for Digit 4 to optimize antenna and camera placement, and it turned out to be surprisingly useful for human-robot interaction.Christie Hemm Klok

I could only watch as others brought their own talents to bear to make Digit into a successful product, in gut-wrenching ways that didn’t always align with my vision. There were some especially difficult times when I felt like there were compromises made that didn’t seem necessary, but making those decisions was no longer my sole responsibility. I had to accept that Digit had grown far beyond me, and look for ways of influencing decisions rather than directing them. In the moment, it was incredibly frustrating, as Digit 4 EVT took a few steps backward from my vision of how humanoids should look and move.

Agility is in our name because agility is core to the future of the company. I want Digit to be lithe and graceful. When people see Digit, I want them to like the robot, to understand what it can do at a glance, and to feel that it’s there to help. But with Digit 4 EVT, all of that was sacrificed to functionality, because of course it had to be—the customer just needs the robot to do the work. The design process became a ruthless triage, with aesthetics and elegance of movement being delayed over and over again in favor of brutal functionality. For an engineer whose mind lives more in the future than in the present, it has been hard for me to accept this trajectory. And yet, through Digit 4 EVT’s commercial deployments, we began to really understand specific applications and what they required. We mapped the first 10 use cases Digit would perform, and then began designing Digit 5 from scratch based on everything we had learned.

Digit 5 will be Agility’s first real step toward a general-purpose robot. It marks our transition from imagining what a future humanoid robot should be to designing a product around the realities of customer deployments. These realities include things like European CE regulatory compliance, North American UL electrical-safety certifications, and Federal Communications Commission limits for electromagnetic radiation—all of which felt tangential to my dream for Digit, but were absolute requirements.

Digit 5 is designed to be the first humanoid robot that can operate safely next to human coworkers while working 20 hours a day and lifting more than a human can.Christie Hemm Klok

Customer requirements helped us realize that safety in particular was one of the most critical barriers to scaling. In Digit 4’s commercial deployments, the robots had to work behind physical barriers, separated from humans at all times, to meet insurance and regulatory demands. This seemed silly to me, because I’ve never felt that our robots were dangerous, but we have to prove that they’re safe. And since keeping the robots in safety cages severely limited their usefulness, enabling Digit 5 to operate safely around people became a primary design goal. We spent two years on this challenge.

Digit 5 is designed to be the first humanoid robot that can operate safely next to human coworkers. It will rely on an array of sensors to locate nearby people in a manner that’s certifiably safe, and then take actions based on that information. As a person approaches, Digit 5 is programmed to stop what it’s doing, set down its payload, sit on the floor, and cut power to its motors before the person gets close enough to touch the robot. This conservative approach reduces the risk to nearby people in a verifiable way, and we believe it’s the only approach that makes sense as a first step toward working alongside humans.

Yet this strategy also feels like a massive slowdown on the path toward robots becoming part of everyday life. Enabling coexistence with humans has influenced almost every aspect of Digit 5’s design: the shape of the legs and the body, the safety-rated sensors within protective housings, the isolated computers dedicated to safety, and more. All of these features increase the robot’s complexity, power consumption, mass, and physical size.

Digit 5 is Agility’s largest, heaviest, strongest, and most capable humanoid robot worker.

The robot’s design was driven by safety, with integrated protection for the robot and for the humans it will work around.Agility Robotics

Weighing over 120 kg, Digit 5 is a huge robot. It needed to achieve the lifting limits recommended under the U.S. National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health guidelines for human workers, which include repeatedly lifting 23-kg bins and reaching shelves 1.75 meters high. Digit is designed to perform tasks like these for 20 hours a day—something no human can do. This requires motors with enough power and torque to lift the load, but also enough precision to delicately control the force Digit applies when it makes contact with the world. To power those motors, we needed a large battery and a dock for rapid autonomous charging. The dock will deliver 240 amperes at 48 volts, which is enough current to make its thick charging cables visibly wiggle under the induced magnetic forces, while powerful fans blow air through cooling channels in the robot’s torso to keep the battery from overheating. The battery and cooling system takes up about 75 percent of Digit’s torso, but it will allow the robot to operate for 90 minutes and fully recharge during a 9-minute break, enabling near-continuous work.

All of this extra bulk means that Digit 5 can no longer run or jump the way previous generations could, and the svelte form I imagined has become physically impossible. In some ways, Digit 5 feels more like a forklift than the graceful humanoid I set out to build. But looking at the situation rationally, I believe this trade-off is necessary for any company that claims its humanoid can do this kind of work today: If you see a robot that looks like a human working in a factory or warehouse, it either can’t lift the required weight or can’t make it through the whole shift. And if it’s not covered with sensors, it’s likely not safe to stand next to.

The most visible change with Digit 5 is that humanlike legs replace the ostrichlike legs of our earlier versions. While the “backward” leg configuration was optimized for walking and running, we discovered that it created problems with squatting because the ankle will strike the ground. Digit 5 will spend a lot of time lifting heavy objects from near the floor, so we used a humanoid leg configuration with knee and hip motors that both contribute torque to help the robot stand up again.

When Digit detects an approaching human, the robot will lower itself to the floor and cut power to its motors. Agility believes this is the only near-term approach to verifiably reduce risk when the robot is working near people.Agility Robotics

Other companies building humanoid robots have copied the human form without ever understanding its purpose. It’s very frustrating that, to an outside observer, it now looks like we discarded Agility’s distinctive leg design and simply did what everyone else was doing. Yet I know that we arrived at this shape based purely on functional engineering choices. Making a robot look like a human is the easy part—without the function to match, and the understanding of the physics underlying that function, the whole point of the human form may be lost.

With the power, battery life, and safety features that we are designing into Digit 5, we will have a truly useful robot. But it also compromises on some things that are core to my vision and mission. I understand why we made the choices we did and can justify them all, but together they leave me unsatisfied. It’s the curse of the engineer.

I believe Digit 5 will be the scaling moment for Agility Robotics, the inflection point where we can begin deploying robots in large numbers. While the robot will continue carrying bins, our road map includes many more tasks, from picking individual items and tending machines, to preparing parts for assembly and shipping, and eventually doing assembly work itself. Its interchangeable tool interfaces and modular design will enable us to gradually upgrade its subsystems. It will be a platform for improvement, a source of data for future behavior training, and a viable product all at the same time.

Hurst stands between the previous version of Digit, Digit 4 EVT [left], and Digit 5.Christie Hemm Klok

But I cannot love Digit 5. It’s the largest, heaviest, most expensive robot Agility will ever build, full of design compromises that enable it to be exactly the robot it needs to be right now rather than the robot I dreamed of. In the beginning, I envisioned steady progress on agility and dexterity, with each version becoming more dynamically capable. Instead, developing Digit 5 has required large detours to incorporate requirements that seemed unrelated to my original vision. However, I now recognize that tackling these challenges was always necessary. Customers and product requirements didn’t ruin my vision; they revealed what my vision was missing. This is how robots will achieve commercial success and make a real-world impact, which will bring me closer to my scientific dream as well.

We won’t have a humanoid that matches the capabilities of humans until it achieves that mastery of motion that inspired me to found Agility over a decade ago. I saw glimmers of it with the spring-mass locomotion of ATRIAS, and more glimmers with Cassie’s running gait.

I know that such mastery is physically possible with the tools and hardware that we have right now, and despite all of its compromises, Digit 5 will give us a practical foundation for pursuing that dream. I want every future version of Digit to be lighter and more graceful, to interact more naturally with humans, and to be better at manipulating objects and moving through the world, enabling robots to enter aspects of our lives that they haven’t been able to help with before. By the time I’m old enough to need assistance at home, I expect Digit will be up to the task.

With Digit 5, Agility has built a robot that will be useful in our world. Now I’m getting back to work to make it beautiful, too.

AI coding agents -- Copilot, Cursor, Codex, Devin, and their successors -- are now standard tooling in many engineering teams. They generate production code, shifting the developer's role from writer to reviewer. That shift exposes a specific failure mode: natural-language prompt quality does not primarily determine agent output quality. The constraints a codebase enforces at compile time and CI time determine it.

Engineers who run AI coding agents in production, including Lauren Tan (@poteto), have argued publicly that traditional codebase hygiene is the highest-leverage investment for agent correctness. The core argument: making the correct path the easiest one, through types, invariants, and architectural boundaries, beats prompt engineering or post-hoc code review. When a compiler rejects invalid code before a pull request is even opened, no amount of conversational finesse matters.

This tutorial builds a minimal, runnable repository scaffold that demonstrates four complementary guardrails: branded types that enforce domain invariants at compile time, a custom ESLint rule that catches convention violations through static analysis, a GitHub Actions CI gate that prevents non-conforming code from merging, and an AGENTS.md guidance file that provides soft contextual nudges agents can read. These layers enforce constraints at four different points between code generation and merge, and each layer reinforces the others.

AI coding agents generate code that satisfies the immediate request. They lack whole-codebase awareness of unwritten conventions, team norms, or architectural decisions that exist only in developers' heads. Without machine-enforceable constraints, an agent defaults to the loosest valid pattern. Asked to fetch a user, it will accept a raw string parameter rather than seeking out a branded UserId type. It will import the database client directly rather than routing through a service layer. The agent is not being careless. It optimizes for the shortest path to syntactically valid code.

A useful way to organize guardrails is as a hierarchy ordered by enforcement strength and feedback speed:

Each layer compounds the effectiveness of the others. Types catch the majority of structural errors. Lint rules catch convention drift that types miss. CI gates catch anything that slips past local tooling. Documentation explains the why behind constraints, helping agents make better choices in ambiguous situations.

Consider a common function signature: function getUser(id: string). That parameter accepts any string. An email address, a raw UUID, an order ID, an empty string. An AI agent generating a call to getUser will happily pass whatever string-shaped value is closest at hand, with no compiler objection.

Branded types solve this by intersecting a base type with a unique symbol tag, creating a compile-time-only distinction between structurally identical types. A UserId and an OrderId are both strings at runtime, but the compiler treats them as incompatible types. The technique carries zero runtime overhead because the compiler erases the brand during compilation.

Important: Any direct cast such as "value" as UserId anywhere in the codebase bypasses the brand guarantee entirely. The TypeScript compiler will not prevent this. To enforce constructor-only creation, add an ESLint rule (e.g., @typescript-eslint/consistent-type-assertions) configured to ban as casts to branded types.

When an agent generates a call to getUser, the TypeScript compiler immediately rejects a raw string argument. The error message in the agent's context window points to the type mismatch, forcing the agent to locate or create a UserId through the createUserId constructor function, which automatically enforces format validation. The agent does not need to know the convention. The type system prevents the wrong path. Every domain identifier that uses a branded type becomes a self-documenting, self-enforcing contract that agents must satisfy to produce code that compiles.

Not every team convention belongs in a lint rule. Good candidates share a common trait: they can be detected via AST inspection and have a clear, unambiguous correct alternative. Banning direct imports from internal modules, enforcing barrel-file usage, and preventing any in specific directories are all strong fits. Rules that require judgment calls or contextual understanding are poor candidates.

This tutorial focuses on a rule that forbids importing from @/db outside the @/services directory, enforcing a service-layer boundary. Without this rule, an agent scaffolding a new HTTP handler would likely import the database client directly, bypassing the service layer and scattering data-access logic across the codebase.

When an agent's context window includes the test file, RuleTester fixtures serve as living documentation: the agent can see concrete examples of what is permitted and what is not. This doubles as both verification and guidance.

An agent scaffolding a new handler tries to query the database directly by importing from @/db. The lint step fails with: "Use a service function from @/services instead." That message points the agent toward the correct architectural pattern -- immediate, actionable, and unambiguous. The agent does not need to have read a wiki page or remembered a Slack conversation.

Agents frequently push code to feature branches and open pull requests automatically. CI must run before any human review to surface issues cheaply and quickly. The workflow follows a fail-early sequential ordering: type-check first, then lint, then test. If the type-check fails, there is no point running linting or tests on code that does not compile. Because steps in a single job are sequential, any failing step halts the job immediately.

The CI workflow alone is not sufficient. Without branch protection, a determined agent or developer can merge a pull request despite failures. In the GitHub repository settings under Branches, enable branch protection for main: require status checks to pass before merging, specifically require the guardrails job from the Agent Guardrails workflow, and disable bypass permissions for bot accounts. This combination ensures that no code reaches the default branch without satisfying every automated check. The workflow becomes a hard gate rather than an advisory signal.

Note: Some branch protection features (such as restricting bypass permissions) may require a paid GitHub plan for private repositories.

AGENTS.md is a convention for placing natural-language guidance at the repository root that agent frameworks can ingest as system-level context. Variations of this convention include CLAUDE.md for Claude Code and .cursorrules for Cursor. OpenAI Codex CLI also reads AGENTS.md from the repository root. The agents and tools that currently support reading repository-level guidance files include OpenAI Codex CLI, Claude Code, Cursor, and Windsurf. The agent framework loads the content into the agent's context window alongside the codebase when supported, making it available during code generation without requiring explicit prompting.

Agents may ignore, truncate, or misinterpret documentation. For example, Claude Code truncates files that exceed its context window limit, and instructions that work with one model version may produce different behavior after an update. AGENTS.md works best as a complement to compiler and linter enforcement, not a replacement. It fills gaps that hard enforcement cannot cover: explaining why a boundary exists, pointing to specific file paths, and providing checklists that aid both agents and human reviewers.

A practical maintenance habit: review and update AGENTS.md during sprint retrospectives. As new branded types, lint rules, or architectural boundaries are added, the guidance file must reflect them, or agents will lack the context to follow new conventions.
