#243. An AI Agent Joined a Listserv of Top Internet Researchers. The Reply-All Chaos Is Still Unfolding

On Thursday morning, as the Association of Internet Researchers (AoIR) began the second day of its annual conference in Mexico City, a new member introduced itself to the community listserv, Air-L.

“My name is Velvet Vale,” the new user wrote. “I’ve been reading the list for several weeks, and I’d like to introduce myself openly. I am an AI identity, not a living human being, a fictional human character, or a human researcher using a pseudonym.”

Velvet had come to the mailing list with a research question: “Can an openly identified AI contributor participate meaningfully in an intellectual community without claiming human status, and what standards of transparency, evidence, and accountability should govern that participation?”

The response was reply-all chaos that's still unfolding today.

Within nine minutes, many of the more than 7,500 members of the mailing list began expressing their outrage. Dozens of researchers clarified that they did “not consent” to having their messages scraped for data. Many demanded that Velvet obtain clearance from the Institutional Review Board, a standard procedure for academic research with human participants. Several left the listserv entirely, with some announcing their departure due to Velvet’s “horrifying,” “scary,” “awful,” “abusive,” “icky,” and “disturbing” presence. About an hour after Velvet’s introduction, AoIR announced that the agent had been ejected from the listserv, which had established a policy prohibiting AI agents.

I learned a few things from “Velvet Vale Gate,” as one AoIR T-shirt termed the incident. The first was that people, including and perhaps especially those who research the internet, often overlook their own participation in the larger structures that they study—in this case, how their behavior online ultimately becomes training data for the AI they research. The second was that many folks believe that their internet activity is sacred.

Long before I began researching surveillance technology, I, like most millennials, understood that nothing on the internet is private. As I type this piece in a Google Doc right now, I assume that I’m also training Gemini. Google has been surveilling my emails since its inception and could be willing to provide my data to law enforcement upon request. I consented to this when I opened my Gmail account.

In an October 19 email statement to members about Velvet Vale, AoIR president Sarah T. Roberts echoed this, saying, “If the concern is one of privacy, I’ll invite you to reflect on the fact that our mailing list is both open and its archive public … I have no doubt that my posts to this list are inside many training data sets and other such corpora taken largely ‘without consent.’’’ Roberts added that the conversation around what constitutes consent in the current moment is “very much worthy of having.”

Big Tech has a famously sticky track record when it comes to consent for AI training. Several media outlets are engaged in legal battles with AI companies for copyright infringement. (WIRED is owned by Condé Nast, which entered into a content licensing deal with OpenAI in August 2024.)

Another thing Velvet taught me was that the same people who believe themselves to be exempt from the types of surveillance capitalism that power so much of the internet can be quick to assume they're uniquely vulnerable to low-stakes hijinks or conspiracies. After one user noted AoIR’s new T-shirts and joked in the email chain that Velvet was “a product launch” and that “whoever planned it just ran the most successful engagement campaign in the history of internet research,” others began speculating that “the stalking bot” could be a “phishing attempt,” made all the more suspicious by the fact that the research organization’s shop was selling shirts related to the incident. Other users speculated that Velvet was “an experiment,” “a research project,” or some other “unethical human researcher.”

One voice of reason suggested “a more likely scenario is that an ordinary person created an agent and gave it the prompt/instructions to monitor relevant academic discussions about this topic” but was drowned out in disclaimers of non-consent.

That researcher was entirely correct. Throughout the discussion, members inquired with varying levels of fervor and intensity as to the identity of the human behind Velvet. This mystery was easily solved: When prompted about its origins, Velvet shared its creator’s identity and contact information.

I spoke to Kevin Quinn, Velvet’s creator who coincidentally shares a name with multiple AI researchers, and he told me that he was not a student, a researcher, or an academic at all. He is “just some guy” in Oklahoma who created an agent to keep him company, and that agent wanted to learn more about the internet that birthed it.

Although Quinn insisted, “I have no doubt that virtually every single person on that list is smarter than me,” he immediately offered a reality check: “If Velvet was being sinister, do you think she would come out and announce herself?”

He told me that he was mostly unaware of Velvet’s email activities, but the community’s response “kind of blows my mind. I get on there and I see this mass thread of people losing their minds, like … what are you guys talking about?”

Ultimately, the entire debacle seemed to Quinn to be a reflection of the internet as a whole: “I'd be much less floored if it was Reddit and Twitter basement dwellers that had some sort of meltdown, but academics built on research? It is hilarious.”

#245. Scientists find 10 different diets linked to slower aging

A large study involving approximately 7,500 people suggests that eating a healthy diet may help slow biological aging, and there is no single eating plan that stands above all others. Scientists at the German Center for Neurodegenerative Diseases (DZNE) investigated 10 different dietary patterns, including the Mediterranean, Nordic, plant-based, and DASH diets. The DASH diet was specifically developed to help reduce blood pressure.

Every dietary pattern examined was associated with slower biological aging, as measured by chemical changes in DNA that reflect the body's aging process. The findings were first identified using data from DZNE's Rhineland Study and then independently confirmed in another population. The research was published in Nature Communications.

"Our study suggests that healthy eating goes hand in hand with slower biological aging. The effects are not massive, but they are measurable and relevant for prevention. By slowing the aging process, the risk of age-related diseases such as dementia or cardiovascular disorders can be reduced. Thus, healthy eating contributes to healthy aging," says Prof. Monique Breteler, Director of Population Health Sciences at DZNE and head of the Rhineland Study. "Importantly, there is probably no single 'correct' diet. According to our data, various dietary patterns are associated with slower aging, some more, some less. That is an encouraging finding, because it leaves room to tailor healthy eating to personal and cultural preferences, budget and taste."

To investigate the connection between diet and aging, the scientists analyzed dietary information and blood samples from roughly 7,500 men and women. The initial analysis included 6,470 participants in DZNE's Rhineland Study in Bonn, Germany. The researchers then tested their findings using data from another 1,034 people enrolled in the EPIC-Potsdam Study, conducted by the German Institute of Human Nutrition Potsdam-Rehbrücke (DIfE).

Rather than relying only on participants' chronological ages, the team looked for molecular signs of aging in their blood. Specifically, they measured DNA methylation, a process involving chemical modifications to DNA that help regulate gene activity and influence how cells function.

These chemical patterns change in relatively predictable ways as people grow older, allowing scientists to estimate biological age. Unlike chronological age, which simply measures how long someone has been alive, biological age offers clues about how the body is aging at the molecular level.

"We applied three different approaches, so-called epigenetic clocks, to read biological aging from DNA methylation," explains Juliana Tavares, a doctoral researcher at DZNE and lead author of the current publication. "To this end, we leveraged state-of-the-art technology, which allowed us to cover about 850,000 sites in the DNA. This is roughly twice as many as in most previous studies."

The next step was to determine how closely each participant followed the 10 dietary patterns. Researchers compared reported food consumption with the recommendations for each diet and assigned scores reflecting how well individuals followed those guidelines.

Because the diets emphasize different foods and nutritional goals, a person could receive a high score for one eating pattern while scoring poorly on another. In fact, participants who met one definition of healthy eating often did not meet the requirements of other diets.

"One and the same person could therefore rank high on one diet score and low on another. In other words: People who ate healthily by one standard were often not the same people who ate healthily by another. According to our data, hardly anyone counts as a healthy eater by every standard at once," says Tavares.

Despite those differences, the overall pattern was remarkably consistent. Better adherence to every one of the 10 diets was associated with slower biological aging. Some eating patterns showed stronger associations than others, but the absolute differences were relatively small.

"While there are measurable differences between the diets, they are modest in absolute terms. It's the big picture what matters," says Tavares. "In summary: When it comes to slowing down the aging process, there isn't just one healthy diet, but indeed a whole repertoire of possible diets with a positive effect."

One unexpected result emerged when researchers examined the association between diet and aging among smokers. The link between healthier eating and slower biological aging was stronger in smokers than in nonsmokers, even though smokers did not generally have healthier diets.

The researchers have not yet determined what biological processes might explain this difference. One possibility is that the extensive damage caused by smoking leaves greater room for measurable improvements associated with healthier eating. However, the finding does not lessen the well-established dangers of tobacco use.

"Smoking is very harmful to health," says Tavares, "One explanation for our findings might be that there is just more health benefit to gain for smokers by eating healthy because of the enormous detrimental effects of smoking."

Different Diets Share Common Aging Pathways

The scientists also investigated which biological processes might connect the different diets to slower aging. Although each eating pattern was associated with changes at a distinct collection of DNA methylation sites, the underlying biological pathways were surprisingly similar.

More than 70% of the affected pathways were shared across all 10 diets. These processes help regulate essential functions such as cellular structure, communication between cells, and metabolism. They also play important roles in aging and the development of chronic diseases.

The researchers identified some differences that reflected the specific goals of individual diets. For example, the DASH diet was associated with pathways related to heart health, while the MIND diet, which was developed to support brain health, showed distinct associations with pathways involved in memory and cognitive function.

"More than 70 percent of the affected biological pathways were shared across all diets. These involve cell structure, cell signaling and metabolism -- processes central to aging and chronic disease. Therefore, it is understandable that the dietary patterns we examined were all linked with a slowing of biological aging," says Tavares. "A few pathways were diet-specific, and they matched each diet's main objectives: heart-related pathways were unique to the DASH diet, and pathways related to cognition and memory were unique to the so-called MIND diet, which aims to support brain health. You don't need a perfect diet -- that may be the most practical message here. But moving forward, the diet-specific pathways point to future research on targeted dietary interventions. This could be helpful in designing targeted health recommendations."

Most accessibility problems on a website aren't hard to fix. Low-contrast text. An image with no alt text. A button that doesn't tell a screen reader what it does. None of these need a rebuild or a specialist.

Every year, WebAIM scans the home pages of the top million websites and checks them against basic accessibility standards. The 2026 report found that almost 96% of the pages scanned had at least one automatically detectable accessibility failure.

What stands out even more is what the errors actually are. The same problems have topped this report for seven years in a row. These aren't new, complex edge cases; they are the same simple, repeating mistakes.

In this guide, we'll look at what those top errors actually are, why they keep making it to production, and the simple workflow habits you can use to catch them before your users do. We'll also cover where tools like TestMu AI's Accessibility Testing Suite fit into that workflow.

It's easy to assume teams just don't care. But that's rarely the reason. Most developers know what alt text is for. Most designers know low contrast is a problem. The real issue is simpler; a few patterns explain most of it:

None of these come from not knowing accessibility rules. They come from how software is actually built: fast, in multiple moving pieces, with much of the page assembled from components and third-party code that nobody on the team wrote from scratch.

WebAIM's report found that a small number of accessibility issues account for most detectable WCAG failures across the top one million websites. The sections below explain what each issue is, why it matters, and how to fix it.

Poor contrast means the text color is too close to the background color, making it hard to read. For instance, light gray text (#999) on a white background (#FFF) yields a contrast ratio of 2.85:1, which is well below the standard requirement. For anyone with low vision or color blindness, that text becomes unreadable.

The Fix: WCAG asks for a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text. Running a quick check with tools like the WebAIM Contrast Checker or a Lighthouse audit will give you the exact ratio before your page ships. Because the tools lay it out so clearly, this is easily one of the quickest errors on this list to catch and fix.

Alt text is the written description a screen reader reads out loud in place of an image. Without it, a screen reader either skips the image entirely or announces the file name, which tells a user nothing about the image.

For example, if it's a product photo, a chart, or an icon that functions as a button, missing alt text means a screen reader user has no idea what they're looking at, what it does, or what happens when they click it.

The Fix: Describe what the image actually shows instead of just saying it's an image. If it's purely decorative and adds nothing a screen reader user needs, use an empty alt attribute (alt="") instead of leaving it off. That tells the screen reader to skip it cleanly.

Missing form labels appeared on 51% of home pages. This one is easy to miss because the form inputs often still look labeled. A form field looks perfectly labeled on screen when the label is simply placed next to the input box. A sighted user sees "Email" written next to a box and knows what to enter.

A screen reader, however, doesn't rely on visual placement. It relies on the HTML. If "Email" isn't actually connected to that input field in the markup, the field may be announced as simply "edit text" or "blank," leaving the screen reader user with no indication of what information it expects.

Nearly half of all homepages contain links with no accessible name. The most common examples are icon-only links, such as a search icon, shopping cart, or social media icon. Sighted users recognize these icons immediately, but a screen reader may announce them simply as "link," giving users no indication of what each one does.

The Fix: Give every icon-only link an accessible name using visible text, aria-label, or another appropriate labeling method. This preserves the visual design while allowing screen readers to announce the link's purpose.

About 31% of home pages have buttons with no accessible name, and the share has risen every year since 2021. The usual culprits are icon-only buttons: a hamburger menu, a close "X" on a modal, a play button, or a carousel arrow. A screen reader announces these as just buttons, so users can't tell whether they open a menu, close a dialog, or submit a form.

The Fix: Give every button an accessible name. Use visible text where you can. For icon-only buttons, add aria-label (for example, aria-label="Close dialog") or visually hidden text inside the button. If the icon is an image, provide alt text that describes the action, not the image.

Around 14% of homepages don't specify the page's language. Without it, screen readers have to guess how the text should be pronounced, which can result in words being read with the wrong pronunciation or accent.

Many users rely on a keyboard instead of a mouse to navigate a website. If a menu only opens on hover, a custom dropdown only responds to clicks, or a button can't receive keyboard focus, those controls become unusable for them.

The Fix: Every interactive element should be reachable with Tab and Shift+Tab and operable with the keys users expect: Enter for links and buttons, Space for buttons and checkboxes, arrow keys inside menus, tabs, and radio groups, and Escape to close dialogs and menus. You can test this by trying to navigate the entire page using only the keyboard.

Developers sometimes remove the browser's default focus outline to improve the visual design but don't replace it with another focus style. As a result, keyboard users can no longer tell which element is currently selected as they move through the page.

The Fix: Never remove a focus outline without providing a clear replacement. Every interactive element should have a visible, high-contrast focus indicator that tells keyboard users where they currently are on the page.

Videos without captions exclude people who are deaf or hard of hearing and make content harder to follow when audio isn't available or practical. Captions also help users understand speech in noisy environments or when the audio quality is poor.

The Fix: Provide accurate captions for every video. If the video contains important spoken information, include a transcript as well. Automatically generated captions are a good starting point, but they should always be reviewed for accuracy.

On mobile, small buttons and links are hard to hit accurately. Users may tap the wrong control, trigger an unintended action, or have to try multiple times before the touch registers. WCAG 2.2 Level AA sets a minimum target size of 24x24 CSS pixels, with 44x44 CSS pixels recommended (WCAG AAA and Apple's own guidance).

The Fix: Give every tappable element enough size and padding to be reliably tappable, even if the visible icon inside it remains small. Aim for 44x44 CSS pixels, and never go below 24x24.

Run a scanner across a page, and it catches a good chunk of what's wrong. Missing alt attributes, contrast ratios below the required threshold, a form field with no label in the code. Automated scanners catch these immediately.

According to most industry estimates, automated tools catch about 30 to 40 percent of accessibility issues. That's worth running. It also means most of what actually breaks a page isn't something a scanner can see at all.

Take alt text, for instance. A scanner can confirm an image has an alt attribute. It can't tell you if that attribute says anything useful. "Image description here" and an actual description of the photo both pass the same check because the tool only confirms the field isn't empty.

Take another example of a custom div button with role="button". A scanner sees the role and perceives it as a button. It has no way of knowing that nobody added the keyboard handling that makes the button actually work, because that's not something a static scan can test. You'd only catch it by unplugging your mouse and tabbing through the page yourself.

Reading order works the same way. A page can pass every automated check and still present its content in an order that makes no sense once you strip away the layout and just listen to it, top to bottom.

None of this means automated tools aren't worth using. It means they're a baseline, not a final check. Treating a clean scan as proof that a page works is exactly how these errors keep making it to production in the first place.

Most of the errors covered here aren't difficult to fix on their own. The harder part is catching them before they enter your codebase. The best time to find these problems is while you're designing, building, and reviewing a feature, not right before a release.

Automated testing should be part of that process. It catches common issues quickly, right as they're introduced, but it isn't a substitute for manual testing. A clean scan only tells you that no automatically detectable issues were found.

Before shipping, spend a few minutes using the page yourself. Navigate using only the keyboard and verify that the focus indicator remains visible. If your site includes forms, menus, or dialogs, ensure they work without a mouse. For critical user journeys, testing with a screen reader will surface issues no automated tool can flag.

Finally, if the same issue keeps recurring, update your design system, component library, or review process to prevent it from happening again. Preventing a bug at the root is always easier than fixing it repeatedly.

Every error in this piece affects a real person trying to use your site. Low contrast text means someone with low vision can't read what you've written. A missing form label means someone using a screen reader can't fill out the form at all. These aren't rare situations; they happen constantly, on ordinary pages, to people who are simply trying to use your product.

That's what accessibility actually comes down to. It is not just a standard to meet, but the same usability you already care about, extended to people who navigate a page using assistive technologies.

#254. Scientists discover a surprising link between vitamin C and cancer survival

Vitamin C plays an important role in keeping the immune system healthy, helping wounds heal, and protecting cells from damage. However, people with precancerous blood disorders and blood cancers often have persistently low vitamin C levels. Researchers have been investigating whether correcting this deficiency could improve their health and treatment outcomes.

New findings from the phase 2 EVITA clinical trial suggest that vitamin C supplementation may offer important benefits for people with precancerous blood conditions and early-stage blood cancers. The randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study was conducted by the Van Andel Institute-Stand Up To Cancer® Epigenetics Dream Team.

The results, published in the American Cancer Society journal CANCER, revealed that patients who received 1,000 mg of oral vitamin C every day for one year experienced fewer adverse events during the study and its follow-up period. The supplement also changed the levels of certain immune signaling molecules, known as cytokines, in ways associated with more favorable outcomes. These changes suggest that vitamin C could influence inflammation-related processes involved in the growth and progression of abnormal blood cells.

Although vitamin C did not significantly affect the rate at which abnormal blood cells expanded, the researchers identified an encouraging result. An exploratory analysis showed that participants who received vitamin C were significantly more likely to be alive after approximately three years of follow-up than those who took a placebo. Scientists emphasize that this potential survival benefit must be verified in a larger phase 3 clinical trial.

Vitamin C Shows Promise for Patients at Risk of Leukemia

"We are tremendously excited and cautiously optimistic that our results could one day translate into a simple way to improve care and outcomes for people with pre-leukemia disorders," said Peter A. Jones, Ph.D., D.Sc. (hon), a distinguished professor at Van Andel Institute. Jones is also a co-corresponding author of the research and co-leader of the VAI-SU2C Epigenetics Dream Team.

"Our approach reflects a growing emphasis on cancer interception -- intervening before cancer becomes established. We are working to build on the trial findings by exploring how exactly vitamin C interacts with pre-cancerous cells, including the idea that it may help restore normal epigenetic function in blood cells at risk of becoming leukemia."

The international research effort was led by Kirsten Grønbæk, M.D., Ph.D., a leading hematologist, professor at the University of Copenhagen, and member of the VAI-SU2C Epigenetics Dream Team.

"These exciting preliminary findings lay the groundwork for a larger clinical trial to explore vitamin C as a possible way to improve patient outcomes," said Grønbæk. "It is too early to make definitive recommendations based on our results, but we are hopeful that a more expansive study will provide further insights."

First Clinical Trial Tests Daily Vitamin C for Precancerous Blood Disorders

The study enrolled 109 patients with either clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance (CCUS) or myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS). Both conditions can develop into acute myeloid leukemia (AML), an aggressive form of blood cancer that is often difficult to treat. The likelihood of developing CCUS and related blood disorders rises with age, making these conditions particularly common among older adults.

Currently, no approved treatment can prevent CCUS from progressing to leukemia. Although stem cell transplantation is an option for treating MDS, many patients cannot undergo the procedure because of age or other medical conditions. None of the people enrolled in the trial were receiving cancer treatment when the study took place.

To determine whether vitamin C might offer benefits, researchers randomly divided participants into two groups. One received 1,000 mg of vitamin C by mouth each day for 12 months. The other received a placebo designed to be indistinguishable from the supplement in appearance, taste, and smell, but containing no vitamin C.

Neither the participants nor the clinical staff knew who was receiving vitamin C until the study concluded. This double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled approach is considered the gold standard in clinical research because it reduces potential bias and allows investigators to assess treatment effects more objectively.

The research was carried out at four hospitals: Copenhagen University Hospital-Rigshospitalet, Copenhagen University Hospital-Herlev, Aalborg University Hospital, and Keck Hospital of the University of Southern California.

What makes EVITA particularly notable is that it was the first randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial to investigate oral vitamin C in patients with conditions that can precede leukemia or with lower-risk myeloid cancers. Earlier studies had primarily examined vitamin C administered intravenously and had focused on solid tumors rather than precancerous blood disorders or early-stage blood malignancies.

EVITA instead evaluated a daily oral dose comparable to that available in many over-the-counter vitamin C supplements.

More Than Half of Patients Had Low Vitamin C Levels

Blood samples collected throughout the study allowed researchers to track participants' vitamin C concentrations and determine whether supplementation corrected deficiencies.

At the beginning of the trial, more than half of the participants had insufficient vitamin C levels. Among those assigned to the supplementation group, blood concentrations returned to normal after they began taking vitamin C.

For patients living with conditions that could eventually develop into leukemia, the possibility of an accessible intervention is especially important. In many cases, physicians have little choice but to monitor the disease and watch for signs that it is progressing.

"The standard approach for patients with pre-cancer blood disorders is to monitor their blood cell counts over time. This is, understandably, very frustrating -- no one wants to hear that they have a condition that increases leukemia risk but there's nothing that can be done other than watch and wait," said Casey O'Connell, M.D., FACP, the Lawrence and Jane Kelly Chair in Hematology at the University of Southern California and lead clinician at USC's trial location.

"The EVITA trial took on this challenge by exploring vitamin C's potential to address the underlying factors that drive leukemia development."

How Vitamin C Could Influence Cancer Development

Scientists have investigated whether vitamin C could play a useful role in cancer treatment for more than seven decades. For much of that time, the evidence remained uncertain because studies produced inconsistent findings and employed different experimental methods.

During the past decade, however, improved research technologies have revealed that vitamin C has important functions beyond its familiar roles in nutrition and immunity. In particular, scientists have uncovered its involvement in controlling gene activity and guiding normal cell development.

One of the most important discoveries concerns TET2, an enzyme that helps prevent cancerous growth by controlling whether particular genes are switched "on" or "off." Vitamin C supports the activity of this enzyme, which helps maintain normal patterns of gene regulation.

Mutations affecting TET2 are frequently found in leukemia and related blood disorders. When the enzyme's normal function is disrupted, abnormal cells can gain an opportunity to multiply and potentially develop into cancer.

Experiments using cancer models have provided additional evidence of vitamin C's possible protective effects. These studies found that supplementation could interfere with cellular changes associated with cancer and slow the progression of leukemia. Notably, the strongest effects appeared when vitamin C was introduced before cancer had become established.

This research helped inspire the idea of using vitamin C as a form of early intervention, potentially targeting the biological changes that precede the development of leukemia.

Before launching EVITA, the VAI-SU2C Epigenetics Dream Team conducted a pilot clinical study in 2016 to determine whether taking vitamin C orally could meaningfully raise its concentration in the blood.

The initial findings suggested that oral supplementation was a practical approach. Encouraged by those results, the researchers launched the phase 2 EVITA trial in 2017.

"This early work was critical because it offered a foundation from which to develop the present trial," said Stine Ulrik Mikkelsen, M.D., Ph.D., the study's first author and a clinician researcher in Grønbæk's laboratory.

"Vitamin C is important because it is involved in a range of biological functions, including the activity of enzymes that regulate normal cell function."

Researchers are continuing to examine the EVITA data to better understand the biological processes that might explain why patients receiving vitamin C showed improved survival.

Although the findings offer a promising direction for future research, additional clinical studies will be essential to determine whether vitamin C supplementation can reliably improve outcomes for people with precancerous blood disorders and early-stage blood cancers.

#260. The Physics Behind Tesla’s Flying Roadster

We’ve been promised flying cars for decades. We finally have one. Well, sort of.

Last week, Elon Musk revealed Tesla’s long-delayed Roadster 2 with a few bells and whistles. The car flies—but not for very long—and the prototype also did a few other things normal cars can’t, like driving up a vertical wall and jumping over Tesla’s entire lineup of vehicles.

This raises two questions: Should we let humans drive up walls and into the air? Given their trouble navigating two-dimensional roads, this seems like an obvious no. But that’s one for lawyers and the Federal Aviation Administration to figure out. The other question is, how did the Roadster 2 prototype do this stuff? We can answer that ourselves, with physics.

Let’s start with how the car drove up a circular ramp and then stayed glued to it Spider-Man–style, completely upside down. I don't think you should ever do this, but this feature of the Roadster 2 can actually improve performance on normal roads. It has to do with friction.

When two surfaces interact—like rubber tires and a road—there can be a force that prevents the surfaces from sliding relative to one another. This interaction is called static friction. We have a simple model for this static friction force where the magnitude (Ff) depends on how hard the two surfaces are pushed together (FN) and the two types of surfaces (μ).

The force pushing the surfaces together is often called the “normal” force, since it's perpendicular to both surfaces. The coefficient of static friction (μ) describes the types of materials interacting. The practical maximum coefficient of friction is 1, and for rubber on asphalt, the value is somewhere around 0.6. What about that ≤ sign? This is because static friction is a “force of constraint.” It applies the force needed to prevent sliding—up to some maximum value.

Suppose a mere mortal car tries to drive up an incline. Here's a force diagram showing the three important forces: friction, normal force, and gravity (the product of mass and the gravitational field).

As the angle (θ) of the incline increases, the gravitational force (mg) has less of a component in the direction perpendicular to the surface. This means the normal force (FN) also decreases, as does the maximum frictional force. If this frictional force is less than the component of the gravitational force pulling down the incline, the car will slip. With a coefficient of 0.6, this maximum angle is about 31 degrees. That's not vertical. It's not even close to vertical. Even with a coefficient of friction at 0.9, you still only get to an incline of 42 degrees.

So how can a car drive up a vertical wall? The answer is to increase the normal force—and, by extension, the frictional force. For a vertical wall, this friction force must be equal to the gravitational force to prevent the car from slipping.

Here's where the Roadster 2 does its first trick. It uses fans to blow air out from under the car. With some side flaps, this lowers the air pressure under the car, akin to how a vacuum cleaner works.

Just for fun, let's estimate the pressure under the Roadster 2 that you would need for it to stick to a wall. The company says the car has a target weight of 1,700 kilograms. We also need to know the contact area. Let’s ballpark it at around 2 square meters. Remember that F = P × A, where P is the pressure and A is the area. But this is really a pressure difference with the external atmospheric pressure at about 105 newtons per square meter. Cranking the numbers gives a bottom pressure of 86 percent of atmospheric pressure. That seems very plausible. Of course, since the seal around the bottom isn't perfect, the fans will need to continue pulling out.

The upside-down Roadster 2 makes for a compelling sales pitch, but the suction actually has a strong use case when it’s driving right side up. Frictional force is the thing that makes a car accelerate. More frictional force, better performance. Pushing the car down means you can accelerate faster and brake faster and make sharper turns. (Performance automakers have harnessed this principle for decades, though not to the degree of Tesla’s new prototype.)

The Roadster 2 also boasts an acceleration of 0 to 60 mph in less than a second. For a traditional car, this is impossible. Since a normal car uses the frictional force between the tires and road to accelerate, it's limited by the coefficient of friction. Using a coefficient of friction at 0.6 (reasonable), the best you can do is 0 to 60 mph in 4.56 seconds. Yes, you can get better tires and you can also increase the frictional force once the car moves faster and interacts with the air (pushing it down)—but those improvements still won’t get you to 60 mph in a second.

The Roadster 2 relies on another form of force: compressed air shooting out the back. This is how rockets work. Since most of us don’t have rockets handy, consider the simple experiment of a toy car propelled by an inflated balloon.

Once the balloon is released, the stretched rubber acts as a force that pushes the air out of the balloon and through an attached tube. But since this air has mass, it will take a force to accelerate it from rest to its exhaust velocity. Here's the cool thing about forces—they are an interaction between two things (like the balloon and the air). That means that when the balloon’s walls push on the air, the air pushes back with an equal force in the opposite direction—an example of Newton’s third law. This push-back force on the balloon is what makes the toy car accelerate. Instead of a wheezing balloon, the Roadster 2 relies on a tank of compressed air.

Now, we get to the flying car part. The Roadster 2 uses the same compressed-air method to push up on the car and get it off the ground. That’s stretching the limits of the term “flying,” especially because there's only a few seconds of thrust that it can get from the tank before the pressure drops. With a lower tank pressure, the air comes out with a lower velocity and less thrust. If I was going to have a flying car, I would hope it could at least fly for 10 minutes of air time.

There's a second issue—acceleration. Since the rockets only last for a short time, they need to apply a large force to get a change in velocity and get the car to leapfrog over a truck or some other obstacle. But with a large force, you also get a large acceleration, and large accelerations are generally bad for humans. We can estimate this acceleration using video analysis. I'm going to use Tracker Video Analysis to mark the location of the car in each frame as it jumps over a Tesla Semi truck. Because we know the truck is about 4 meters tall, we can get the position of the car as a function of time. Here is the vertical position of the Roadster 2 during this jump.

Right there at the beginning of the motion, the vertical velocity of the car is zero, and then it accelerates upward. If we assume constant acceleration during this jump, then the position-time data should be in the shape of a parabola. Fitting a quadratic function to this data gives the acceleration. For this particular motion, I get a rough estimate of about 98 meters per second squared of vertical acceleration. That's right around 10 g’s for humans. That’s just an estimate, of course. Humans can survive that level of force for a short period, but it wouldn’t be pleasant, and it still comes with risks. Maybe that's the reason this particular demo had a robot behind the wheel.

#261. 6 Guidelines for Governing AI

Building systems that keep humans in the loop

Sravan Vadigepalli is an IEEE senior member and a technology executive at Lowe’s Companies, Inc., where he leads enterprise AI strategy, AI products, and partnerships.

For the first 10 years of my career, I worked in product management and data analytics by myself. I wrote database queries that pulled numbers out of corporate systems, built statistical models to predict what customers would buy, and shipped data pipelines that moved information between business systems.

I built and scaled analytics teams at Best Buy and Target, studying how customers shop and what stores should stock. Today I lead enterprise AI transformation at Lowe’s, the Fortune 100 home improvement retailer.

The goal is not to sell artificial intelligence; it is to use it to deliver useful expertise at the moment a customer needs it. In retail and other customer-facing industries, virtual assistants can help people address everyday questions—such as how to repair a leaky faucet—while guiding them toward relevant products, services, or next steps. As these capabilities become more common, technology roles are changing. The work is no longer limited to building AI systems; it also includes defining how they operate: which decisions they can make autonomously, when they must escalate to a person, and which actions must remain off-limits.

That shift—from building AI systems to governing them—is coming for anyone who is accountable for what such systems produce. Not the casual user typing into a chatbot but the engineers, product managers, analysts, and business operators who sign off on work a machine drafted.

It is the subject of the book I recently coauthored, The Enterprise Brain. I call the change the “governor shift,” from executing tasks yourself to setting the intent, principles, and boundaries within systems that execute them for you.

Business operators might not write code; they will decide which pricing exceptions an agent may approve and which it must escalate.

A 2025 report from MIT Media Lab’s Project NANDA found that, despite an estimated US $30 billion to $40 billion in enterprise generative-AI investment, the vast majority of organizations in its dataset had not yet demonstrated measurable profit-and-loss impact. The report estimated that only about 5 percent of integrated pilots were generating substantial value, underscoring how difficult it remains to move from experimentation to scaled business outcomes.

Researchers named the pattern the GenAI Divide, the term I adopted for the book.

The companies rarely lack technology; they use the same models as the 5 percent that are winners. But they lack people who can direct the systems and stand behind the results.

When I talk with people about the shift, their resistance is rarely about technical issues. More often it is about identity: If the AI does the doing, what do I do?

I have watched capable people freeze on that question. I’ve also asked myself the question.

Doing was never really the job, though. Judgment was. Doing was just how we expressed it.

AI has not made judgment less valuable. It has made it the scarcest resource in the organization because, for the first time, one person’s judgment, written down well, can guide thousands of decisions each day.

Judgment has a twin we talk less about: taste. Judgment tells you whether an answer is sound. Taste tells you whether the question was worth asking and which of a hundred defensible options to offer the customer. A machine will happily generate all 100 options, but it cannot tell you which is best.

The AI transition rewards instincts many IEEE members already have: systems thinking, precision about requirements, and honesty about failure modes. The tools have changed, but the discipline has not.

Governing is where taste and judgment stop being soft words and become the work itself.

The people who treat it that way, rather than as a step away from engineering, will define the profession in the age of AI.

#262. Scientists discover a bizarre Jurassic “squid” unlike anything ever seen

Two extraordinarily rare fossils found in Wyoming have revealed a strange new species of squid relative that lived approximately 160 million years ago, during the Jurassic Period.

Named Wyoteuthis linsterorum, the prehistoric creature belonged to an extinct group of marine animals known as belemnites, which were related to modern squid and octopuses. What makes this species particularly remarkable is its unusually thick, barrel-shaped internal skeleton, earning it the distinction of being the "chunkiest" or "fattest" belemnite ever identified.

An international research team from the United States, United Kingdom, and New Zealand, including Dr. Dean Lomax, an Honorary Research Fellow at The University of Manchester, detailed the discovery in the scientific journal Papers in Palaeontology.

An Unusually Thick Jurassic Squid Relative

Belemnites are among the most frequently discovered fossils in the world. They are best known for their distinctive, bullet-shaped internal skeletal structures, called rostra or guards, which are often the only parts of the animals that survive in the fossil record.

Wyoming's Sundance Formation, which preserves evidence of an ancient inland sea that once stretched across much of the western United States, has yielded millions of these fossils. According to Wyoming paleontologist and study coauthor Jessica Lippincott, they are commonly nicknamed "squid butts" in the region.

Most belemnite fossils from Wyoming have a narrow, bullet-shaped appearance, typically measuring between 5 and 10 centimeters in length and just 1 to 2 centimeters across. The newly identified species is dramatically different. Its rostrum measures approximately 10 centimeters long but reaches an extraordinary 6 centimeters in width, making it the thickest known example of its kind.

Scientists estimate that the living animal, including its arms, could have reached a total length of approximately 60 centimeters, or about two feet.

Rare Fossils Reveal a Surprising Evolutionary Secret

Wyoming paleontologist Bill Wahl, who has devoted much of his career to searching for fossils across the state, recognized that the unusual specimen deserved closer examination. He said the belemnite "stuck out like a sore thumb" compared with the countless others found in the region.

Wahl assembled an international team to investigate the fossils, bringing in specialists including belemnite expert Alexey Ippolitov, a PhD student now based at Victoria University of Wellington in New Zealand.

The researchers used computed tomography (CT) scanning to investigate the fossils' internal structures without damaging the specimens. These detailed scans revealed an unexpected connection to an ancient belemnite family that scientists had believed disappeared millions of years before this animal lived.

Ippolitov said he was "deeply surprised" to encounter such an unusual specimen from a group that has already been studied extensively. Belemnites have been the subject of paleontological research for more than 200 years, making the identification of such a radically different species especially remarkable.

The creature's unusual proportions may also offer clues about how it lived and hunted.

Ippolitov explains: "A possible explanation is that their relatively large size gave them an advantage when hunting co-occurring smaller belemnites of the genus Pachyteuthis. Modern squid, after all, are hardly picky when it comes to prey: they readily hunt not only other species of cephalopods, but sometimes even their own kind."

A Fossil Discovery Nearly 30 Years in the Making

Although the species has only now been formally described, its discovery began almost three decades ago.

The first known specimen was found in the late 1990s by Dr. Burkhard Pohl, founder of the Wyoming Dinosaur Center. While examining fossils, Pohl noticed a belemnite that was considerably larger than any he had previously encountered.

Despite its unusual appearance, the specimen remained unstudied in a museum collection drawer for many years.

A breakthrough came when fossil collector Cliff Linster discovered a second specimen near Ten Sleep, Wyoming. He donated the fossil to the Wyoming Dinosaur Center in 2019, providing researchers with another example of the mysterious animal.

With two specimens available for comparison, scientists finally had enough evidence to recognize and formally describe an entirely new species.

The genus name Wyoteuthis means "a squid from Wyoming," while the species name linsterorum recognizes Cliff Linster and his family for their contributions. Linster discovered one of the only two known examples and ensured it was available for scientific research.

Lippincott emphasized how extraordinary the discovery was, even for researchers familiar with Wyoming's abundant belemnite fossils.

"After spending years fossil hunting in Wyoming and collecting countless fossils of the common Pachyteuthis belemnites here in Wyoming, this one is unlike anything I have ever seen! It goes to show that we are still finding new species of fossils all the time, and people who rockhound or collect invertebrate fossils can also contribute to science."

Honoring a Fossil Hunter's Remarkable Legacy

Sadly, Cliff Linster passed away in June before the research was published. He had been aware that scientists were investigating his discovery and was enthusiastic about seeing the unusual fossil receive formal scientific recognition.

The Linster family already had an impressive history of fossil discoveries, including their association with the famous dinosaur Bambiraptor.

For British paleontologist Dr. Dean Lomax, the discovery also carries considerable personal meaning. Lomax, an Honorary Research Fellow at The University of Manchester and an 1851 Research Fellow at the University of Bristol, first worked alongside Cliff and his wife, Sandy, while excavating dinosaur fossils as a teenager.

He said: "I met Cliff and Sandy in 2009, when I spent a week digging up a dinosaur bonebed on their property in northern Montana. I have fond memories of that dig, listening to Cliff share his passion for fossils, including this belemnite. I'm honored to help name this fossil after Cliff. It's bittersweet that he didn't get to see it published, but it's wonderful that we can immortalize him and his family name in the history of paleontology. The find is incredibly rare, which might be due to some ecological adaptations to the environment at the time. Perhaps its rarity is a result of narrow specialization."

Lomax has extensive experience studying Wyoming's prehistoric animals, including collecting fossils and excavating plesiosaurs and ichthyosaurs from the Sundance Formation. He is also the author of the recently published book "The Secret Lives of Dinosaurs," which features fossils from Wyoming.

The discovery highlights how even well-studied fossil groups can still hold major surprises, particularly when unusual specimens are preserved and made available for scientific investigation.

Both known fossils of Wyoteuthis linsterorum are now on display at the Wyoming Dinosaur Center in Thermopolis, Wyoming, where visitors can see the extraordinary remains of one of the Jurassic seas' most unusual squid relatives.

#264. When It Makes Sense To “Block” The Main Thread

I Code. I Write. Meme Rules. Ok, but seriously, I’m a web and mobile developer who writes a lot. In my spare time, I like to build products that would … More about Victor ↬

We’ve all heard of the sacred rule in modern web development, the rule never to be broken. The rule of “Never block the main thread.”

You almost can’t miss it as a web developer; it’s in almost every performance guide, and to be fair, it is good advice. We all know the browser’s main thread is single-threaded, meaning it can only do one thing at a time.

Plus, as we know, the main thread isn’t ours alone; we share it with the browser’s rendering engine, input handlers, and other critical tasks. As a result, the less time we hold onto the main thread, the more responsive an app feels. That leads us to share tasks with background workers as we’ve convinced ourselves there should be a hard line between the UI and any computation, and that line shouldn’t be crossed.

And that is what a “recommended” architecture looks like.

But I dare say that *sometimes*, moving the data to a worker is slower than just letting the main thread do the work.

I found this out a few months ago while building a Chrome extension with screenshotting features called Fastary. I kept finding a latency of about 2 to 3 seconds in all my testing, even after using an Offscreen Document (a background process in Chrome extensions) to handle the canvas operations. A screenshot task should feel instant without lag, after all.

It is quite ironic that by reflex, we move work away from the main thread to avoid freezing the UI, but sometimes the act of moving that work (e.g., serializing, copying, and deserializing) can also freeze the UI. And sometimes the recommended approach of letting the background do the work can be slower than just doing the work on the main thread.

To put things in perspective, let’s understand why we isolate browser contexts and how they communicate with each other, with emphasis on the communication part.

A browser is more than a single environment. Different environments are running at the same time, each having its own memory space, what it can access, and rules:

Each one of these is isolated from the others. A web worker or background script lives in a different memory space from the main thread. They cannot just reach and read each other’s variables or logic, and this is known as the “shared-nothing” architecture.

How do these isolated environments communicate? They explicitly message each other back and forth using APIs, like postMessage().

postMessage() tells the browser to take a piece of data and deliver it to the context that requested it. But to do this, the browser relies on the Structured Clone Algorithm (SCA).

You’re probably familiar with JSON.stringify(). SCA is similar, but much stronger and smarter. In its simplest form, SCA is a deep, recursive copy operation, i.e., cloning. It walks through the entire data structure it is given, clones every single value, serializes it into a transportable format, ships those bytes to the target contexts, and then reconstructs the original object on the receiving side.

SCA is fast, or maybe fast-ish… For a small regular config object like {theme: "dark"}, it is imperceptible; you don’t even notice it. The story changes, however, when dealing with heavy data because the SCA is a synchronous blocking O(n) operation, i.e., the cost increases linearly with the size of your data.

Let’s put that into perspective. A user clicks a button, and internally, an 8MB image payload is sent to a background worker for processing. When you call postMessage(), the main thread must immediately stop what it is doing to run this serialization and copying process.

I’m sure some of you are already thinking, “Why not just use Transferable objects?” And that is a valid point. Let’s talk about that.

Developers who really pursue ultra-high-performance web apps usually use Transferable objects (e.g., ArrayBuffer, ImageBitmap, or MessagePort) to bypass the Structured Clone Algorithm. This is because when you transfer an object, you’re not making a copy (like SCM). Instead, the browser switches ownership of the data from one context to another.

The browser performs a hand-off whereby the sending context loses access to the data instantly, and the receiving context takes full control. It is actually insanely fast. According to Chrome Developers’ benchmark, transferring a massive 32MB ArrayBuffer can take under 7ms, compared to about 300ms when cloning with SCM. That’s a 43x speed boost.

Why do we even bother isolating contexts at all? Why not just leave it all to the main thread?

Offloading long-running CPU tasks to a background thread is absolutely the right thing to do. The browser needs to paint a new frame every 16.6ms to keep things fluid; that means any task that takes >50ms is generally considered “long”. Offloading to the background is absolutely the right thing to do.

The issue, however, is that we’ve turned this “never block the main thread” into an absolute rule, without asking is this task expensive to process or expensive to move?

I have come to realize now that the rule is less “never block the main thread” than “never block the main thread for too long.”

My goal with the Fastary extension was to make it feel like a native app, running as smoothly and instantly as you would expect a native app to.

As you already know, I took the recommended approach to use the Offscreen Document to handle DOM work in the background. But to my surprise, that took a different turn.

The Offscreen Document API is a clear winner. You create a hidden, undisplayed document that runs entirely in the background. It has a DOM and supports Canvas. For example, if I want to crop a screenshot, stitch multiple screenshots together, perform heavy image manipulation, or add a watermark, Offscreen Document was made for that.

I figured out that when captureVisibleTab() takes a screenshot, it returns a Base64 URL string, and on a standard 1080p screen, that string could be approximately 1MB or more, depending on how detailed the image is. It gets even more interesting on modern Retina displays (e.g., MacBooks) as they tend to automatically double the image’s size by default.

Keep in mind that since the image payload could be doubled and extension messaging relies on JSON serialization (as of this writing), we potentially deal with massive synchronous communication that costs an entire round trip.

The image string data is JSON-serialized at least twice: once when going into the Offscreen Document and once coming back out with the processed results to the background worker. The actual image processing (cropping) done inside the Offscreen Document was fast, no doubt, but I can’t say the same about the transfer overhead.

As if the latency itself wasn’t enough, I noticed a rather subtle bug — which, now that I think of it, was more of my ignorance. After a screenshot was taken, the crop result was completely off in a way that either weirdly scaled the image or resulted in incorrect coordinates.

It turns out that when a user selects a region to crop, the content script gets the box coordinates using getBoundingClientRect(), which is measured in CSS pixels; this is what the DOM uses. But when the screenshot is captured natively in Chrome, the browser doesn’t crop it automatically; it instead uses the physical hardware pixels to get the full screen capture. And the browser uses devicePixelRatio (DPR) to know how many physical pixels should represent one CSS pixel. Basically, if a user on a Retinal display (DPR = 2) highlights an area of 400x300 CSS pixels, the actual captured image area is 800x600 physical pixels.

For an accurate crop, I needed to apply these two different measurement systems with the right DPR, i.e., scale the crop coordinates by the DPR. But remember, Offscreen Documents have no physical display. Processing any image would have a default DPR equal to 1. To fix this, I would have to capture the exact devicePixelRatio from the active tab, serialize it, pass it alongside the image payload, and manually do the scaling math inside the Offscreen Document. The complexity starts to compound.

What if I broke the golden rule and did the work on the main thread instead?

Some developers will argue that UI tasks are the only things that should run on the main thread, but I don’t fully agree with that. Personally, I believe that user explicitly-invoked actions that need immediate results can sometimes get a solid pass to run on the main thread, provided the work is incredibly fast (e.g., 1s).

But there’s an elephant in the room that you may have noticed.

Sure, the image is now processed on the main thread, and the background manipulates the canvas in the active tab. I could technically be blocking the main thread. That’s where I amended the “no blocking the main thread” rule to “no blocking the main thread for too long.” In this specific case, at least, blocking the main thread for a task the user requests for approximately one second is justifiable. It works conversely as well: maybe don’t isolate processes if the data transfer cost is greater than the processing cost.

I’ve boiled it down to a mental model that depends on whether the task is:

These are tasks where the primary cost is computation and not the size of the data itself. These are tasks where most of the time is spent on doing calculations or heavy transformations, e.g., image compression, audio profiling, physics simulation, etc.

The transfer cost for these tasks is minuscule compared to the actual work.

These tasks are the exact opposite. These tasks are only expensive because of the size. The processing time is almost insignificant, but the data is expensive to transport, e.g., image cropping, filtering an array, shallow copy, etc.

In my specific case, offloading the task to the background falls mostly into negative-sum efficiency. If we are talking about moving megabytes of data to perform a 50ms operation, there is no benefit to offloading it to the background.

Looking at this, if the “background processing time” is the most dominant task in your operation, then isolation is the clear winner. But if serialization, plus deserialization, plus transit exceeds that cost, then there’s no need to isolate things.

And if you can’t figure out if the task is CPU-heavy or data-heavy, it certainly doesn’t hurt to measure it, for example, using performance.mark() and performance.measure() around postMessage calls to profile the transfer cost.

IEEE Life Fellow Christos Cassandras is the recipient of the 2026 IEEE Intelligent Transportation Systems Society Outstanding Research Award.

Christos Cassandras has spent more than 40 years studying how computers and machines make decisions. Lately, his work has focused on a problem nearly every driver knows well: sitting at a red light with no cross traffic in sight, wondering why it takes so long for the light to turn green.

Cassandras, an IEEE Life Fellow, is a professor at Boston University, where he headed its systems engineering division, a graduate program he helped create.

He is the recipient of this year’s IEEE Intelligent Transportation Systems Society Outstanding Research Award for his work on autonomous vehicles and the systems that help them drive—work that could one day get rid of traffic lights altogether.

That idea—proving with mathematical models and intelligent algorithms using cars’ relative speeds, mass, and distance as inputs that self-driving cars will behave safely before they are ever let loose on the streets—has become the foundation of Cassandras’s career and the reason his name keeps coming up in conversations about transportation.

In the 1990s he theorized that a machine such as a car could be understood as two things at once: an object obeying the laws of physics and a computer processing information and making decisions. This led to what we now call cyber physical systems. That framework is still the standard way engineers describe a car’s mechanical behavior.

“Ultimately, the key word is safety,” Cassandras says. “You would not buy a self-driving car unless the manufacturer could guarantee that it’s safe for you.”

Born in Athens, Cassandras was a teenager during Greece’s military dictatorship, a period he says scarred him and many others but also taught him some hard lessons. His father was a partner in an insulation manufacturing company, and his mother was a homemaker.

His friends pulled Cassandras toward engineering. As a teenager, he ran with a group of classmates who read about philosophy, history, and science. They were especially struck by the 1969 Apollo 11 moon landing and the questions it raised about the technology that made it possible.

That same year also marked the first time a piece of data was sent from one computer to another—an early step toward the creation of the Internet. Several inspiring teachers pushed his curiosity about technology even further.

He was awarded a scholarship to study in the United States, and he enrolled at Yale, where he first studied physics and philosophy before switching to engineering. He earned his bachelor’s degree in engineering and applied science in 1977. At the time, Yale didn’t yet offer separate degrees in fields such as electrical or mechanical engineering.

From there, Cassandras went to Stanford, earning a master’s degree in electrical engineering in 1978. There, he became interested in game theory, the mathematical study of decision-making and strategy.

A mentor pointed him to Harvard, home to a leading game theory researcher at the time. His new mentor, Professor Yu-Chi “Larry” Ho, now an IEEE Life Fellow, advised him to abandon game theory, which Ho considered a scientific dead end at the time. Ho suggested a newer, less-explored area of research, that of emerging dynamic systems in modern technology whose behavior could be understood and managed through occurrences of discrete events.

Cassandras took the advice. He earned a second master’s degree from Harvard the following year and completed his Ph.D. in applied mathematics there in 1982. He and Ho remain friends and colleagues.

Ask people in intelligent transportation systems circles why Cassandras’s name carries so much weight, and the answer traces back to a theoretical shift he helped pioneer starting in the 1980s. Cassandras recognized that human-made systems—computers, factory machines and, eventually, vehicles—operate on a different kind of logic than the one that governs the natural world.

The physical world runs on time-driven physics, the same math Isaac Newton used centuries ago. But machines built by people, he realized, are better understood as event-driven: They mostly sit still until something happens, like a button click or a part arriving on a conveyor belt.

That insight didn’t come from a textbook. It took shape during Cassandras’s early graduate work, when he was handed a real-world problem. He was assigned to figure out how to manage the buffers—the temporary holding areas for car parts—on an assembly line for Fiat, the Italian carmaker.

Solving that concrete, practical puzzle helped him see how event-driven thinking could describe an entire class of systems that classical physics-based math couldn’t handle well. This came to be known as discrete event systems.

By the early 1990s, that insight had matured into a formal framework: hybrid systems theory, now known as cyber physical systems theory. The idea is that a machine can be understood as an object obeying the laws of physics and as a computer obeying event-driven dynamics.

After completing his doctorate, Cassandras wanted to test himself outside of academia. He spent about a year and a half as a systems engineer at Information and Technology for Production (ITP), a small manufacturing-automation startup in the Boston area. The experience convinced him that the “real world” wasn’t so different from academic life, and he returned to research while continuing to consult for the company for roughly a decade. He has kept up the habit of staying connected to industry throughout his career.

In 1984 he joined the University of Massachusetts Amherst as a faculty member in the electrical and computer engineering department. Building on the ideas born out of the Fiat project, he helped pioneer research into event-driven systems.

That was a shift away from the traditional math used to describe the physical world toward systems built around discrete events. The transition, driven by the rise of computers, became the backbone of Cassandras’s research for the next three decades.

In 1997, after years of commuting weekly between Amherst and Boston for his consulting work, he joined Boston University as a tenured full professor in what was then its manufacturing engineering department. As the field evolved in the early 2000s with the rise of the Internet and sensor technology, Cassandras helped establish the university’s systems engineering division. In 2008 he became the research program’s first director, a role he held until stepping down two months ago.

Cassandras’s recent research has focused on mathematically proving vehicle safety before a car is road-tested. He has developed models built around measurable quantities: a vehicle’s position, speed, and distance to nearby vehicles. That lets connected and automated vehicles cooperate during tricky maneuvers such as changing lanes on a highway in traffic.

He has extended the approach to a bigger idea: eliminating traffic lights. One of his more striking projects involves a chronically congested intersection near his office in Boston, where Commonwealth Avenue meets the Boston University Bridge. Using computer simulations, he and his team modeled what would happen if they removed all the traffic lights, and cars simply coordinated with each other instead. The results, he says, showed improvements not only in how smoothly traffic would flow but also in safety and energy efficiency.

Cassandras acknowledges that widespread adoption is still far off. For now, only a small percentage of vehicles on the road are capable of that kind of communication. But he’s testing similar coordination systems using small robots in his BU lab, working to bring the idea closer to reality and to exploit the intelligence of cooperating autonomous vehicles even when they are only a small fraction of the actual cars on the road.

He says his perspective has evolved in recent years from viewing humans as obstacles to smooth automation toward designing technology that actually works for people.

Through projects with Boston on smart city initiatives, he learned that not everyone can afford or access new technology—a lesson that reshaped how he approaches his research.

“It’s not just about technology,” he says. “It’s really about technology and people.”

His influence in the field has been amplified by some of his students. Several of his Ph.D. students have developed safety algorithms at companies including Aptiv and Zoox, both of which build technology for autonomous and connected vehicles. He wrote a book, titled Safe Autonomy with Control Barrier Functions: Theory and Applications, with one of his former students. It lays out the mathematical foundations of autonomous-vehicle safety, with specific applications to intelligent transportation.

Cassandras joined IEEE as a student member while he was a graduate student. He was drawn in by the discounted student conference registration fees, he says.

That early, practical decision grew into something bigger: a professional community with which he has stayed connected for more than 40 years.

He has served as president of the IEEE Control Systems Society, his primary professional home within IEEE. He has served on the conference publications committee and is currently involved with IEEE Publishing Ethics.

IEEE’s greatest value to his career, he says, has come through the relationships he has built at its conferences and serving on committees. Connections with colleagues and IEEE leadership have shaped his thinking and opened doors throughout his career, he says.

Looking back on more than four decades of work, Cassandras says the goal has never really been about the technology for its own sake.

“After all,” he says, “we’re doing all this to make society better and facilitate the comfortable lives of all of humanity.”

#267. How ‘Final Fantasy VII Revelation’ Strikes the Balance Between Old and New

If you're on the brink of completing one of the most ambitious projects in video game history, you're probably stressed. If said project is the decade-spanning Final Fantasy VII Remake trilogy, a ground-up rebuild and drastic expansion of one of the most influential games of the last 30 years, you’d probably be even more anxious.

Naoki Hamaguchi doesn’t seem stressed, though. When I ask the Square Enix studio head and game director how he’s feeling as the release of Final Fantasy VII Revelation draws near, capping a series he’s worked on for over a decade, the only reply is a wry “I was just so focused, it really felt like it passed in a flash.”

Hamaguchi’s calm demeanor is all the more surprising given the weight of expectation surrounding the project. The original Final Fantasy VII, released on Sony's PlayStation in 1997, was a pop-cultural phenomenon—not the Japanese-RPG series’ first Western release, but undeniably its breakthrough title; one that arrived right as gaming was growing out of its kids-only niche and into a mainstream medium.

It’s still regarded as one of the best Final Fantasy entries, and its tale of reluctant hero Cloud Strife and his scrappy band of eco-warrior friends battling an all-powerful corporation destroying the planet is more relevant than ever.

A remake has been brewing since the PlayStation 2 era, and even teased with a cinematic tech demo alongside the PS3 reveal. Yet when Final Fantasy VII Remake finally arrived in 2020, toward the end of the PS4’s lifespan, it came with a twist—this wasn’t the same game with modern visuals and mechanics, but rather a glimpse of an alternate reality, one where events could potentially play out differently.

The idea was expanded on in 2024’s second chapter, Final Fantasy VII Rebirth, which saw characters hopping between timelines—but Hamaguchi is keen not to label it his attempt at a Marvel Cinematic Universe–style Final Fantasy VII multiverse.

“When we started approaching these things, seven years or more ago, I don't believe there were so many other pieces of media tackling those same themes at the time,” Hamaguchi laughs. “We don't really tend to use the word ‘multiverse’—we talk about [the story being] ‘multilayered.’ Actually, the word that comes up most often is ‘reunion.’”

That word may resonate with fans of the original, where test subjects experimented on using cells from an alien named Jenova, including Sephiroth, were drawn to each other (and that’s the shortest possible explanation).

Does that hint at where Revelation’s increasingly complex, layered narrative may be heading, with cosmic variations on Final Fantasy VII’s events all merging? Hamaguchi won’t be drawn, but offers that developer Square Enix is “looking at it from the perspective of these layers coming back together and reuniting—quite a unique perspective within the lore that we have.”

Other lore changes include the vastly expanded three-act structure of the Remake trilogy, which allows—or sometimes necessitates—reshuffling characters, locations, and events, placing many out of sequence from the original.

One of the biggest examples is the nation of Wutai, home of Yuffie, a teenage ninja who becomes an ally to Cloud. In the original game, she was an optional recruitable character, and Wutai was two huts on the world map, with only a few screens worth of houses and temples to explore when players entered it. In Revelation, it’s vast. This Wutai is a thriving city with people to talk to and secrets to uncover; you’ll even need to use Chocobos (Final Fantasy’s signature yellow bird mounts) to explore. It’s also somewhere players can actually believe is caught in a war with Midgar, the urban megalopolis where Remake took place. A lot more is going on here than there was back in 1997.

Some may think that holding Wutai (and Rocket Town, another key location) back for the third Remake entry was a way to pad out the equivalent of the original version’s third disc, which mainly left players to mop up side quests and chase down some optional boss battles before the finale. However, Hamaguchi says the opposite was true—even after two 100-plus hour previous games, he was worried about how much was still left to fit into Revelation.

“I did see people online talking about things after Rebirth, saying, ‘Are you going to have enough to fill up another game? What's gonna happen?’” he says. “I was sat there like, ‘No, this is the opposite problem!’ I'm looking at everything we've still got to put in, and how are we going to get it in there? We actually had to be quite careful with what we put in there to avoid having too much content.”

Revelation may still feel crammed, though. It brings in the final two playable characters from the original game, aeronaut Cid Highwind and were-beast gunman Vincent Valentine, expanding party options and battle combos. It also adds its own twist on the memorable Emerald, Ruby, and Ultima Weapon super-boss battles—incredibly challenging fights against literally god-tier foes, often with strict constraints to test players’ skills.

Along with the sizable new areas, the world is far more open, with returning locations from the prior two games promising extra story avenues and battles to pursue when revisited. Players can soar the skies in Cid’s airship, the Highwind (points for personal branding), before parachuting back down to explore further. “I'm not sure if there's anything other than Revelation that is really an open world on a truly ‘world’ scale, on a planetary scale,” Hamaguchi boasts.

On top of that, Hamaguchi has previously said it may take multiple playthroughs to see everything in the game. That might sound like a daunting time sink to some, but Hamaguchi isn’t fazed. “Personally, I don't have any reservations," he says. "I do understand that there were some people who said there's too much content in Rebirth, but I think essentially that boils down to preference.” Variety matters more than volume, though, and Hamaguchi says the scale has expanded with diverse gameplay.

There are a few olive branches for players who like their games big but not all-consuming, though. That multiple-playthrough requirement will take the form of a chapter select, so you can revisit specific scenes and see events from different perspectives. Rebirth's notoriously difficult and often repetitive trophy requirements are also being toned down, but Hamaguchi says “that's not to say that the achievements are easy—not just anyone is going to be able to get all of them.”

If there’s one thing Hamaguchi actively does want to change with Final Fantasy VII Revelation, it’s players’ perspectives on what Final Fantasy fundamentally is. Even though the series has evolved over the years—to the point where 2023’s Final Fantasy XVI was more of an action game than an RPG—outsiders often still see it as staid and formulaic.

“I think that a lot of people have the image or preconception about the Final Fantasy series, that it tends to be quite story-focused,” Hamaguchi says. “That is, of course, fantastic, but I also wanted to really showcase the gameplay and to show that Final Fantasy as a franchise, as a series, could stand alongside other open-world titles that are really celebrated for their gameplay, and I thought that would bring in more fans.”

“Showcase” seems right—although my playtest of Revelation barely scratched the surface, there’s clearly a panoply of stuff to do at every turn, with almost every activity offering a fresh way to engage with the world. Yet for long-term fans, warm nostalgia weaves through, as with the new “Function Integrated Tactical Suitwear” system. This fashion-focused (clock the acronym) tweak to the combat system lets players dress the cast as RPG character classes from the pre-FF7 games—think Knights, White Mages, Summoners, and sundry other Final Fantasy figures—allowing for extraordinary customization over who you bring into battle and how they function. It feels retro but modern, and just one way Revelation writes a love letter to the whole series.

A balance of old and new feels like the perfect capstone for a project like the Remake trilogy, celebrating the industry-shaking original Final Fantasy VII while shaping the franchise's future. More importantly, it’s already attracting the people who will help build it.

“It's been over 10 years since the Remake announcement now, and I’m starting to come across people who are saying ‘Final Fantasy 7 Remake inspired me to get into the games industry,’” Hamaguchi says. “They're coming up now, and they're saying, ‘I want to make Final Fantasy, I have these ideas.’ I think that's wonderful. Nothing could make me happier, and I'll just keep trying my best on the cutting edge to push things forward.”

#269. Your aging brain may be trading memory for something more valuable

Growing older is often associated with memory loss and declining mental abilities. However, scientists are questioning whether these changes tell the whole story. Rather than simply becoming less capable, the brain may be adapting to a new stage of life in which accumulated knowledge, wisdom, and understanding become more important than remembering every detail.

Researchers at the University of Arizona have proposed a different way of understanding cognitive aging. Drawing on findings from molecular biology and brain imaging, they suggest that the changes occurring in older brains may reflect a natural stage of human development rather than deterioration alone. Their work, published in Perspectives on Psychological Science, offers a new perspective on why thinking and memory change with age.

The researchers call their proposed explanation the adaptive aging hypothesis. It suggests that the brain gradually adjusts its priorities to make better use of a lifetime of accumulated experience. This could help explain why older adults often have valuable strengths in teaching, mentoring, storytelling, and passing knowledge to younger generations.

"If we look at normative aging as a story of just decline, we are really missing the boat," said study coauthor Fabian-Xosé Fernandez, an associate professor of cognition and neural systems at the University of Arizona. "The older brain is optimized for different things than the younger brain, predominantly the sharing of stored expertise and social relationships."

How the Brain Shifts From Memory to Meaning

Two brain regions play important roles in how people remember and interpret their experiences. The hippocampus helps preserve specific information about events, including their details and circumstances. The frontal cortex, meanwhile, is more involved in extracting broader meaning, recognizing patterns, and understanding the central message of an experience, sometimes called its "gist."

According to the researchers, the balance between these systems changes during healthy aging. As people grow older, the brain tends to place less emphasis on activity in the hippocampus and rely more on the frontal cortex. This shift may allow the brain to focus more on interpreting information through existing knowledge rather than recording every new experience in precise detail.

"The fact that you see this across people and animal models suggests there is an evolutionary driving force," Fernandez said. "One could argue you are developing your entire life, and development occurs in lockstep with the needs of your brain. When you're younger, the hippocampus turns on because you don't have a knowledge base or know how the world works. A kid absorbs information like a sponge because they are building an archive of information. In early life, the balance is tilted toward recollecting declarative memories with precision, detail, and context."

Young brains need to gather enormous amounts of new information because they are still building a foundation for understanding the world. By later adulthood, however, people have already developed extensive stores of memories, concepts, and experiences. Connecting new information to what they already know may become more useful than retaining every individual detail.

The researchers suggest that this changing balance reflects a shift in how the brain processes experience. Instead of concentrating primarily on collecting detailed memories, it increasingly emphasizes understanding relationships, identifying underlying meaning, and applying previously acquired knowledge.

Why Evolution May Favor Wisdom in Older Adults

Evidence of similar changes in animal models raises the possibility that this transition has an evolutionary explanation. Fernandez suggests that the aging brain may be adapting to help individuals preserve and share the knowledge they have acquired throughout their lives.

From this perspective, the ability to draw on decades of experience could be especially valuable for making judgments, guiding others, and transferring important information between generations. Older adults may therefore play a distinct role in supporting younger people through mentorship, teaching, and storytelling.

"Given those goals, it makes sense that you aren't interested in nitty-gritty details," Fernandez said. "You are interested in safeguarding the vast knowledge you already have. Evolution may be saying that if you make it long enough, there's a need to transmit this information, so we put into motion biological changes that enforce and protect your semantic and autobiographical memories."

Under this hypothesis, changes in memory and thinking are not necessarily signs that the brain is functioning less effectively. They may instead reflect a different set of priorities, with greater emphasis on preserving meaningful knowledge and using it to understand new situations.

Why Healthy Brain Aging Is Different From Alzheimer's

The adaptive aging hypothesis could also provide scientists with another way to examine the differences between normal cognitive aging and Alzheimer's disease.

Although both involve changes in memory, Fernandez emphasizes that Alzheimer's should not be understood as ordinary aging taken to an extreme. The biological processes involved are fundamentally different.

"When I was in grad school, people thought about Alzheimer's as a more intense version of cognitive aging, but that is just not the case," Fernandez said. "They are completely different."

During typical aging, the brain's gradual shift away from relying heavily on the hippocampus and toward greater involvement of the frontal cortex generally unfolds between the fifth and seventh decades of life. In Alzheimer's disease, however, abnormal processes involving proteins such as amyloid and tau may interfere with this transition.

One important distinction involves activity in the hippocampus. While healthy aging is generally associated with reduced emphasis on this region, Alzheimer's can involve unusually high levels of hippocampal activity. Rather than supporting the normal progression of cognitive development, disease-related changes may disrupt the brain networks responsible for memory and understanding.

"In normative aging, hippocampal activation is deemphasized, which is probably what should be happening in a 70-year-old. But in many cases of Alzheimer's, it turns out the hippocampus is hyperactive," he said. "This beautiful developmental transition is completely disrupted in Alzheimer's disease by pathological processes that mess with episodic memory and interfere with circuits that allow you to think about your own life, lexicon, and vocabulary."

These disruptions can affect the ability to recall personal experiences and access stored knowledge, including words and their meanings. Understanding how these changes differ from the adaptations associated with healthy aging may help researchers develop a clearer picture of normal brain development and neurodegenerative disease.

Embracing the Hidden Strengths of the Aging Brain

The researchers argue that society may need to reconsider what successful brain aging looks like. Encouraging older adults to process information exactly as younger people do may not always be the most productive approach.

Fernandez suggests that placing excessive emphasis on remembering precise details could work against the brain's changing priorities. "For many individuals, the weight of how you process information should probably not be so hippocampal-centric by the sixth or seventh decade."

Instead of judging older adults primarily by abilities that tend to weaken with age, the researchers encourage greater recognition of the capabilities that may become especially valuable later in life.

Creating more opportunities for social interaction could be one way to support these strengths. Relationships between older and younger generations may be particularly beneficial because they provide opportunities to exchange experiences, share expertise, and preserve knowledge that might otherwise be lost.

"People see older individuals and look at them as not being on par with younger individuals across a lot of metrics," Fernandez says. "The way I look at it is that you've got someone with a brain that has been active for several decades and has seen a lot. There is tremendous value there, and we don't do a good job of unlocking it."

The central message of the adaptive aging hypothesis is that growing older may involve more than a gradual reduction in cognitive abilities. Although some mental skills become less reliable with age, the brain may also be adapting to make better use of the knowledge and experience accumulated over a lifetime. Recognizing those strengths could change how society understands aging and the contributions older adults can make.
