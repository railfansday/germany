New Jersey’s Senate Judiciary Committee has advanced S597 with bipartisan support, proposing to replace criminal penalties for gambling by people under 21 with civil fines of up to $500 for a first offense, $1,000 for a second and $2,000 for a third. The bill would direct the money collected to compulsive-gambling prevention, education and treatment programs.

The proposal changes the legal framework but does not remove penalties. It would cover under-21 gambling at casinos or simulcasting facilities, as well as adults who allow someone in their legal care or custody to gamble and casino licensees or employees who permit underage gambling.

S597 applies to people who gamble at a casino or simulcasting facility before turning 21. Its scope also reaches people responsible for a minor and casino licensees or employees when they allow underage gambling at those facilities.

The bill therefore concerns more than the young person placing a wager. For casino operators, the proposed framework retains a direct compliance consequence where a licensee or employee allows someone under 21 to gamble. The available reporting does not describe changes to age-check procedures or other operator requirements, so the proposal’s stated effect is the change in penalties.

That distinction matters: S597 does not simply eliminate consequences for underage gambling. It would replace a disorderly persons criminal offense with escalating civil fines, while preserving accountability for the categories of conduct identified in the measure.

Age-verification enforcement has also featured in other jurisdictions’ gambling regulation. In Ontario, the Alcohol and Gaming Commission of Ontario fined Betty Gaming over underage-gambling issues, a separate case that illustrates how regulators can address age-related compliance concerns through operator enforcement.

Under S597, the proposed maximum fine would rise with repeat offenses: up to $500 for a first violation, up to $1,000 for a second and up to $2,000 for a third. The source reporting describes those amounts as the proposed civil-fine schedule, rather than criminal penalties.

Money collected would support programs for compulsive gambling, including prevention, education and treatment. The Press of Atlantic City reported that the funding could go to programs such as those provided by the Council on Compulsive Gambling of New Jersey.

The funding arrangement is part of the bill’s approach to enforcement: penalties would remain, but their proceeds would be directed toward efforts intended to address gambling-related harm. The proposal does not specify in the available reporting how funds would be divided among programs or how the state would assess their results.

That focus on prevention sits alongside the bill’s provisions for casinos and their staff. Casino licensees and employees are among the parties the measure identifies when they allow an underage person to gamble, so the proposed civil framework would continue to make operator conduct relevant.

New Jersey regulators have separately taken action against licensed operators over responsible-gambling requirements. The state’s fine against Caesars Sportsbook provides context for the regulator’s use of enforcement in the market, but it is distinct from S597 and does not establish what penalties would apply under the bill.

Sen. Vince Polistina, a Republican from Atlantic County and one of the bill’s sponsors, has argued that casinos must follow the law while describing a criminal offense as too severe a consequence for a young person who makes a mistake. He also cited young people’s exposure to gambling through sports broadcasts, advertising and social media as a reason to reconsider how violations are handled.

Polistina’s stated position combines continued enforcement with a different form of penalty. Under the proposal, repeat offenses would carry higher civil fines, and collected revenue would go toward gambling prevention, education and treatment rather than leaving the matter solely within the criminal system.

Sen. James Beach, a Democratic sponsor, has emphasized the risk of gambling problems among young people and the possibility that criminal punishment could leave them with records. He has argued for focusing on behavioral changes and prevention instead.

Those positions frame the debate around how to respond when underage gambling occurs: the bill’s supporters say young people should remain accountable, but that a civil penalty linked to prevention and treatment is a more constructive response than a criminal offense. S597 would apply that approach to the covered gamblers, responsible adults and casino personnel specified in the proposal.

The measure now goes to the Senate Budget and Appropriations Committee after advancing from the Senate Judiciary Committee. Its progress is not final approval by the Senate, and the proposal has not become law.

The bill mirrors a measure that was debated and received preliminary approval in the previous year but was ultimately not enacted, according to the reporting. That earlier effort did not result in the proposed change taking effect; S597 must still complete the legislative process.

As reported by the Press of Atlantic City and PlayUSA, the bill must pass the relevant committees in both the Senate and Assembly, win approval from the full membership of both chambers, and then go to the governor for action. Until those steps are completed, New Jersey’s existing criminal-penalty framework remains in place.

Why do we "fall back" in November? We answer your questions.

Daylight saving time ends in less than two weeks, with the clocks officially changing at 2 a.m. While it’ll still be light enough for trick-or-treating on Halloween, the time change will hit the day after, when most of the US adjusts back one hour.

The time shift is notorious for disrupting sleep patterns and schedules. Some politicians are advocating for the abolition of time changes and the permanent adoption of daylight saving time, or DST. The House passed the Sunshine Protection Act in July, but it still needs Senate approval and the president’s signature to become law — meaning that, for now, we still have dark winter hours coming.

If you, too, dislike how early it gets dark each day through winter, just remember that brighter evenings will return in mid-March.

Daylight saving time in the US ends at 2 a.m. local time Sunday, Nov. 1. It will return at 2 a.m. local time on Sunday, March 14, 2027. The end of DST has the motto “fall back,” whereas in March, we “spring forward.”

The US isn’t alone in observing daylight saving time. Check out this list of other countries that use DST, along with their start and end dates.

The official US time website shows where time zone boundaries are located.

The exact start dates for daylight saving time and standard time float a little. DST kicks off on the second Sunday of March and ends on the first Sunday of November with the return of standard time.

We spend about eight months of the year in DST. We can thank the Uniform Time Act of 1966 for bringing some order to what had been a complicated history of time changes in the US.

“Motivated by transportation improvements, this act mandated standard time within the existing time zones and established a permanent system of uniform DST, including the dates and times for twice-yearly transitions,” the Bureau of Transportation Statistics states in a history of time zones.

The country’s time zones date to the railroad boom of the late 1800s. DST formally entered the chat in 1918, but its application was inconsistent until 1966. The country tried year-round daylight saving time in January 1974 under President Richard Nixon to address the energy crisis. It didn’t go over well. Congress and President Gerald Ford restored standard time in October 1974.

If you truly hate time changes, then consider moving to Arizona (except for the Navajo Nation) or Hawaii. Those states are on standard time all year long, and their residents don’t have to deal with the physical and mental health impacts of the change. Puerto Rico, Guam, the Virgin Islands, American Samoa and the Northern Mariana Islands also skip DST.

Read more: Why Is NASA Working on a Time Zone for the Moon?

Organizations such as the National Sleep Foundation, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Society for Research on Biological Rhythms are united in calling for permanent standard time, saying it’s better for human biology.

“There’s a mismatch between the outside world and our internal clocks during daylight saving time that can result in some negative health consequences,” says Joseph Dzierzewski, senior vice president of research and scientific affairs at the National Sleep Foundation.

Dzierzewski cites mental health concerns and upticks in cardiovascular events and drowsy driving. He also calls out safety issues with young children walking to school or sitting at bus stops in the dark. Those safety issues were a big reason why permanent DST didn’t last in 1974.

Time changes aren’t popular. An October 2024 YouGov poll (PDF) of more than 1,100 US adults found that 63% would like to see the changing of the clocks eliminated. Only 17% wanted to keep time changes, and 20% weren’t sure.

Efforts to end the time changes have so far failed to pass into law. The bipartisan Sunshine Protection Act, which would have made daylight saving time permanent, passed the Senate in 2022 — the new version of the bill passed the House earlier this year and was sent on to the Senate to review in July.

While many people agree on eliminating time changes, the schism between daylight saving time and standard time remains. Standard time may have a bit of an image issue compared to DST.

“Part of the issue is that people associate daylight saving time with summer. People love summer, right?” Dzierzewski said. “But the simple fact of the matter is, it would still be summer if we were on permanent standard time.”

President Donald Trump criticized time changes in a 2024 Truth Social post: “The Republican Party will use its best efforts to eliminate Daylight Saving Time, which has a small but strong constituency, but shouldn’t! Daylight Saving Time is inconvenient and very costly to our Nation.”

Bills and resolutions at the state level haven’t had real-world impacts yet. Politicians are still split on whether they want permanent daylight saving or standard time. Dzierzewski is hopeful that permanent standard time will win out.

“This is the first time in several years where I’ve seen more legislation introduced at a state level in support of permanent standard time as the solution,” he said.

If you’d like to see time changes end and want to advocate for either daylight saving or standard time, contact your state and national representatives and express your opinion.

Daylight saving time is happening, so now it’s a matter of figuring out how to deal with it. It’s not just about the day of the time change.

“You’re in a better situation to cope with the effects of the clock change if you have good sleep health generally,” Dzierzewski said. He recommends exposure to bright light in the morning, physical activity during the day, eating meals at regular times and practicing a relaxing wind-down routine at night.

The majority of Americans struggle to get the National Sleep Foundation’s recommended 7 to 9 hours of sleep for most adults. A CNET survey found that more than half of US adults use some method to cope with sleep-related challenges. Check out some expert-backed tips for improving your sleep quality.

“If you’re doing these healthy sleep behaviors, you’re probably more resilient to some of these changes,” Dzierzewski said. “If you have poor sleep to begin with, you’re probably going to feel it a little bit more.”

Establishing a better sleep routine can take time. There are two things you can do in direct response to the time change. Dzierzewski suggests adjusting your bedtime and wake-up time gradually ahead of the change. For example, you could shift your schedule by 15 minutes per day and ease yourself into DST. He also recommends getting a good dose of bright morning light on the day of the time change to help set your internal body clock.

Think of DST as another kind of wake-up call, one that can motivate you to evaluate your sleep routine and quality and make positive changes. Start with these six simple habits. And don’t forget to change your clocks. Do it the night before, and you won’t have to worry about it later.

#72. Docker and CNCF partner on an open spec for agent permissions

Secure, minimal container images for every team, free with enterprise features, if needed.

Docker and CNCF: Making what an agent may do as portable as the agent itself

Ten years ago, the software industry faced a choice. Every vendor could ship its own image format and its own runtime, and developers would have to pick a side. Or the industry could agree on one artifact. The second option won. Docker donated its image format and the Runc runtime to the Linux Foundation, and the Open Container Initiative (OCI) formed around them. Today, a Docker image built anywhere can run anywhere. The format is the backbone of the cloud native ecosystem and a de facto standard.

Now, we see a similar problem forming around agents. There is no shared format for what an agent is allowed to do. We are proposing the same kind of answer: one artifact, built on OCI, governed in the open.

Containers were built for immutable software. The image is the application. If you want to change it, you rebuild it, and it behaves the same way everywhere. That’s why a container image describes how software is built and says nothing about what it may do once it runs. For a web service, that was fine. It got a network and a port, and that was enough.

Agents are mutable by definition. Claude Code and Codex install packages, call APIs, and use credentials on your behalf. They change the environment they run in, and they decide what to do next. So, every team writes its own rules for what an agent may reach: a network rule here, a token there, a volume mount to get a task done. Those rules live in shell history, in dashboards, and in someone’s memory. A few months in, nobody can answer a simple question: what is this agent allowed to do?

Every team answers that on its own today, and every runtime vendor could ship its own way of answering it. That’s the kind of fragmentation OCI was created to prevent.

Kits are not new. Kits have been part of Docker Sandboxes as the way you package an agent, its tools, and what it may reach into something a team can share. What’s new is the artifact. A Kit is now an ordinary OCI image, and the format that describes it is open.

Today at WeAreDevelopers, we announced the Docker Sandbox Kit Spec, open source under Apache 2.0. A Kit carries three things in one image: the agent, its tools, and a typed list of everything it asks to reach, such as hosts, credentials, and volumes. Because the list is part of the image, pinning the image pins the agent and its requests together.

A Kit is not a new artifact type and not a fork of any OCI specification. It uses an extension point OCI already defines. It builds, pushes, pulls, signs, and scans like any image you run today, because it is one.

Today, we’re bringing the spec to CNCF, under their neutral governance, just like we did when the image format went to OCI.

Adoption is free. Because a Kit is an OCI image, every registry, scanner, and signing tool you already run handles it. There is nothing new to deploy.

The answer travels with the agent. Because the requests are in the image, “what may this agent do” has one answer. A teammate can pull it. A reviewer can diff it. A conforming runtime can enforce it. When a new version asks for more, the change shows up as added lines someone can refuse.

Docker has always had an ecosystem-first mindset. The Dockerfile mattered because anyone could write one, any registry could store the result, and any runtime could run it. Kits follow the same approach, and we did not build them alone.

We have worked with AWS, Box, Datadog, Dynatrace, JFrog, NanoClaw, OpenClaw, Palo Alto Networks, Snyk, and more to build Kits for their tools. Cloud platforms, observability, security, artifact management, content, and agent frameworks are all represented. The Kits we unveiled during the opening keynote at WeAreDevelopers today are the product of all that work, and they are the first of many.

MCP gave agents a standard way to talk to a tool. Kits give the ecosystem a standard way to publish the whole arrangement: the agent, its tools, and what it asks to reach, in one image anyone can pull. That’s what turns a format into a supply chain. For example, a database vendor can publish a Kit that connects any agent to its service with the scope it recommends.An agent maker publishes its own Kit, so the request list comes from the people who built the agent. A platform team publishes one for the company, and every engineer starts from the same place.

None of that happens if the format belongs to one vendor. A standard for deciding what an agent may do is worth a good deal less if it belongs to whoever sells you the runtime. Docker Sandboxes is the first runtime that enforces it. It should not be the only one, and under CNCF governance, it will not be.

Containers made software portable. Kits make authority portable: the set of things you deliberately hand over to an agent travels with the agent, in the same image, and means the same thing wherever a conforming runtime opens it.

If you make a tool agents use, publish a Kit for it. If you run agents, start from one and share it with your team. The specification, the capability pages, and a worked tour of a real Kit are at docker/sandbox-kit-spec. If there is a Kit you cannot express, or a rule a runtime cannot implement, open an issue. Every Kit published and every issue filed is how a standard gets built.

The CFTC is proposing clearer rules for event contracts as New Jersey asks the Supreme Court to review whether it can enforce gambling laws against Kalshi’s sports markets. Neither step settles the dispute over how federal oversight intersects with state authority.

Announced on Oct. 9, the CFTC’s proposed amendment would classify contracts tied to sports, politics, culture and weather as swaps. The agency says their exchange-based structure differs from ordinary betting: buyers and sellers set prices through orders, while exchanges collect trading fees rather than making money when a customer loses.

Chairman Michael S. Selig said, “The Commodity Exchange Act gives his agency sole regulatory authority over event contracts.” That is the CFTC’s position, not a court’s final ruling on the reach of state gambling laws. The proposal remains open to public comment and has not been finalized.

A separate interim final rule puts the agency’s longstanding exclusion for qualifying casino games and sportsbook wagers into its regulations. The exclusion applies when an operator is authorized by a state or tribal government and the wager is governed by the relevant gambling laws. It does not extend to transactions negotiated or carried out among participants on exchanges or other markets.

The distinction between event contracts and conventional wagers is central to the CFTC’s approach. Its proposed framework for event contracts is still under consideration, while the gambling exclusion takes effect when it is published in the Federal Register.

Kalshi can continue offering sports contracts in New Jersey under a preliminary injunction. On April 6, the Third Circuit upheld that protection, finding Kalshi had a reasonable chance of showing that federal law overrides the state’s restrictions. The court did not issue a final judgment in the lawsuit.

New Jersey filed its Supreme Court petition on Sept. 2. It asks whether federal commodities law prevents states from regulating sports bets offered on markets registered with the CFTC. The justices have not agreed to hear the case, and filing the petition does not lift the injunction or require Kalshi to stop offering its contracts in the state.

What baffles me is that container queries aren’t a new feature, as it currently sits at around 94% browser support. And yet, very few people are actually using it. The bottom line is that incorrect use comes down to the same impression I had when learning about them: they absolutely look just like media queries at first glance. And since they look similar, it’s easy to assume they serve similar purposes and work the same way.

Note: I should state up front that what I’m focusing on in this article is using container size queries, i.e., a responsive design technique for responding to the size of a particular container. The viewport is a proxy. It always has been. Media queries are what gave us the illusion that screen width alone is responsible for how responsive apps adapt to their environment.

Ask yourself this: When you write @media, what are you asking the browser?

I, like most developers, am asking the browser: How wide is the screen right now? That’s it.

Media queries answer that beautifully, but what happens when this .card component is placed in a grid cell that’s 300px wide on a 1920px desktop screen?

The media query doesn’t care; it does its job. The viewport is still 1920px, so min-width: 1024px fires and the matched query styles are applied, even though the card only has 300px of space to work with. Eventually, everything in the card deforms, overflows, or cramps up.

It’s common to think of responsive design purely as a system for updating complete page layouts, like going from two columns on a large screen to a single column on a small screen.

Container queries are smarter than that. They make responsive layouts more reliant on what’s happening inside a component rather than on the outer context that has no insight into a component’s contents. It is more like: “How much space is available for me in this specific spot, right now?”

A very interesting way to distinguish @media and @container queries is the layout type.

Media queries are for the “macro” layout; they look outward. Stuff like page structure, the header that spans the window, footers, main grid layout, system preferences (prefers-color-scheme), device capabilities (touch screens). You know, anything that is majorly true to the entire page structure.

Container queries, I’d say, are for “micro” layouts, i.e., most things that live inside the “macro” layout. We use them when any content needs to responsively fit whatever space it is allocated. Components that come to mind are things like cards, widgets, forms, navigation, and so on.

In other words, think “page layout” vs. “component layout”.

An element shouldn’t magically become “tablet-sized” just because the width exceeds an abstract width threshold like 768px. Instead, it should switch layout when it has enough space to do so, whether that happens on a mobile viewport or inside a desktop sidebar.

In case you’re still not convinced, did you know there are over 2,300 unique viewport sizes on the modern web? Do you think it is possible to account for all of them?

I’m not hating on media queries. It’s that in this era of responsiveness and component re-use, layout logic is closer to the container than the viewport. When we think in terms of containers and components, we’re effectively relying on the content to determine layout, not the viewport.

Responsive typography is a good example of something many of us have relied on media queries for. The fact that media queries come with their own CSS units — e.g., vw, vh, and so on — that are relative to the viewport size makes media queries look really good for adjusting font size based on the user’s screen size.

This works until that same component is moved into a different context, like a sidebar, where the viewport is completely irrelevant. Now, because we tied the responsiveness to the wrong reference point, the scaled typography can get too big or too small.

It’s not bulletproof, but it is also something media queries simply cannot do because they only observe the external browser window and are structurally blind to internal layout events like when, for example, flex items wrap onto a new line. #78. Announcing DPoP Support for Google OAuth: Securing Refresh Tokens

Traditional OAuth bearer tokens operate on a "finders-keepers" model, making them vulnerable to Infostealer malware (e.g. RedLine, NodeStealer) and Adversary-in-the-Middle (AiTM) attacks.

DPoP (Demonstrating Proof-of-Possession) is an application-layer OAuth 2.0 security profile (RFC 9449) that neutralizes token theft and replay attacks by cryptographically binding tokens to a client-generated asymmetric keypair at the application layer, ensuring that even if a token is stolen, it cannot be replayed without the corresponding private key.

This article is meant to inform you on where and how to adopt DPoP and describes Google's specific DPoP implementation. A DPoP adoption SKILL.md is also available to help implement DPoP.

Some optional features and implementation decisions in RFC 9449 are delegated to Google as an authorization provider. Google Identity, and its OAuth Platform, function as a developer-facing service where engineers implement OAuth 2.0 and cryptographic security controls like DPoP to protect API integrations. Separately, Google Workspace Context-Aware Access (CAA) leverages DPoP to secure enterprise AI workloads on the Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform by cryptographically validating agent identities at the Agent Gateway.

Google's OAuth Platform supports DPoP binding specifically for refresh tokens. This design choice reflects a pragmatic engineering tradeoff: by sender-constraining the long-lived refresh token, we neutralize the primary vector for persistent offline impersonation. Meanwhile, short-lived access tokens remain standard Bearer tokens, ensuring that high-QPS resource APIs do not incur the per-request asymmetric cryptographic verification overhead of DPoP. This approach delivers robust protection for your most sensitive credentials while maintaining the high performance and low latency of Google API integrations.

Unlike standard Bearer token flows, DPoP requires client-side cryptographic key generation, strict timestamp and nonce handling, specific claim computation (htm, htu, iat, jti), exact header construction, and DPoP proof JWT construction. These changes may introduce additional system constraints to support secure computation, handling, and storage.

The IETF’s Best Current Practice for OAuth 2.0 Security (RFC 9700) discusses sender-constrained refresh tokens. Additionally, the OAuth 2.0 for Browser-Based Applications IETF draft discusses three models: Backend-for-Frontend (BFF), Token-Mediating Backend, and Browser-based OAuth Client.

Following this guidance, Google recommends using DPoP to sender-contrain refresh tokens in these models.

Unlike transport-layer token binding (e.g., mTLS/RFC 8705) which often breaks or terminates at modern cloud proxies and load balancers, DPoP operates at the HTTP header level. This makes it highly resilient for complex, multi-hop environments, including emerging Model Context Protocol (MCP) and autonomous agent architectures.

A common question from security architects is: If DPoP is designed to sender-constrain tokens, why are Google's access tokens still standard Bearer tokens?

The answer lies in operational scaling and performance at Google's scale. Verifying asymmetric cryptographic signatures (such as ES256) requires significant CPU cycles. If every API call to high-throughput Google resource servers (like Gmail, Calendar, or Drive APIs) required verifying a DPoP proof, the compute overhead at the API Gateway layer would be massive, introducing measurable latency and scaling costs.

By constraining DPoP binding strictly to the refresh token, Google's architecture achieves a balance:

Long-Lived Credentials Protected: The highly sensitive, long-lived refresh token (which can be used to obtain new access tokens indefinitely) is cryptographically bound to the client's key pair. If exfiltrated, it is useless to an attacker.

High-Performance Serving: Short-lived access tokens (typically valid for 1 hour or less) remain standard Bearer tokens. This ensures that the vast majority of API requests incur zero cryptographic verification overhead, maintaining the high performance and low latency developers expect from Google APIs.

DPoP is a powerful tool for securing OAuth 2.0 tokens, but it is important to understand its boundaries.

As autonomous AI agents and Model Context Protocol (MCP) architectures emerge, securing tool-use tokens becomes critical. While DPoP is highly resilient in single-hop client-to-server connections, multi-agent workflows (where Agent A delegates a task to Agent B, which calls Resource C) introduce delegation challenges.

In a multi-agent chain, Agent B cannot sign a DPoP proof on behalf of Agent A without access to Agent A's private key—and sharing private keys violates the core security principles of DPoP. Because Google's core consumer OAuth endpoint (oauth2.googleapis.com) does not support OAuth 2.0 Token Exchange (RFC 8693), developers building multi-agent or federated cloud workloads should consider Google Cloud Secure Token Service (STS). By routing delegation through STS, the intermediate agent can exchange the upstream token for a new, short-lived federated token cryptographically bound to its own local key pair, preserving the sender-constrained security model across the entire agent chain without key exposure.

That said, my Eero Pro router also tops out at Wi-Fi 5, and it’s likely the same situation in plenty of other households. I encountered zero issues in terms of connection reliability, stability, or performance.

The Roku operating system continues to offer the most intuitive and novice-friendly TV viewing experience, marked by its colorful, oversize app icons and simple menus. There’s a bit more UI customization available now than in Rokus past, like the option to display icons in four columns instead of three. And there’s a sense of whimsy here not found elsewhere, including the lively “Roku City” screensaver that appears when the TV is idle. You can also opt for Roku Backdrops, an art-themed screensaver that does a solid Samsung The Frame impression.

Ads do crop up in various places—nearly a third of the home screen, a banner at the bottom of the menu screen, etc.—but they’re mostly streaming-related, not obnoxiously product-oriented like in Amazon’s and Google’s UIs.

One small gripe: There are still some streaming apps—HBO Max, Paramount+, etc.—that don’t let you sign into your account via app or QR code, even though their Google TV counterparts do. So be prepared for the occasional hassle of having to manually navigate onscreen keyboards with the remote.

Thankfully, Roku’s remote still rocks, with a cheerful purple navigation pad, dedicated play/pause and shuttle buttons, a programmable shortcut button, and side-mounted volume and mute controls. It’s more comfortable and intuitive than most remotes and is backlit. Unfortunately, it’s not rechargeable, an annoying oversight for a “Pro Series” TV. And its push-to-talk voice controls are fine for the likes of basic searches and playback functions but are constrained to Roku’s UI; you don’t get the added “smarts” afforded by Alexa+ and Google Assistant. (The exception: Roku’s branded smart-home gear, such as security cameras and light bulbs, can be controlled via voice commands.)

At least if the remote goes missing in the couch cushions (or an entirely different room, which is where I tried it), a handy remote-finder button on the right side of the TV will trigger “Here I am!” beeps.

To test the Roku Pro Series, I watched a wide range of sports, movies, and TV shows and fired up my Xbox Series X. As with other budget OLEDs, the colors impress; the brightness, not so much.

I started by streaming Solo: A Star Wars Story on Disney+. The first five minutes take place in the dark (literally and figuratively) underbelly of Corellia, and I’ve found that many TVs struggle here with contrast and brightness; several of these early scenes can look muddy or just plain dim. Roku’s OLED fared well on the contrast front, but I found myself wishing for a bit more visibility when Han lays out his plan to Kira and, later, confronts Lady Proxima. Even watching in a relatively dark room, those moments revealed suboptimal brightness.

Next up, the white-hallway scene in Mythic Quest, season 3, episode 1—a great measure of both contrast and black levels. Ian Grimm’s shirt appeared dark as night and juxtaposed perfectly against the smooth white walls. And within those walls, different shades appeared in different areas as the characters walked around; the TV expertly contrasted all of them. Did I find myself wishing for slightly whiter whites? Yes. Did I enjoy the scene any less? No.

While I had the Apple TV app running, I tuned into some live MLB soccer. The action: sharp and smooth (and dull, obviously, because soccer), and the same was true when I switched over to an NFL game on DirecTV. That’s as expected with a 120-Hz refresh rate.

Speaking of which, that rate, coupled with low-latency game mode (which kicked on automatically) served my Xbox experiences well. From the 1930s palette of the cartoonish Cuphead to the candy-colored arcade racing of Burnout: Paradise, everything looked crisp and vivid, with fast response times and zero blur.

For my color investigations, I clicked into Planet Earth II on HBO Max, Pixar’s Coco on Disney+, various “8K” nature videos on YouTube, and an Inglorious Basterds 4K Blu-ray played on the Xbox. Across the board, the Roku Pro Series produced vibrant, richly detailed color—but with less of the “pop” I’ve witnessed on high-end LG and Sony OLEDs. That’s partially due to the panel’s limited brightness.

Whether that’s a bad thing is open for debate; some would argue that higher-end OLEDs oversaturate, the shiny colors more of a distraction than an enhancement. Discuss.

A notable Roku innovation is Smart Picture Max, which automatically detects the type of content you’re watching and adjusts various display settings accordingly. Though it’s hard to gauge the impact of this on things like color and brightness, there’s one clearly quantifiable benefit: motion-smoothing. Smart Picture Max disables it for most TV shows and movies, meaning you don’t get the dreaded soap-opera effect. That alone gives the Pro Series a leg up over most OLEDs, which require manual tinkering to disable this heinous feature (and again to re-enable it for sports and gaming, the only situations in which it’s not heinous).

The one catch here is that any Dolby Vision content overrides Smart Picture Max, leaving motion-smoothing switched on. Fortunately, Roku makes it refreshingly easy to modify that and other picture settings; just press the star button on the remote. I found (and toggled off) smoothing with just a few clicks. Another nice perk: Any change you make applies instantly to whatever you’re viewing, so you can immediately gauge whether it’s what you want; you don’t have to exit the menu to see the results.

On the audio front, the presence of Dolby Atmos does nothing to improve the TV’s speakers, which produce plenty of volume but get harsh and shrill the higher you go. Indeed, the opening measures of Nick Perito’s “The Green Leaves of Summer” in Inglorious Basterds were deeply unpleasant, even at a modest volume. This came as something of a surprise, as Roku’s previous Pro Series TV sounded much better overall. The speakers here are tolerable at low volumes in a small room; for everything else, you’re going to want a soundbar.

In the end, Roku’s first OLED won’t win over any die-hard fans of premium panels; it’s a budget model through and through, probably on par with LG’s similarly affordable B6. But it’s still an OLED, with all the vivid color, pitch-black black, and strong contrast that technology affords. And the Roku remote, interface, and automated image adjustments contribute to a splendid user experience. Watch for the next sale (Black Friday, anyone?) and you’ll feel like you’re getting a steal.
