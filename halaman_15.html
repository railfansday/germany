#335. Scientists win 2026 Nobel Prize for controlling brain cells with light

Scientists who worked out how to control brain cells with light have won this year’s Nobel prize in physiology or medicine. Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel were honored for their research into “light-gated ion channels and optogenetics.”

Peter Hegemann, at Humboldt University of Berlin, and Georg Nagel, at the University of Würzburg, wanted to understand how a tiny, single-celled alga called Chlamydomonas senses light. Light-sensing is not unique to animals with eyes: organisms throughout the living world use light to detect their surroundings and respond to changes in their environment. Even single-cell organisms have proteins that respond to light. These proteins are used to move towards or away from light, to generate energy and to respond to the various demands of a 24-hour day.

Hegemann and Nagel discovered that Chlamydomonas has a beautifully ingenious mechanism of light detection that relies on a single protein called channelrhodopsin.

Channelrhodopsin is built into the outer membrane of the algal cell. Part of the protein faces the water outside the cell and part faces the inside. The protein also contains a small molecule called retinaldehyde, which is able to absorb light.

When light hits the retinaldehyde inside channelrhodopsin, it changes shape. This makes the channelrhodopsin protein change shape too. That change opens a tiny passage through the cell membrane. Positively charged particles, including sodium, can then flow from the water outside the alga into the cell. This movement of charged particles sends a signal inside the alga that helps it swim.

How fantastic to have solved such an interesting biological question, but why the Nobel prize in physiology or medicine? The answer lies in the way nerve cells work. Nerve cells use the movement of charged particles (called “ions”) across their membranes to control their activity. When positively charged particles flow into a nerve cell, they can make it more active.

The researchers realized that if they could put channelrhodopsin into nerve cells, they might be able to switch those cells on simply by shining a light on them. That is exactly what Hegemann, Nagel and the third Nobel-prize winner, Karl Deisseroth, a psychiatrist and bioengineer at Stanford University in California, went on to do.

The ability to control nerve cells with light is called optogenetics. It has become a powerful research tool because scientists can use it to switch specific groups of nerve cells on and off and see what happens.

First, scientists deliver the gene for channelrhodopsin to the nerve cells they want to study. The gene is usually carried into the cells by a virus that has been altered so that it cannot cause disease. The cells then make the channelrhodopsin protein, which makes them respond to light.

In animals such as mice, scientists can shine light into the brain through a thin optical fibre. When the light reaches the cells, channelrhodopsin opens and the cells become active. Nearby nerve cells that do not contain the protein are unaffected.

Light can be aimed at a very small part of the brain and switched on and off extremely quickly. This makes optogenetics a powerful way to investigate how the brain works. For example, if scientists want to know whether a particular group of nerve cells is involved in memory, sleep, sensation or movement, say, they can switch those cells on with light and see what happens.

In one experiment in 2012, a team at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology gave mice a mild electric shock in a particular cage. They used optogenetics to tag the nerve cells in the brain’s memory center that were active during that the fear-conditioning experiment. Days later, in a different cage, they switched those cells on with light. The mice froze in fear, as if they remembered the shock.

Mice that were not shocked did not freeze, which showed that the light was reactivating a specific memory. Deisseroth was one of the study’s authors.

Optogenetics has transformed scientists’ ability to investigate how the brain and nervous system work. It may also have a future as a treatment. Optogenetics is already in early-stage clinical trials for people with retinal degeneration. In these trials, researchers introduce light-sensitive proteins into nerve cells that remain in the retina after the cells that normally detect light – called rods and cones – have been lost. This can make surviving cells respond to light and restore some vision.

Researchers are also investigating whether optogenetics could eventually be used to treat neurological conditions, including Parkinson’s and epilepsy.

This year’s Nobel prize celebrates work that started with a question that may sound esoteric: how do Chlamydomonas swim toward light? This question has no obvious practical importance. It doesn’t address an urgent need in human health, nor is it a clear route to riches. The success of optogenetics is an illustration of the importance of continuing to ask questions simply to understand the world around us because it’s impossible to predict where knowledge will take us.

Teams adopting LLMs for documentation are running into a predictable failure mode: AI-generated documentation that looks polished but collapses under actual use. The docs compile, the formatting is clean, the headings are logical, and yet nobody can follow the procedures they describe. Worse, when AI-generated docs become the input for further AI-generated docs, the result is a feedback loop of degrading quality that no amount of manual review can sustainably catch. This playbook provides a CI-enforced system, combining a doc skeleton, PR checklist, Vale prose linting, and structural shell checks, to reject unreadable documentation before it merges.

The pattern is now familiar enough that it surfaces regularly in engineering communities. Engineers describe the same frustration in community discussions: teams use LLMs to draft documentation, those docs get committed, and then other LLMs ingest them as context for generating further docs. Each generation drops decision rationale first, then environmental assumptions, leaving only bare commands wrapped in syntactically valid but semantically hollow instructions.

The symptoms are concrete and recognizable. You find giant CLI commands with unexplained flags that no reviewer questions because they "look right." Terraform blocks stretch 200 lines, dropped in without a single annotation explaining why a particular value was chosen. Nobody writes any human context around YAML walls. Verification steps that would confirm whether the procedure actually works? Missing entirely. The documentation exists, technically, but it serves no reader.

This is not a theoretical concern. It is the default outcome when AI generates documentation without structural constraints. The text is fluent. The Markdown renders correctly. The content is wrong, or at minimum, unverifiable.

Reviewers give documentation PRs less scrutiny than code PRs. They skim them. AI-generated text is particularly dangerous here because it reads as complete and professional. Clean formatting, complete sentences, and plausible-sounding steps are enough to pass a 30-second skim, and no one is running the commands or verifying the steps during the review process.

Without structural expectations, there is no objective standard against which to reject a doc PR. A reviewer might sense that something is off but lacks a concrete rule to point at. The cost of this gap surfaces downstream: onboarding engineers who cannot follow a setup guide, incident responders who discover runbooks are wrong under pressure, and knowledge transfer failures that compound over quarters.

The core mechanism for enforcing readability is a four-part skeleton that every documentation file must follow. Each section exists to surface a specific failure mode.

One to three sentences stating what the document enables the reader to do and when they would need it. The limit is deliberate. Forcing authors, whether human or AI, to articulate a document's purpose in three sentences or fewer exposes docs that exist for no clear reason. If the purpose cannot be stated concisely, the document conflates multiple concerns and should be split.

A plain-language paragraph explaining the approach and key decisions, with no code, no jargon, and no implementation detail. AI-generated docs almost universally skip this section. LLMs jump straight to implementation because their training data is dominated by implementation-heavy content. The human summary forces a different register: it demands that the author (or the prompt engineer directing the AI) explain the reasoning before the recipe.

Every CLI command, configuration block, or code snippet must include inline or adjacent annotations explaining non-obvious flags, values, and choices. The rule is strict: no command block longer than 10 lines without a preceding or interleaved explanation. Ten lines is roughly one terminal screenful without scrolling; adjust to your team's threshold. Note that this 10-line rule is a convention enforced through review and the PR checklist; the shell script described later enforces a separate section-level line limit.

The difference between raw AI output and annotated commands is the difference between a reference dump and a usable guide. A raw Terraform block might set min_capacity = 2 without explaining that this value was chosen to survive a single availability zone failure. An annotated version makes that reasoning visible, turning an opaque configuration into a decision record.

An LLM can generate a plausible-looking procedure, but fabricating a verification step that actually passes requires the procedure to be correct. When authors are forced to specify what "done" looks like, hollow docs become obvious.

A PR template tailored to documentation makes expectations explicit for every contributor. It shifts the burden of proof from the reviewer to the author, and it provides a concrete checklist that both humans and AI-assisted workflows can follow.

Document this in your contributing guide.

The critical item is "I have personally run the verification steps." This is an honesty gate. It cannot be automated, but its presence in the template creates social pressure and an auditable expectation. If a doc merges with a broken verification section and this box is checked, the conversation is no longer about the doc; it is about the process violation.

Vale is a prose linter, not a spell-checker. It enforces style rules against Markdown content, catching problems like excessive sentence length, passive voice, weasel words, and jargon. You extend it with YAML-based rules, and it supports multiple style packages out of the box.

What Vale cannot do is check structural requirements. It will not verify that a document contains a "How to Verify" section, nor will it enforce section length limits. The shell script described in the next section handles those structural concerns. Vale and the shell script are complementary: Vale handles prose quality, the script handles document architecture.

The .vale.ini file scopes Vale's enforcement to the docs directory and specifies which style packages to apply. The write-good and proselint packages cover the most common AI-generated prose problems: passive constructions, hedge words, and unnecessary complexity. Package names are case-sensitive and must match the Vale package registry exactly.

Place this file at the repository root. The Packages directive declares which style sets to use. Run vale sync separately to download them before linting. The glob pattern docs/**/*.md restricts enforcement to Markdown files in the docs directory, avoiding false positives on READMEs or changelogs that follow different conventions.

After creating or updating .vale.ini, run vale sync once locally (the CI action in the workflow below runs it for you) to fetch the declared packages. You can verify available rules by running vale ls-config after syncing.

A common AI documentation failure is consecutive code blocks with no prose between them. This produces a wall of commands with no context. Three structural rules that Vale cannot cover: required headings exist (Purpose, Human Summary, Annotated Commands, How to Verify), no single section exceeds a configurable line-count threshold (default: 80 lines, an upper bound to prevent wall-of-text sections while accommodating sections that legitimately contain annotated code blocks), and at least one prose line exists between consecutive fenced code blocks.

The following script is a starting point. It has not been tested against every edge case and must be adapted to the specific repository structure and documentation conventions in use. Save this file as scripts/doc-structure-check.sh in the repository root and commit it with execute permissions (git update-index --chmod=+x scripts/doc-structure-check.sh).

When the script finds a violation, it prints a human-readable error message that tells the author exactly what is missing. "Missing required heading: 'How to Verify'" is actionable; a generic "lint failed" is not.

The comment provides an escape hatch for genuinely unusual documents. The script only recognizes the skip directive when it appears as a bare HTML comment line at the start of the file, not when it is embedded inside a fenced code block or elsewhere in the document. This is a social convention, not an automated enforcement: the skip directive should be visible in PR diffs so that reviewers can question its use. Consider adding a CODEOWNERS rule for files containing the directive so that skips require explicit approval. Having an override does not eliminate accountability; it channels it.

The workflow triggers on PRs that modify files in the docs directory. The Vale action installs Vale, syncs packages, and runs prose linting; a separate step then runs the structural shell script. If either step fails, CI posts actionable annotations on the PR.

Note: before adding the shell script to CI, validate it against your documentation corpus and address the edge cases noted above. The fail_on_error: true flag on the Vale step blocks the merge only if your repository has branch protection rules configured to require the doc-lint check to pass.

The paths filter ensures the workflow only runs when documentation files change, avoiding unnecessary CI costs on code-only PRs. The fail_on_error: true flag on the Vale step ensures that prose violations block the merge, not just warn. The Vale action is pinned to a specific commit SHA rather than a floating tag to prevent supply-chain compromises or breaking changes from silently affecting CI. The SHA above is the v2.1.1 release of vale-cli/vale-action; check that page for newer releases and update it deliberately. The action installs Vale and runs vale sync itself, so the workflow has no separate install or sync step.

Not every document fits the four-section skeleton. Incident postmortems, architecture decision records (ADRs), and repository READMEs have different structures and serve different purposes. The skeleton is designed for procedural and operational documentation, not for every Markdown file in a repository. The skip-doc-lint directive exists for these cases, but require a comment on every override explaining why the standard structure does not apply. Making exceptions visible prevents the escape hatch from becoming the default path.

The verification section should be treated the same way engineers treat tests. If a team would not merge code without passing tests, it should not merge documentation without verified procedures.

Encouraging reviewers to actually run the verification steps, rather than just reading them, closes the gap between "this looks right" and "this works."

#338. Designing With Uncertainty: How AI Supercharges Probabilistic Thinking

Pratik Joglekar is a Senior Product Designer working with HubSpot. He loves to solve complex and challenging UX problems. When not working, he can be found at … More about Pratik ↬

In 2024, an Air Canada customer asked a chatbot about bereavement fares. The bot confidently gave him a refund policy that didn’t exist. The airline refused to honor it. A tribunal ruled in the customer’s favor. The bot hadn’t decided anything; it had predicted an answer based on patterns in its training data. The company treated that prediction as policy.

This is the risk at the heart of designing with AI today: probabilistic systems wrapped in deterministic interfaces. The AI offers a guess, the interface presents it as truth, and the user, or the organization, acts on it.

Humans are wired for deterministic thinking. We prefer to believe that past actions determine future outcomes. Flip a coin 999 times and get heads every time, the deterministic mind assumes the coin is rigged. The probabilistic mind accepts that the 1000th flip could still go either way. That second mindset is harder to hold onto, but it is exactly what designers need right now.

Products operate in complex, nonlinear environments, and AI is accelerating that complexity. When designers and product teams treat AI outputs as the answer rather than one of many possible answers, they build fragile experiences, and in some cases, like medical diagnostics or financial forecasting, genuinely dangerous ones.

This article is a practical guide to designing probabilistically with AI as a partner. It is about using AI to sharpen your thinking rather than outsource it, accounting for model bias, human sentiment, and perceived risk along the way.

Most questions we ask AI do not produce binary answers. They produce probabilities based on patterns in data. If you ask, “Do aliens exist?” the answer will be somewhere between plausible and uncertain. Scientists consider life elsewhere in the universe likely, but without any concrete evidence, we cannot confirm it. The answer doesn’t resolve the question; it frames it as a probability.

Designers should read AI outputs the same way. They are signals, not conclusions, possible outcomes that have to be interpreted within the context of product goals, user behavior, and business constraints.

Many digital products already work this way. Netflix doesn’t know you’ll enjoy Superstore because you watched The Office; it estimates the probability and surfaces the title accordingly. The interface is responding to a prediction.

Design decisions can follow the same logic. AI models can combine behavioral analytics with research insights to estimate the likelihood of certain outcomes, and those probabilities can act as a yardstick for design strategy. Consider a scenario where analytics suggest a 60% versus 90% confidence that users will complete a purchase. At 60%, the design has to do more persuasive work, testimonials, explanations, comparisons, and reassurance signals may help the user move toward a decision. At 90%, the user is already motivated, and the design should start removing friction so the action can happen quickly. Same screen, very different design problem.

AI can also simulate outcomes using historical data and behavioral models before you commit to a direction. The value of those simulations depends heavily on how prompts are structured, the context they define, the hypothesis being tested, user motivation, and the edge cases you want stressed.

I can think of one such practical use: evaluating early designs through structured prompts, especially when you don’t have direct access to the user group you’re designing for. The prompt below is a starting point for evaluating a design from the perspective of neurodivergent users as well. Treat it as a template, adapt the user group, criteria, and output format to your product, and use it as a conversation starter with your team rather than a verdict.

That said, simulations do not replace experimentation. Because models are trained on historical data, they reflect past behavior more strongly than they predict future change. Imagine designing a voice interface for elderly users who struggle with touchscreens. A model trained on mobile interaction data might predict low engagement, not because the idea lacks value, but because the dataset reflects different user behavior. Simulations should always surface assumptions, not prevent innovation.

AI systems are built on historical data, more specifically, on the datasets they are trained on. That foundation shapes the outputs we receive. During the AI Summit in France, India’s Prime Minister Narendra Modi shared an example that illustrates this well. If you ask an AI model to generate an image of a person writing with the left hand, the output may still show a person writing with their right hand. The reason is statistical: most people are right-handed, and the training data reflects that. This may have improved over time, but the point remains relevant. I still occasionally see this behavior when generating images with similar models.

What you receive is not truth. It is the most statistically likely outcome given the data available. Always ask whether past data meaningfully predicts future behavior. If additional context can improve the prediction, include it. Without context, the output is just one of many possible answers dressed up as the only one.

Confidence scores deserve the same scrutiny. Overtrusting a high-confidence output leads to the Air Canada situation. Dismissing a low-confidence one can cause teams to miss a real signal buried in noisy data. A prediction with 90% confidence is not necessarily correct, and a 40% signal is not necessarily useless. Designers must still weigh the possibilities, consider the case in front of them, and bring judgment to what the AI recommends.

Transparency is how you make that possible. As AI systems increasingly shape decisions, people need visibility into how outputs are generated, the sources, the reasoning, and the summaries behind a recommendation. Black-box systems breed distrust. Systems that reveal their reasoning let users evaluate outputs for themselves. That transparency is good design and ethical practice. It respects the trust people place in these tools.

Thinking in probabilities often means resisting the temptation of quick answers. AI can accelerate research and surface patterns faster than ever before, but those outputs are starting points, not final decisions.

Design shapes how a product is ultimately experienced — the decisions designers make determine whether the experience feels adequate, intuitive, or exceptional. And design is inherently full of assumptions and bets. Even the most rigorous research can yield multiple valid solutions to the same problem, each carrying a different probability of success.

Thinking probabilistically means recognizing that design decisions rarely produce binary outcomes. They lead to a range of possible results, and the role of the designer is to navigate those possibilities and identify the path most likely to create value. This mindset also builds adaptability: user needs evolve, strategies change, and sometimes ideas fail. Teams that lean on data signals, experimentation, and learning loops move faster toward the most effective solution.

Before the practical principles, one fundamental idea:

Design decisions should be optimized for likelihood, not certainty.

Every design decision is a bet, not a guarantee. Even when decisions are informed by research and data, they are still based on smaller samples and assumptions about how users will behave at scale. A well-researched idea can still fail in the real world.

The Air Canada chatbot from the introduction is a design lesson as much as a legal one. The bot was doing what language models do, predicting plausible text. The interface, however, communicated that prediction with complete confidence, no caveats, no “here’s what our policy usually says,” no obvious path to a human. The user read confidence as commitment, and legally, so did the tribunal.

This is what happens when probabilistic systems are wrapped in deterministic interfaces. The interface transforms likelihood into certainty, and that is where the risk emerges.

Designers should avoid binary thinking — a great idea does not mean guaranteed success, and a familiar idea is not guaranteed to fail. Examine variations, confidence levels, and edge cases instead. AI can certainly help here, acting as a portfolio-thinking engine that surfaces different interpretations, highlights risks, and generates structured recommendations. The goal is not to optimize for certainty, but for value: it should always be value-driven.

Think of the moment in Avengers: Infinity War when Doctor Strange tells Tony Stark that out of millions of possible futures, there is only one where they win. AI cannot tell you the future, but it can help you explore the possible paths. Instead of asking whether an idea will succeed, ask AI to estimate the likelihood and get a score, and use those signals to guide decisions.

Even an actual probability is not a final answer. Imagine an AI model predicts an 80% likelihood that users prefer a minimal checkout experience. That does not mean the solution is simply “build a minimal checkout.” Data should function as a compass, not a map.

These questions help designers validate predictions through usability testing and additional research. AI excels at identifying patterns, but it rarely explains why those patterns exist. Understanding motivation is still a human-centered research task.

The clearest cautionary tale here is Amazon’s experimental AI recruitment tool, which the company reportedly scrapped after discovering that the model had learned to downgrade resumes from women. The training data, roughly a decade of historical hiring decisions, was skewed toward male candidates, and the model inherited that skew. It began penalizing resumes that included the word “women’s,” as in “women’s chess club captain,” and favoring language more commonly found on men’s resumes. The system was not intentionally biased — the data was. Amazon reportedly tried to adjust it and eventually shut the project down because they could not guarantee it would not surface other discriminatory patterns.

Examples like this are why interpreting AI output critically matters. Designers need to understand the data behind a prediction and evaluate the reliability of the models they depend on. A recommendation is only as good as the data it was trained on, and the only way to know what that data is hiding is to ask.

Experimentation is usually framed as a way to validate a design decision. Want to lift the click-through rate of a CTA? Run an A/B test. Probabilistic thinking reframes this. Experiments should not only confirm solutions but also reduce uncertainty.

Traditional A/B testing is expensive. It costs engineering time, traffic allocation, and user exposure, especially when a losing variant runs against a significant chunk of your audience. AI simulations can help filter weaker ideas before they reach production by making experimentation more efficient. User needs shift constantly, and the most effective teams iterate fast.

AI can help evaluate assumptions early by modeling potential outcomes based on historical and behavioral data. These simulations act as a hypothesis filter, pointing to the directions worth investing engineering effort in. This also supports personalization — different users may respond better to different experiences. Version A may resonate with high-intent users while version B works better for exploratory ones. Multiple experiences living side by side are not a flaw; they can be an intentional strategy.

AI amplifies probabilistic thinking by surfacing scenarios, assigning likelihood scores, and enabling personalization at scale. Experimentation becomes a continuous feedback loop:

Predict → Test → Learn → Adjust → Repeat!

Use this template to define the hypothesis:

We believe [behavioral assumption] will impact [metric] because [reason]. We’ll know we are right when [evidence].

Example: We believe simplifying the onboarding flow from 5 steps to 3 will increase completion rate because users experience decision fatigue when too many choices are presented. We’ll know we’re right when we see at least a 15% increase in step-to-step conversion with no drop in activation rate.

One of the hardest things for designers is making uncertainty understandable and actionable. When uncertainty is hidden, users treat AI outputs as facts. When it’s communicated clearly, trust increases.

Ranges, estimates, and confidence indicators go a long way. A delivery window of “Friday to Monday” tells the truth about variability without misleading anyone, whereas a specific timestamp that slips erodes trust every time. A face recognition feature that says “this looks like Pratik, is that right?” sets more honest expectations than one that just labels the photo with a name.

Communicating uncertainty does not weaken trust — it strengthens it. The goal is not to eliminate uncertainty but to design for it intelligently.

Different users respond to uncertainty differently, and your design should account for that:

AI should augment human judgment, and certainly not replace it. The most trustworthy systems are designed with clear moments where people can review, challenge, correct, or override machine suggestions. Human-in-the-loop (HITL) is not a safety net — it is a refinement engine. Every override, correction, or rejection becomes high-quality feedback that improves the model over time.

Control is a prerequisite for adoption. Users are more willing to rely on AI when they understand how a suggestion was generated, can evaluate its implications, and can easily intervene. Well-designed products make this explicit: who is acting, what happens if the suggestion is wrong, and where the user can step in.

These interactions are also critical for system improvement. Every accept, reject, or edit is a strong signal, and compared to passive analytics, this kind of feedback produces far more meaningful training data. It closes the loop between real-world usage and model performance.

GitHub Copilot is a good everyday example. It offers inline code suggestions that developers can accept with a tab, edit, or ignore entirely. The system never commits code on the user’s behalf. Authorship stays with the humans. Every data point becomes implicit feedback about which suggestions were useful. Gmail’s Smart Compose works similarly, presenting predicted text as optional, keeping tone and intent in the user’s hands.

In higher-stakes contexts, HITL becomes more explicit. Risk and fraud systems typically use probability scores to route decisions: low-risk: proceed automatically; medium-risk: trigger additional verification; and high-risk: escalate to a human reviewer. This balances speed with judgment without removing oversight.

In safety-critical domains like healthcare, human oversight is non-negotiable. AI may flag anomalies or suggest a diagnosis, but the clinician retains final authority. Tools that explain the details help the practitioner understand why a recommendation was made, reinforcing confidence without removing accountability.

From a UX perspective, HITL is about matching the interaction pattern to the level of risk. Simple accept/reject affordances work well for low-risk suggestions that improve speed without real consequences. As the stakes climb, impacting data, money, or people, preview and approval steps become essential. Explanations help users calibrate trust rather than blindly accept outputs.

What happens behind the scenes matters just as much. The system should capture user decisions with context, feed them into learning workflows, and log overrides for auditability. Over time, teams can track signals like override rate, confidence accuracy, time-to-approval, and perceived trust. A high override rate is not a user failure. It is a signal that the design or the model needs attention.

Poorly implemented HITL systems can fail in subtle ways. Human review can devolve into a rubber stamp. Workflows can slow down so much that users route around the safeguards. Feedback can skew toward a narrow subset of users. These risks are real, but they are design problems, not reasons to remove HITL.

The goal is not to maximize human involvement. It is to focus it where uncertainty, impact, or ethics demand it. Keeping HITL is less about control and more about clarity: clarity about who decides, when uncertainty matters, and how responsibility is shared between people and machines.

Good design adapts as the landscape shifts. Product design, especially in AI-powered systems, can no longer afford to optimize only for short-term conversion metrics. User intent is fluid as well as ever-changing, environments change rapidly, and probabilistic systems continuously evolve too. What works today can quietly break tomorrow. Designing for resilience means building products that stay reliable, trustworthy, and useful even as assumptions, data, and user behaviors change.

Resilient design shifts the question from:

How do we maximize this metric right now?! → How does this system behave over time, under stress, and in uncertainty?

Do not just consider last quarter’s numbers. Peek into the following quarters to identify the shift and make changes accordingly.

Likelihoods shift constantly, AI models drift, contexts evolve, and user needs mature as well, so designing as if conditions are stable creates fragility in probabilistic environments. A resilient approach assumes volatility as the default.

Think about how recommendation systems tend to evolve. The early version of a content feed optimizes for engagement, and for a while, engagement goes up. Then users start to notice the feed feels narrow, repetitive, maybe even exhausting. Resilient systems rebalance, introducing novelty, diversifying signals, and pulling in long-term satisfaction measures alongside short-term clicks.

Short-term conversion gains often hide long-term costs. Speeding up onboarding can reduce comprehension. Maximizing notification CTR can erode trust. Optimizing engagement alone can produce unhealthy usage patterns. Fragile systems maximize numbers while ignoring second-order effects, the downstream consequences that show up weeks or months later.

Duolingo’s hearts system is a good example of designing against this. It introduces friction: if you make too many mistakes, you run out of hearts and have to wait or practice older material to earn more. On paper, that looks like a conversion killer: fewer lessons per session. In practice, the team has publicly discussed how it supports long-term motivation and retention, which is the metric that actually matters for a learning app. Short-term engagement dips, but long-term outcomes improve.

Meta has made a similar, if more reluctant, shift. The company publicly acknowledged that optimizing purely for “time spent” produced unintended emotional and societal effects, which led to a stated pivot toward “meaningful social interactions” as a guiding metric. Whether that shift fully landed is up for debate, but the acknowledgment itself is the point: optimizing for the wrong thing at scale has real downstream cost.

Teams routinely plan for traffic spikes, but rarely for uncertainty spikes. Yet AI systems degrade, adversarial behaviors evolve, and external shocks can reshape user behavior overnight. Resilient design assumes variability and prepares for it.

This means designing for degrading confidence. What does your interface do when the AI isn’t sure? Does it quietly fail, or does it gracefully hand off? Does the experience still make sense if AI assistance goes away entirely? A good fallback strategy is as important as the happy path.

If you take one thing from this article into your next design review, make it this:

That single reframe changes how you write hypotheses, interpret AI output, scope experiments, and design for the moments when the system is wrong. Starting this week, name the assumption behind every AI recommendation you accept, find one place in your product where a probabilistic output is presented as a certainty, fix the framing, and design the fallback before the happy path.

The shift from deterministic to probabilistic design is less about new tools and more about a new posture. AI has not introduced uncertainty into our world. It has simply made the uncertainty that was always there impossible to ignore. AI can estimate, simulate, and recommend, but it cannot decide what matters, which users are being overlooked, or which unconventional idea is worth defending against a model trained on yesterday’s data. Those remain human responsibilities. Think in ranges, not points. Test assumptions, not features. Build for adaptation, not perfection. In a world where prediction is cheap, and judgment is rare, the most valuable thing a designer can do is keep asking, What else might be true?

#339. Scientists discover why some people still can’t taste food after COVID-19

Scientists have uncovered changes inside taste buds that may help explain why some people struggle to taste food more than a year after recovering from COVID-19. The findings point to disruptions in the molecular signals responsible for detecting certain flavors, along with physical abnormalities in the structure of taste buds.

The study, published in Chemical Senses, offers the first direct biological evidence connecting persistent taste problems reported by patients to measurable changes within their taste cells.

Why Does Taste Loss Linger After COVID-19?

To investigate the lasting effects of COVID-19 on taste, scientists from the University of Colorado Anschutz and two Swedish universities examined 28 people who had experienced taste disturbances for more than a year following infection. None of the participants had been hospitalized for COVID-19.

Testing revealed that eight of the 28 participants had clearly abnormal taste scores. Eleven reported difficulty detecting specific flavors, particularly sweet, bitter, and umami, the savory taste associated with foods such as mushrooms and broth. In contrast, their ability to detect salty and sour flavors remained largely unaffected.

Seeking an explanation for this unusual pattern, the researchers collected small tissue samples from the taste buds of 20 participants and examined them for biological abnormalities.

Scientists Identify a Molecular Defect in Taste Cells

The research team, organized by Göran Hellekant, PhD, of the University of Wisconsin and the Swedish University of Agricultural Sciences, identified reduced amounts of messenger RNA (mRNA) involved in producing a protein known as PLCβ2.

This protein plays an essential role in how taste receptor cells communicate with the brain. Specifically, it amplifies the signals generated when the tongue detects sweet, bitter, and umami flavors. Without sufficient levels of the protein, those signals may become too weak to register normally.

"PLCβ2 acts like a molecular amplifier inside taste cells," said Thomas Finger, PhD, professor of cell and developmental biology at the University of Colorado Anschutz and corresponding author of the study. "It strengthens the signal before it's transmitted to the brain. When levels are reduced, the taste signal weakens."

The discovery may also explain why some flavors are affected more than others. Cells responsible for detecting salty and sour tastes use different signaling mechanisms that do not depend on PLCβ2, potentially allowing those sensations to remain intact even when other taste signals are disrupted.

Hidden Structural Changes Inside Taste Buds

The researchers also discovered that the abnormalities were not limited to molecular signaling. When they examined taste bud tissue under a microscope, they found that some participants had changes in the physical arrangement of their taste cells.

"Some subjects had normal-looking taste buds, while others showed structural disorganization," Finger said. "This suggests that both molecular and architectural changes may contribute to persistent taste dysfunction."

Together, these findings suggest that lingering taste problems after COVID-19 may involve multiple biological disruptions, affecting both the way taste cells are organized and how effectively they transmit information.

Why Can COVID-19 Taste Loss Last More Than a Year?

One of the most intriguing aspects of persistent taste loss is how long it can continue despite the body's natural ability to replace taste cells.

Under normal circumstances, cells in the taste buds are renewed approximately every two to four weeks. Yet the researchers found signs that the molecular processes needed for normal taste signaling may remain disrupted for much longer in some people.

Most individuals who experience taste changes during COVID-19 regain their normal sense of taste within weeks or months. However, a small group continues to experience symptoms long after the infection has resolved. The new findings provide measurable biological evidence that could help explain why recovery takes so much longer for these patients.

"Our findings offer measurable evidence of long-term taste disruption in some post-COVID patients long after the virus has been cleared," the authors write.

Although the discovery sheds light on a previously unexplained symptom, important questions remain. Scientists still need to determine whether these molecular abnormalities can eventually return to normal and whether treatments designed to restore healthy taste signaling could help people recover their sense of taste.

#348. "Mind-blowing" Alzheimer’s breakthrough: Two hours of sleep restored without clearing brain plaques

Scientists have discovered an unexpected reason why Alzheimer's disease disrupts sleep, and their findings suggest that the problem may be reversible. In experiments involving mice with Alzheimer's-like brain changes, researchers restored more than two hours of sleep per day without removing the amyloid plaques associated with the disease.

The surprising discovery points to an overlooked culprit: the brain's own immune cells. Rather than simply protecting the brain from damage, these cells can trigger inflammation that interferes with healthy sleep.

Researchers at the University of Kentucky found that temporarily eliminating most of these immune cells allowed the mice to sleep longer and spend more time in the deep, restorative stages of sleep. The results suggest a potential new approach to treating one of the most disruptive symptoms of Alzheimer's disease.

The findings, published in the journal Alzheimer's & Dementia, were reported by a team led by Shannon L. Macauley, Ph.D., an associate professor of physiology at the University of Kentucky College of Medicine, and first author Nicholas J. Constantino, Ph.D., a recent doctoral graduate.

Macauley described the discovery as "paradigm-shifting" because it challenges the prevailing understanding of why people with Alzheimer's often experience severe sleep problems.

How the Brain's Immune System Can Disrupt Sleep

To understand what the researchers uncovered, imagine a small fire breaking out in a kitchen. Instead of containing the flames, the building's sprinkler system activates everywhere, flooding the entire house and causing widespread damage.

Something similar may happen in the brain during Alzheimer's disease.

Amyloid plaques are deposits of sticky protein fragments that accumulate between nerve cells. They are one of the characteristic features of Alzheimer's and have long been investigated for their role in the disease.

The brain also contains specialized immune cells known as microglia. These cells normally protect the nervous system by removing debris, responding to injuries, and helping maintain a healthy environment for neurons.

However, when microglia detect amyloid plaques, their defensive response can become excessive. Instead of simply addressing the threat, they release inflammatory signals that may interfere with normal brain activity.

Scientists have traditionally linked sleep problems in Alzheimer's primarily to damaged neurons or the accumulation of amyloid plaques. The new findings suggest that the immune system's reaction to those plaques plays a major role.

"Basically, we showed that it is not the plaques themselves, or solely dysfunctional neurons, that cause sleep loss but actually microglia," said Macauley. "Microglia are immune cells that, when they respond to plaques, kick off this elaborate cascade of inflammation, as if the microglia are partying all night, and keeping the brain awake."

In other words, the brain's response to Alzheimer's pathology may create additional problems beyond those caused by the plaques themselves. The researchers compared this widespread reaction to a "whole house response," in which a defense mechanism ends up causing more disruption than expected.

Tracking Alzheimer's Sleep Changes Inside the Brain

To investigate the connection between microglia and sleep, the scientists studied two groups of mice. One group was genetically predisposed to develop amyloid plaques, while the other consisted of normal "wild-type" mice that experienced typical aging.

The researchers examined the animals at two ages: six months, when plaques first appeared in the genetically modified mice, and 18 months, when the disease model showed more advanced pathology.

Comparing these groups helped the scientists distinguish changes associated with Alzheimer's pathology from those that naturally occur as the brain ages.

The team used several advanced technologies to measure sleep and examine what was happening inside the brain.

Each mouse was fitted with a small device that recorded electroencephalography (EEG) and electromyography (EMG).

EEG measures the brain's electrical activity, including the patterns and rhythms produced when networks of neurons communicate. These electrical signals change depending on whether the brain is awake, dreaming or experiencing deep sleep.

EMG measures electrical activity in muscles. Combining the two techniques allowed researchers to identify different sleep stages and determine how long the animals remained in each one.

The scientists also wanted to visualize the relationship between amyloid plaques and immune cells throughout the brain.

For this, they used light-sheet microscopy, an imaging technique that makes biological tissue transparent and illuminates it with a thin layer of laser light. The resulting images can be assembled into detailed three-dimensional maps, allowing scientists to examine structures that would otherwise be difficult to see.

This approach revealed the distribution of plaques and microglia across the brain, helping researchers understand where immune cells were responding to Alzheimer's pathology.

Scientists Temporarily Remove 87% of Brain Immune Cells

To determine whether microglia were actually responsible for disrupted sleep, the team conducted an experiment designed to temporarily eliminate most of these cells.

The researchers administered a drug called Pexidartinib (PLX3397), which was originally developed for cancer research. It blocks a cellular signal that microglia depend on for survival.

After the mice received the medication in their food for 14 days, approximately 87% of their microglia had been depleted.

This allowed the scientists to test whether sleep would improve when the cells driving the inflammatory response were largely absent.

The researchers also performed a detailed analysis of the animals' brain activity using a mathematical method called Fitting Oscillations and One Over Frequency (FOOOF).

This technique separates electrical signals into periodic activity (the rhythmic waves we usually think of as brain waves) and aperiodic activity (the background electrical noise).

By examining these different components, the team could identify changes in how the brain was functioning, including whether it remained unusually active during periods when it should have been resting.

The process is somewhat like monitoring a car engine to determine whether it continues running at high speed even when the vehicle is supposed to be idle.

A Surprising Discovery About Alzheimer's Brain Plaques

Macauley called the results "mind-blowing and unexpected."

One of the most surprising findings was that sleep problems did not continue worsening as amyloid plaques accumulated.

The researchers initially expected that increasing plaque buildup would lead to progressively more severe sleep disturbances. Instead, the mice developed substantial sleep problems relatively early, and those problems remained largely unchanged even as plaque levels increased dramatically.

"I expected that as plaque burden became more severe, sleep disruption would also worsen," said Constantino. "The disruptions in sleep and cortical EEG activity that occur at 6 months, when plaques first emerge, did not worsen by 18 months, despite more than double the amount of plaque burden."

The team described this pattern as a ceiling effect. Essentially, sleep disruption appeared to reach a certain level early in the disease process rather than increasing in direct proportion to the amount of amyloid in the brain.

This suggests that the initial inflammatory response triggered by plaques may be particularly important. Once microglia become activated and disrupt sleep, additional plaque accumulation may not necessarily produce an equivalent increase in sleep loss.

The discovery offers an important clue as to why sleep disturbances can emerge relatively early in Alzheimer's disease.

Alzheimer's Disrupts a Different Type of Sleep Than Normal Aging

The study also revealed a notable difference between sleep changes caused by normal aging and those associated with Alzheimer's pathology.

Sleep is not a single, uniform state. Throughout the night, the brain moves through several stages that perform different functions.

Rapid eye movement (REM) sleep is strongly associated with vivid dreaming and plays an important role in processing memories. The researchers found that normal aging primarily reduced this type of sleep.

Alzheimer's pathology, however, had a different effect. It selectively disrupted non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, which includes the deepest and most physically restorative stages of sleep.

During NREM sleep, the brain performs essential maintenance functions that support learning, memory, and recovery. Deep sleep is also associated with processes that help clear metabolic waste products from the brain.

"That restorative sleep is super important for physical repair, learning and memory and washing out the toxins of the day," Macauley said.

Losing this important stage of sleep could have consequences beyond daytime fatigue.

Because healthy sleep helps maintain the brain's internal environment, disruptions may interfere with processes that normally remove potentially harmful waste. Researchers suspect this could contribute to a self-reinforcing cycle in which Alzheimer's pathology disturbs sleep, and inadequate sleep may further aggravate the disease.

Breaking that cycle could therefore become an important treatment strategy.

More Than Two Hours of Sleep Restored Without Removing Plaques

The most striking result emerged after the researchers temporarily removed most of the microglia from the brains of mice with Alzheimer's-like pathology.

The animals gained more than two hours of sleep per day.

They also experienced longer periods of restorative NREM sleep, giving them more opportunities to transition naturally into REM sleep, which is important for memory formation.

Even more surprisingly, these improvements occurred without any reduction in amyloid plaque levels.

The plaques were still present, but the mice were sleeping substantially better.

This finding provides evidence that the inflammatory response driven by microglia contributes directly to sleep disruption and that suppressing this response can reverse at least some of the associated sleep problems.

It also suggests that researchers may not need to eliminate amyloid plaques to improve sleep in Alzheimer's disease.

Although these findings are promising, the experiments were conducted in mice, and the same benefits have not yet been demonstrated in people. Temporarily removing most of the brain's immune cells is also not an established or practical treatment for Alzheimer's patients.

The researchers are now interested in whether the beneficial effects can be reproduced by adjusting microglial activity rather than eliminating the cells themselves.

If such an approach eventually proves successful in humans, it could provide a way to improve sleep and potentially reduce some of the additional stress that poor sleep places on the brain.

The work was conducted in Macauley's laboratory in the Department of Physiology at the University of Kentucky's Sanders-Brown Center on Aging.

Macauley credits the achievement to a "beautiful partnership" with students and researchers who were willing to challenge established ideas about Alzheimer's disease.

"I love people who take initiative, find their passion, are curious, and keep pushing to find an answer," Macauley said.

She encourages members of her laboratory to be "calculated risk-takers," emphasizing that meaningful discoveries often require scientists to investigate possibilities that may initially seem unlikely.

A quote from hockey legend Wayne Gretzky hangs in her office: "You miss 100% of the shots you don't take."

Constantino, who recently completed his doctorate at the university, said the laboratory's collaborative environment encouraged him to pursue questions that crossed traditional scientific disciplines.

"Dr. Macauley has also taught me to embrace uncertainty and failure as part of the scientific process," Constantino said. "Some of the most interesting studies I have been a part of emerged because our original hypothesis was wrong."

Macauley reinforces this mindset with a simple instruction to her team: "Follow the data, ask better questions, and figure out what is actually happening."

That approach helped the researchers move beyond the traditional emphasis on neurons and amyloid plaques to examine the broader role of the brain's immune system.

Could Existing Medications Help Restore Sleep in Alzheimer's?

The research team is now exploring ways to translate these findings into treatments and diagnostic tools that could eventually benefit people with Alzheimer's disease.

One promising direction involves calming overactive microglia without removing them.

Because microglia perform essential protective functions, eliminating them is not an ideal long-term strategy. Instead, the researchers hope to modify how these immune cells respond to amyloid plaques and regulate inflammation.

The laboratory is investigating existing medications, including the diabetes drug Metformin and the anti-seizure medication Stiripentol.

These drugs are being studied for their potential to alter how microglia produce and use energy, a process known as cellular metabolism. Changing that energy use could potentially reduce excessive immune activation and its harmful effects on sleep.

The hope is to prevent the brain's inflammatory response from becoming so intense that it disrupts normal sleep activity.

Researchers are particularly interested in whether intervention during the earlier stages of Alzheimer's could improve sleep and quality of life before substantial memory loss develops.

"If we can target that process, it might help with quality of life, attention, cognition and confusion," Macauley said.

The proposed drug strategies remain experimental for this purpose, and additional research will be necessary to determine whether they can safely produce similar benefits in humans.

A Simpler Way to Detect Early Alzheimer's Changes

Beyond identifying a potential treatment target, the study uncovered electrical activity patterns that may help distinguish Alzheimer's pathology from normal brain aging.

The researchers believe these patterns could eventually make EEG a useful tool for detecting and monitoring changes associated with Alzheimer's disease.

Unlike more expensive imaging procedures, EEG records electrical signals through electrodes placed on the scalp. It is relatively affordable, does not require surgery, and can be repeated to follow changes over time.

The team envisions EEG becoming a "readily accessible, affordable and longitudinal biomarker of Alzheimer's disease."

A biomarker is a measurable biological sign that provides information about a person's health or a particular disease. A longitudinal biomarker can be tracked repeatedly to monitor how a condition develops or changes.

This article targets Next.js 15.1+, App Router only. Caching behavior differs in Next.js 13 and 14; consult the relevant migration guides if you are on an earlier version.

Stale data that ignores revalidate is one of the most common Next.js support issues. A developer sets revalidate = 60, deploys to production, and pages never reflect the latest content. Or worse, a page that was serving perfectly valid content silently transforms into a cached 404. When Next.js caching revalidate is not working, the root cause is rarely a single misconfiguration. It is nearly always an interaction between multiple cache layers, each with its own lifetime and invalidation logic.

The Next.js App Router maintains four distinct caching mechanisms: the Router Cache (client-side, in-memory), the Full Route Cache (server-side rendered HTML and RSC payload), the Data Cache (individual fetch response storage), and Request Memoization (per-request deduplication). A page can appear stale because any one of these layers holds an outdated response, and diagnosing the responsible layer requires systematic work.

This article walks through the most common reasons ISR revalidation fails, exposes a documented but underappreciated pattern where transient upstream outages produce permanently cached 404s, and provides a copy-paste-safe data-fetching helper that prevents it. A diagnostic decision tree is included as a reference for debugging future issues.

Understanding why next.js ISR stale data appears requires knowing where cached content lives.

Next.js stores individual fetch responses in the Data Cache, keyed by URL and request options. When a fetch call includes next: { revalidate: 60 }, the Data Cache holds that response for 60 seconds before marking it stale. Next.js persists this cache across requests and deployments on platforms like Vercel.

The Full Route Cache stores the complete rendered output of a route, both the HTML and the React Server Component payload. For statically generated routes, Next.js populates this cache at build time. For ISR routes, Next.js populates it on the first request and refreshes it according to the revalidate interval. This is the cache most developers think of when they configure ISR.

On the client side, the Router Cache lives entirely in-memory in the browser. In Next.js 15 the staleTimes defaults are 0 seconds for dynamic pages (not cached) and 5 minutes for static pages and fully prefetched links; 14.x used 30 seconds for dynamic pages (source: Next.js docs, staleTimes). Check the values for your installed version. A page might be fresh on the server but stale in the Router Cache, leading to confusion during manual testing.

A page can be "correct" in the Data Cache but outdated in the Full Route Cache, or fresh on the server but stale in the client's Router Cache. Each layer must be considered independently during debugging.

Next.js ISR follows a stale-while-revalidate model. When a request arrives for a page whose revalidate interval has elapsed, Next.js serves the existing cached page immediately to the visitor. Simultaneously, it triggers a background re-render of the page.

If the background render succeeds, the newly rendered page replaces the old cached page. Subsequent visitors receive the fresh content.

If it throws an error, Next.js retains the previously cached page and logs the error. The stale page keeps serving, which is generally the desired behavior during transient failures.

If the background render calls notFound(), however, the behavior changes in a way that catches many developers off guard. This is where the 404 caching trap lives, and it is the single most damaging failure mode in ISR.

The most frequent cause of nextjs revalidate not working is a configuration conflict between route-segment-level settings and fetch-level settings. Within a segment tree, the lowest export const revalidate value wins. However, fetch-level next: { revalidate } settings apply per-request and can differ from the segment default. A missing value on a nested segment can silently inherit from the parent, producing unexpected intervals.

More subtly, mixing fetch-level caching directives with route-segment config can cause one to override the other entirely:

Setting cache: 'no-store' on a fetch call opts that fetch out of the Data Cache and causes the route to be treated as dynamic, bypassing the Full Route Cache. The segment-level export const revalidate cannot override a no-store directive on an individual fetch.

The quickest diagnostic is checking the build output. Next.js marks each route in build output with symbols indicating static or dynamic status. In Next.js 13/14, ○ indicates static and λ indicates dynamic. Next.js 15 prints ○ for static, ● for SSG with params and ƒ for dynamic routes; the legend printed at the end of next build lists the symbols for your installed version. If a route expected to be statically generated shows a dynamic indicator, something in its render path is triggering dynamic behavior.

Even when Next.js caching is configured correctly, your deployment platform may override these headers. Vercel, Cloudflare, and self-hosted setups each add their own Cache-Control headers. A CDN s-maxage value that outlives the revalidate interval will serve stale HTML from the CDN edge regardless of whether Next.js has regenerated the page on the origin.

The x-nextjs-cache response header is an essential debugging signal. It returns HIT when the response comes from cache, STALE when the cached response is being served while revalidation occurs in the background, and MISS when the page is rendered fresh. If this header shows STALE but the page content never updates, the problem is likely upstream in a CDN layer. The x-nextjs-cache header is reliably emitted on Vercel. On self-hosted deployments, confirm your reverse proxy forwards it.

revalidatePath purges the Full Route Cache for a specific path, while revalidateTag invalidates all cached fetch responses and unstable_cache entries tagged with a specific string. Using the wrong granularity, or forgetting to tag fetch calls, is a common source of next.js cache not updating.

Both revalidatePath and revalidateTag return void, so there is no result to check. Wrap the calls in try/catch and log caught errors. In Next.js 16, revalidateTag takes a second profile argument (the docs recommend "max") and the one-argument form is deprecated.

The notFound() function internally throws a special error to signal a 404 response. This is an internal implementation detail subject to change; rely on the documented behavior described in the Next.js notFound API reference, not on the internal error string. Its behavior depends on when it is called during the rendering lifecycle.

During prerendering or static generation, calling notFound() causes Next.js to serve a static 404 page with a 404 HTTP status code and cache that 404 as the route's canonical response.

#352. Modern neuroscience is rediscovering a surprising idea freud had 130 years ago

What if the human brain works in ways that Sigmund Freud began describing more than 130 years ago? A new scientific analysis suggests that some of his ideas about the mind share surprising similarities with one of the most influential theories in modern neuroscience.

Published in the scientific journal Entropy, the paper explores connections between Freud's original theories, later developments in psychoanalysis, and a modern approach to brain research known as the prediction paradigm.

This approach describes the brain as a prediction machine. Rather than simply responding to information from the outside world, the brain is constantly anticipating what will happen next. It then compares those expectations with incoming information from the senses and works to reduce any differences between what it predicted and what actually occurred.

Scientists believe this ongoing process plays a central role in how people perceive their surroundings, make decisions, behave, and regulate emotions.

Researchers Erik Stänicke, Bendik Hovet, and Line Indrevoll Stänicke, together with colleagues, argue that this modern understanding of brain function has important parallels with ideas that psychoanalysts have been developing for more than a century.

"For over 130 years, psychoanalysis has developed psychological theories about how predictions take place at a subjective level, which cognitive neuropsychology is now studying at a physiological level."

How Freud's Ideas Connect to the Predictive Brain

Although neuroscience and psychoanalysis approach the mind differently, the researchers suggest that both may be describing related processes.

Neuroscience examines the physical mechanisms behind thought and behavior, often using mathematical models to explain how the brain processes information. Psychoanalysis, which grew out of Freud's work, focuses more on unconscious mental processes, emotional experiences, and the ways past relationships influence present behavior.

In other words, one field investigates what happens inside the brain, while the other explores how those processes are experienced by the individual.

A particularly interesting connection involves the psychoanalytic concept of projection and the neuroscientific concept of prediction.

In psychoanalysis, projection involves attributing feelings, intentions, or qualities to someone else. The researchers suggest that this resembles how the brain uses previously learned expectations to interpret new experiences.

"When we attribute qualities, intentions or feelings to other people, our brain shapes our experience of the world in line with established expectations," says Stänicke.

For instance, someone who has repeatedly experienced rejection may become especially sensitive to signs that other people are rejecting them. Even an ambiguous remark or facial expression might be interpreted negatively because past experiences have shaped what the person expects.

Over time, these expectations can influence how someone approaches unfamiliar situations and forms new relationships.

"This corresponds to the neuroscientific distinction between changing one's own predictions, perceptual inference, and the attempt to make the world conform to them, namely active inference."

Perceptual inference refers to the way the brain interprets sensory information and updates its expectations in response. Active inference, by contrast, involves taking actions that help bring experiences into line with predictions or reduce uncertainty.

Together, these concepts help explain why people do not always experience the world as it objectively is. Their expectations can influence what they notice, how they interpret events, and how they respond.

Why the Brain Holds On to Negative Expectations

The researchers believe the similarities between Freud's ideas and modern neuroscience could offer valuable insights into mental health disorders, particularly those involving deeply established patterns of thinking and behavior.

Both approaches portray the mind as constantly seeking stability and predictability. This tendency is related to homeostasis, the process through which a system maintains a relatively stable internal state.

In the predictive brain model, reducing uncertainty is an important part of maintaining that stability. The brain relies on familiar expectations to make the world easier to understand, even when those expectations are inaccurate or no longer helpful.

"Psychoanalysts refer to the tendency in the mind to recreate familiar relational patterns, even when these are poorly adapted," says Stänicke.

This could help explain why people sometimes repeat harmful patterns in relationships or continue interpreting situations negatively despite evidence that challenges their assumptions.

"Rigid and persistent symptoms, such as paranoid ideas or an internalized critical voice, may be stable but not very flexible prediction models," says Stänicke.

"For example, there may be people who automatically expect criticism, rejection or hostility from others, and therefore interpret situations through this filter despite the fact that reality does not warrant it."

Such expectations can become deeply entrenched because they make experiences feel familiar and predictable. Even when the resulting interpretation is distorted, maintaining an established mental model may feel less uncertain than changing it.

According to Stänicke, this offers a possible explanation for why some psychological symptoms can persist for years and why changing longstanding patterns of thought and behavior often requires considerable time.

The connection is particularly interesting because it suggests that some mental health difficulties may involve more than consciously held beliefs. They may also reflect deeply learned expectations that automatically shape emotional responses and interactions with others.

How Psychotherapy Could Help Change Deeply Rooted Patterns

The researchers also highlight an important distinction between what people consciously believe and what they have learned through experience.

"In addition, both models give us insight into how parts of our expectations of the outside world are not only anchored cognitively, but in procedural memory that is expressed in relational ways of being," he says.

Procedural memory is a type of long-term memory involved in learned habits and skills. It allows people to perform familiar actions without consciously thinking through every step.

In the context of relationships, the researchers suggest that a similar form of learning may help explain why certain emotional reactions and patterns of interaction become automatic.

Someone might intellectually understand that other people are not necessarily hostile or critical, for example, yet still instinctively respond as though rejection is inevitable.

These expectations are not simply thoughts that can be changed through reasoning. They can also be embedded in habitual ways of reacting to people and navigating relationships.

This distinction may help explain why psychotherapy sometimes needs to address personal relationships and emotional experiences rather than focusing only on conscious thoughts.

"Therefore, psychotherapy sometimes has to work relationally. For example, new experiences in the relationship between therapist and patient can gradually help to change entrenched relational patterns."

Through repeated experiences that challenge familiar expectations, patients may gradually develop different ways of interpreting situations and relating to other people.

Bringing Freud's Psychoanalysis and Neuroscience Together

The researchers argue that connecting psychoanalysis with modern predictive brain theory could benefit both fields.

Neuroscience may provide a biological framework for understanding processes that psychoanalysts have long described through subjective experience. At the same time, psychoanalytic theories may offer more detailed explanations of how expectations are experienced emotionally, interpreted personally, and expressed through relationships.

The proposed similarities do not establish that Freud's broader theories were correct. Instead, they suggest that two very different traditions of studying the mind may offer complementary ways of understanding some of the same psychological processes.

By examining both the brain's physical mechanisms and the individual's inner experience, researchers hope to develop a more complete understanding of human behavior and mental health.

"Bringing these two fields together can open up for a more holistic psychology, in which both neurological mechanisms and subjective experience are included. In this way, we can understand subjectivity in a more scientific manner."
