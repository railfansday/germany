When front-end developers and UX engineers are tasked with building a web interface that feels tactile, bouncy, or destructive, the industry instinct is almost always the same: reach for a physics engine. Frameworks like Matter.js, Cannon.js, or custom WebGL solutions have become the gold standard for creating immersive, gamified websites.

When our team at Isadora Agency set out to build Stress Release, a digital stress-relief squeeze toy designed to let burnt-out creatives smash, stretch, and distort animated UI characters, we initially explored that route. The goal was to build a highly tactile experience where every click yielded a satisfying, squishy reaction.

But as we began prototyping, we realized something crucial: Physics engines produce plausible motion, but in our case, the animators produced intentional motion.

We didn’t need our characters to act like realistic rubber balls bouncing uncontrollably around a canvas. We needed them to react in very specific, highly designed ways. So, we scrapped the physics engine entirely.

In this article, we’ll break down how we built a real-time stress-relief squeeze toy without a single line of WebGL or Matter.js, relying entirely on programmatic Lottie state controls, DOM manipulation, and distance-based math.

Our core requirement for Stress Release was absolute deterministic control. Our animators had crafted bespoke .json Lottie files that required exact, frame-by-frame sequencing.

For instance, our ‘mega squeeze’ reaction required a precise 181-frame build-up followed by a specific release sequence. To honor this design, we needed an architecture that wouldn’t overwrite the animators’ crafted keyframes with algorithmic approximations.

The tighter the click-feedback loop (click → squish → score), the more you need deterministic frame control. By choosing programmatic state control using Lottie’s native API, we ensured that the interaction layer acted as a flawless trigger for the animation layer.

Because our architecture relied on Lottie and the standard DOM, rendering is handled directly by the Lottie runtime, which plays the JSON-based vector animations as SVGs internally. We selected elements directly by ID and CSS class, driving their behavior using a combination of Lottie animation segments, CSS transforms, and click-event math.

#194. Meet the Anthropic Engineer Keeping Its AI Agent Trustworthy



If you’ve ever wondered how Claude and similar AI agents can answer complex questions in seconds and where they get the information, you’re not alone.

Gurmeet Saran works to find that answer every day as an engineer at Anthropic in San Francisco, the AI research company behind Claude. The IEEE senior member is developing an infrastructure to understand the mechanics behind the chatbot’s behavior and to ensure its underlying architecture produces reliable outputs.

“My job is to make sure the data is accurate, structured, and trustworthy, and that private data stays private,” he says. “As I learned earlier in my career, privacy can’t be a layer you bolt on—it has to be part of the architecture from the start.” If you can’t trust the logic, he adds, you can’t make reliable claims about the behavior, and that has real safety implications.

Safety is a priority for Anthropic, he says. According to Claude’s mission statement, the company wants the AI agent to be “genuinely helpful to the people it works with or on behalf of, as well as to society, while avoiding actions that are unsafe, unethical, or deceptive.” Crucial to the mission is ensuring that researchers can observe and understand how AI models behave, a challenge that is especially acute for large language models, Saran says.

“We have decent tools to monitor databases and web services,” he says, “but for LLMs, we’re still figuring it out.”

Solving data quality problems at Yahoo, Intuit, and Meta

Saran’s interest in computing systems dates back to his childhood in Kolkata, where he logged as much time as he could in the school computer lab.

“I remember the first time I used one of the computers,” he says. “What struck me was that you could write instructions, and the machine would follow them exactly. No ambiguity.

“That felt magical to me.”

After graduating from high school in 1999, he stayed in India to pursue advanced degrees. He earned a bachelor’s degree in electronics and telecommunication engineering in 2004 at the University of Kalyani. In 2010, he earned a master’s degree in software engineering from the Birla Institute of Technology and Science in Pilani.

While a graduate student, he began his career in 2007 at Yahoo as a technical lead of the data team in Bengaluru, India. While there, he encountered the project that shaped his career trajectory: working on web analytics and business intelligence reporting solutions.

“We kept running into a frustrating problem,” he recalls. “Different teams would calculate the same website metric—something like daily active site users—using slightly different logic, and end up with conflicting answers. It sounds like a small issue, but when leadership is making decisions about resource allocation and product development using that data, not knowing which number is correct becomes a problem.”

The team built a central data repository with defined metrics, essentially serving as a single source of truth. Saran created a model that defined what “daily active site users” means, breaking down which events counted, how to handle edge cases (or when standard code fails), and more.

With the new model, data teams across the company could rely on a standard method to determine the number of daily active users for the projects and sites they were responsible for and obtain comparable results using the same logic.

From there, Saran and his team built Yahoo! Digits, an internal web analytics tool used for all the company’s products.

Saran left Yahoo in 2010 to join Intuit as a staff engineer in Mountain View, Calif. He helped build the analytics infrastructure that unified data across the company’s products, including TurboTax and QuickBooks accounting software. During his tenure, Intuit developed a feature that detected fraudulent tax filings in real time. It required Saran and other engineers to build a system to protect taxpayer data while adhering to strict U.S. Internal Revenue Service policies.

“IEEE operates in a lot of countries where engineers don’t have easy pathways into the global professional community. That kind of connection matters as AI development spreads beyond a handful of organizations.”

“This taught me that privacy and governance can’t be layers you bolt onto software after the fact,” he says. “They have to be part of the architecture from the start.”

Saran joined Meta (known as Facebook at the time) in 2016 as a lead software engineer in Menlo Park, Calif. The company was facing controversy over its video metrics: Meta publicly acknowledged an error that caused it to overstate the number of views for posted videos.

“This wasn’t a coding bug; it was an architectural problem,” Saran says. He built a system that worked similarly to what he had developed at Intuit, whereby one single truth was defined to govern all video view metrics across the site.

That system, Metric360, eventually became part of the site’s architecture. By the time he left Meta in 2021, he says, Metric360 was being used across the company to calculate video metrics.

He left the company to become a principal software engineer at Wealthfront, a financial services company in Palo Alto, Calif. He helped build the company’s data infrastructure.

Building trustworthy AI systems at Anthropic

Saran joined Anthropic in 2025 as a member of the technical staff.

To describe the implications of his work, he uses a medical analogy: “Imagine a doctor is monitoring a patient’s vital signs and making treatment decisions based on those numbers. What if one or a few of the medical devices used were slightly miscalibrated? Even if most readings are fine, it’s a serious problem because the difficult decisions happen in the margins.

“It’s the same with AI systems. My job is essentially to make sure the instruments are giving accurate readings—that the data means what it says it means.”

His job comes with high stakes and complex problems to solve.

“AI agents are especially tricky to observe,” he says. “When a system is taking sequences of actions [generating conversational responses with different users], you need a way to capture what it did, what information it was working from, and whether outcomes matched what the end user expected. And you have to do all of that without exposing anyone’s private information—the systems are designed to recognize sensitive data and keep it protected as it flows through. It’s a much more demanding observability problem than anything I’ve worked on before.”

Solving for system reliability while AI is still in its early days of adoption is both Saran’s passion and the problem that keeps him up at night, he says.

“I think making AI systems genuinely auditable and transparent is one of the more important engineering problems of the next decade,” he says, “and it doesn’t get talked about as much as model capabilities do.”

IEEE can play a role in shaping AI development

Saran joined IEEE in March 2025.

“After more than 20 years of building data infrastructure across organizations like Yahoo, Intuit, Meta, and Wealthfront,” he says, “I wanted to contribute back to the technical community through peer review, publishing, and engaging with the broader engineering research ecosystem.”

He says he believes IEEE can play an essential role in shaping the future of AI through its standards work.

“We have standards for everything, from building codes to software testing,” he says. “AI infrastructure needs something similar, and IEEE has the credibility and the reach to do that.”

Another problem that IEEE can solve for, he says, is access: “IEEE operates in a lot of countries where engineers don’t have easy pathways into the global professional community. That kind of connection matters, especially as AI development spreads beyond a handful of organizations in a few cities.”

#196. Massive study of 460,000 people challenges ultra-processed food cancer fears

A massive study involving more than 460,000 Americans is challenging assumptions about ultra-processed foods and colorectal cancer. Researchers found that eating more ultra-processed food (UPF) was not associated with a higher overall incidence of colorectal cancer (CRC). However, the findings revealed an important distinction: Some types of ultra-processed foods were linked to lower cancer rates, while others were associated with higher rates.

The research, led by Erikka Loftfield of the National Cancer Institute and published October 15 in the open-access journal PLOS Medicine, suggests that the health effects of ultra-processed foods may depend heavily on what those foods actually contain.

Ultra-processed foods account for more than half of the calories consumed by American adults. These products have attracted increasing scrutiny because of their potential connections to chronic diseases, including colorectal cancer (CRC). Yet the category includes a remarkably diverse assortment of foods, making it difficult to judge their health effects as a single group.

For example, some ultra-processed whole grain and dairy products have been associated with a lower risk of colorectal cancer, while processed meats have been linked to a higher risk. This raises an important question about whether the level of processing alone is a useful indicator of a food's potential health effects.

A Massive Study Tracks Cancer Risk for Nearly 19 Years

To investigate these differences, researchers examined health and dietary records from 461,682 Americans who were between 50 and 71 years old when they joined the NIH-AARP Diet and Health Study in 1995 and 1996.

At the beginning of the study, participants completed questionnaires describing how frequently they consumed different foods. Researchers used these responses to estimate each person's intake of ultra-processed foods.

Participants were then tracked through 2018, with a median follow-up period of 18.8 years. This allowed the team to examine how dietary patterns were associated with the development of colorectal cancer over nearly two decades.

The results showed no overall association between total ultra-processed food consumption and colorectal cancer incidence. In other words, participants who consumed more ultra-processed foods did not, as a group, experience higher rates of colorectal cancer.

However, a closer examination of individual food categories revealed a much more complicated picture.

Some Ultra-Processed Foods Linked to Lower Cancer Risk

The researchers found that higher consumption of whole grains, fiber, dairy products, and calcium from ultra-processed food sources was associated with a lower incidence of colorectal cancer.

Meat from ultra-processed sources showed the opposite pattern, with greater consumption associated with higher cancer incidence.

The team also examined what might happen when ultra-processed foods were replaced with less processed alternatives. Their analysis found that substituting minimally processed foods for 10% of ultra-processed food intake by weight was associated with a 2% lower incidence of colorectal cancer.

These findings suggest that the nutritional characteristics of individual foods may be more informative than simply classifying everything as ultra-processed. Foods placed in the same broad category can have very different associations with cancer risk.

Why the Findings Come With Important Limitations

Despite the study's large size and lengthy follow-up period, the researchers caution that its conclusions may not apply equally to everyone.

Most participants were older, white Americans, meaning the findings may not accurately reflect the experiences of younger adults or more racially and ethnically diverse populations.

Another important limitation involves how dietary information was collected. Participants reported their eating habits only once, at the beginning of the study. Researchers therefore could not account for changes in individual diets over the following decades.

The availability and variety of ultra-processed foods also increased during that time. As a result, the initial dietary assessments may have underestimated participants' actual consumption during the study period.

Because the research was observational, it also cannot establish whether particular foods directly increase or decrease cancer risk.

Rather than treating all ultra-processed foods as equally harmful, the findings support evaluating foods according to their nutritional content and specific health associations.

The results also align with existing dietary recommendations for colorectal cancer prevention, which encourage eating whole grains, fiber, dairy products, and calcium-rich foods while limiting processed meat.

"In this large population of older US adults, we did not observe an association between total ultra-processed food (UPF) intake and colorectal cancer incidence; however, when we looked at food groups, like whole grains, dairy, and meat, by UPF or non-UPF source, we found that associations were in line with current dietary guidance for colorectal cancer prevention such that higher whole grain and dairy but lower meat intakes were associated with lower colorectal cancer incidence. "

Ultimately, the study highlights why the label "ultra-processed" may not tell the whole story. When it comes to colorectal cancer, the kinds of foods people eat appear to matter more than their processing category alone.

#203. Rejuvenation breakthrough: Scientists reverse 60 years of aging in human cells

Scientists in Germany have found a way to dramatically reverse the molecular age of human cells, transforming blood cells from an 80-year-old person into stem cells with an age signature of less than 20 years.

Researchers at the University Hospital of Bonn and the University of Bonn achieved this remarkable transformation by reprogramming immature red blood cells into neural stem cells, which can eventually develop into brain cells. During the process, molecular markers associated with aging were effectively reset, making the cells more than 60 years younger at the molecular level.

Unlike earlier approaches, the rejuvenation happened gradually, allowing scientists to follow the changes over an extended period. This slower process could provide important clues about how cellular aging can be reversed and potentially help researchers investigate treatments for diseases associated with aging.

The findings were published in the journal Aging Cell.

How Scientists Turn Blood Cells Into Brain Stem Cells

The human body contains hundreds of specialized cell types, all of which originate from a single fertilized egg and carry essentially the same genetic instructions. As cells develop, however, they take on specific roles. Under normal circumstances, a skin cell cannot transform into a liver cell, and a blood cell cannot become a nerve cell.

Modern laboratory techniques have made it possible to overcome these biological restrictions. Scientists can now reprogram certain mature cells to adopt entirely different identities. For example, skin cells can be converted into cells capable of producing nerve tissue, an approach that could eventually contribute to treatments for neurodegenerative diseases.

The process relies on a combination of proteins called transcription factors, which regulate how genes are activated. By changing which genetic instructions a cell follows, these proteins can redirect its development and give it a new cellular identity.

"Using this method, we have directly converted red blood cell precursors into neural stem cells," explains Prof. Dr. Oliver Brüstle, director of the Institute of Reconstructive Neurobiology at the University Hospital of Bonn.

Earlier experiments by the Bonn team had already demonstrated that nerve cells generated through this approach could successfully connect with existing neurons after being transplanted into the brains of mice.

Scientists Reverse More Than 60 Years of Cellular Aging

The latest investigation focused on an especially intriguing consequence of cellular reprogramming: the dramatic reversal of molecular aging.

"In our current study, however, we focused on a different phenomenon: namely, the observation that cells become significantly rejuvenated during reprogramming," emphasizes Brüstle, who is also a member of the Transdisciplinary Research Area (TRA) Life & Health at the University of Bonn.

To determine how much the cells had rejuvenated, the researchers examined their molecular clocks, particularly biological markers known as epigenetic clocks. These clocks estimate a cell's biological age by analyzing chemical modifications to DNA that tend to accumulate or change as organisms grow older.

Importantly, these modifications do not alter the DNA sequence itself. Instead, they affect how frequently certain genetic instructions are used (epigenetics). Because these patterns change predictably with age, scientists can use them to estimate how old a cell appears at the molecular level.

The results were striking. After reprogramming, cells taken from an 80-year-old individual displayed molecular age signatures corresponding to someone younger than 20. In other words, the process had reversed more than six decades of cellular aging.

"We also know that this epigenetic rejuvenation ensures that the cells actually behave like young cells," says the scientist.

A Slower Rejuvenation Process Reveals New Clues

Scientists have previously observed that reprogramming cells can reset their biological age. However, earlier demonstrations of this effect involved a more complicated process requiring two distinct stages.

First, researchers would convert an existing cell, such as a blood cell, into a pluripotent stem cell. These versatile cells have nearly unlimited developmental "career paths" because they can give rise to many different cell types throughout the body.

The pluripotent stem cell would then be guided into becoming a neural stem cell, which is more specialized and can produce cells found in the nervous system.

This conventional approach produces rejuvenation relatively quickly, making it difficult to examine all the changes taking place.

The Bonn researchers instead bypassed the pluripotent stage entirely.

"In contrast, we converted blood cells directly into neural stem cells -- that is, without first passing through the pluripotent cell stage," said Brüstle. "In our approach, rejuvenation occurred gradually and could be tracked for over 100 days."

The researchers observed a dramatic reduction in molecular age, with the study highlighting rejuvenation of approximately 60 years after 50 days. Because the process continued over a much longer period, scientists could examine the biological changes as they unfolded rather than observing only the final result.

What Cellular Rejuvenation Could Mean for Alzheimer's Research

The ability to watch cells gradually become younger could be particularly valuable for scientists trying to understand the mechanisms that control biological aging.

By studying this extended transformation, researchers can investigate which molecular pathways are involved and test whether particular substances can speed up or slow down the rejuvenation process.

"Rejuvenation that extends over such a long period of time is ideal as an experimental model: Since the epigenetic clocks are slowly reset over several weeks, we can use this model to investigate which factors and active substances accelerate or slow down the rejuvenation process."

The findings could be especially relevant to neuroscience, where aging is closely connected to the development of serious neurological conditions. Although the work does not establish a treatment capable of reversing aging in people, it offers a laboratory model for investigating how cells regain youthful characteristics.

This could eventually help scientists better understand the cellular changes associated with conditions such as Alzheimer's disease.

"After all, age is the most important risk factor for neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's!"

#210. How Baseline Can Help You Ship Less JavaScript

Jad is an independent JavaScript & Web Performance consultant and educator who has trained developers worldwide through online platforms, in-person … More about Jad ↬

Weekly tips on front-end & UX.Trusted by 182,000+ folks.

Most of us install a dependency once and never look at it again. It does its job, the tests pass, and we move on. But the web platform keeps moving too, and a surprising number of the libraries sitting in your package.json today are now built into the browser.

In a typical mid-sized JavaScript app, you can often find somewhere between 60KB and 90KB (minified and gzipped) of dependencies that the platform can now handle on its own. Date and number formatting, HTTP requests, modals, tooltips, deep cloning, grouping arrays: these were all real gaps a few years ago. A lot of them aren’t gaps anymore.

The reason those libraries stick around isn’t laziness. It’s that most teams don’t re-audit their dependencies on a Baseline cadence, or are simply not aware of how fast browsers are shipping these days. You check npm audit for security, but is this library still doing something the browser can’t? is a question that rarely gets asked. So the libraries stay.

In this article, we’ll run that audit together. Instead of going through dependencies one by one, we’ll work in clusters, because the wins tend to come in groups. We’ll do the bundle math, build a small decision framework you can reuse, and stay honest about the cases where the platform still falls short. By the end, you’ll have a repeatable process you can run on your own package.json.

Before we start deleting things, let’s quickly recap what Baseline is. Feel free to skip this section if you’re already familiar.

Baseline is a project from the WebDX Community Group that tells you, in plain terms, how safe a web feature is to use across the major browsers (Chrome, Edge, Firefox, and Safari). A feature can be in one of three states:

That 30-month gap between “Newly” and “Widely” matters a lot for this audit. A feature that’s Widely available is something you can usually drop a library for today. A feature that’s only Newly available is something you can drop a library for if you check your audience first, or if you’re comfortable with a small feature check. We’ll treat those two cases differently throughout.

You can look any feature up on webstatus.dev, on MDN (every reference page shows a Baseline badge near the top), or programmatically with the web-features npm package. We’ll use all three later when we run the audit on a real project.

It’s tempting to read “the browser does this now” and start ripping libraries out. Let’s not do that. A swap that looks free on paper can quietly break things for a chunk of your users, or cost you a feature you were relying on without realizing it.

So before dropping any library, ask three questions. We’ll reuse these in every cluster below.

1. Is the replacement Baseline-safe for my audience?

Not “is it Baseline” in the abstract, but “is it safe for the people who actually use my app.” If the native feature is Widely available, this is usually a yes. If it’s only Newly available, check your analytics or your browserslist config and see what share of your users would miss out. A B2B dashboard where everyone’s on the latest browser is a very different situation from a public-facing site with a long tail of old Android devices.

Dropping a library isn’t always free. Sometimes the native feature isn’t supported widely enough yet, so you’d reach for a polyfill. If that polyfill is heavier than the library you’re removing, you’ve made your bundle bigger, unless you load it conditionally. We’ll see exactly this with Temporal later.

3. Does the platform feature cover my real use case?

Libraries often do more than the platform feature they resemble. axios isn’t just fetch with automatic JSON parsing; it has interceptors, request cancellation, and retries. If you’re using those, a straight swap to fetch will leave you reimplementing them. Check what you actually use before assuming it’s a drop-in replacement.

Keep these three in mind. Every cluster below is really just these questions applied to a different corner of your dependencies.

#211. NASA’s Perseverance Mars rover just shattered the all-time off-world driving record

NASA's Perseverance rover has officially set a new driving record on another world, traveling farther across an extraterrestrial surface than any vehicle before it. On October 11, its odometer reached 28.10 miles (45.22 kilometers), surpassing the previous record of 28.06 miles (45.16 kilometers) held by NASA's Opportunity rover, which explored Mars from 2004 to 2018.

Even more impressive is how quickly Perseverance accomplished the feat. Opportunity needed nearly 15 years to cover its record distance, while Perseverance did it in just five and a half years. The milestone came on the rover's 2,006th Martian day, known as a sol.

The record was set as Perseverance made its way toward a location called "Idubi," traveling through an area beyond the western rim of Jezero Crater known as "Lac de Charmes." This region contains what may be some of the oldest terrain ever examined in the solar system.

The landscape preserves a remarkable glimpse into the distant past. Similar rocks on Earth have largely disappeared because of erosion and the continual movement of the planet's crust through plate tectonics (Mars doesn't have tectonic plates). At Idubi, scientists hope to uncover evidence of ancient hydrothermal systems, where heated water once circulated through rocks and may have created conditions suitable for life.

How NASA's Self-Driving Mars Rover Broke the Record

One of the biggest reasons Perseverance has been able to travel so far is its sophisticated autonomous driving system.

Known as AutoNav, the technology allows the rover to navigate much of the Martian landscape without waiting for detailed driving instructions from Earth. As Perseverance moves, it continuously captures images of its surroundings, constructs a three-dimensional map of potential obstacles, and selects a safe route between destinations chosen by mission controllers.

NASA engineers describe this process as "thinking while driving."

The system operates while Perseverance travels at or near its maximum speed, allowing the rover to cover as much as 1,351 feet (412 meters) in a single Martian day.

More than 90% of the distance Perseverance has traveled has involved AutoNav evaluating the terrain. By comparison, the earliest versions of autonomous navigation used by Opportunity and its twin rover, Spirit, were responsible for assessing only about 5% of their driving distances.

The technological progress is even clearer when compared with NASA's first Mars rover, Sojourner. That early robot evaluated just 20 measurement points to determine the safest route ahead. Perseverance now analyzes approximately 600,000 points each time it examines the surrounding terrain.

"It is an honor to be working on Perseverance at a moment when the technology has evolved to the point that drives like these are routine," said Steven Lee, the project manager of the Curiosity and Perseverance rovers at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Southern California. "The original mission called for no more than 12.4 miles of driving. We've blown well past that, and with all mobility systems fully operational and favorable extended-ground-life test results, we expect Perseverance to keep adding to this record for many years to come."

NASA has already approved Perseverance's first mission extension. Beginning in October, the extended mission will allow the rover to continue investigating the rim of Jezero Crater through approximately the end of 2028.

Nearly 30 Years of Mars Rover Exploration

Perseverance's achievement builds on almost three decades of robotic exploration on Mars. With each new generation, NASA's rovers have become more capable of traveling longer distances, navigating difficult terrain, and investigating locations that earlier vehicles could never have reached.

The journey began in 1997 with Sojourner, a small rover roughly the size of a microwave oven. During its 83-sol mission, it traveled approximately 330 feet (100 meters).

NASA took a major step forward in 2004 with the arrival of Spirit and Opportunity. These golf-cart-size rovers were originally designed to operate for just 90 Martian days, but both dramatically exceeded expectations.

Spirit covered 4.8 miles (7.7 kilometers) before its mission ended in 2010 after it failed to survive the harsh Martian winter.

Opportunity continued operating much longer, remaining active for an extraordinary 5,111 sols. In 2015, it became the first vehicle to complete a marathon distance on another world. Its total mileage eventually established the driving record that Perseverance has now broken.

Meanwhile, NASA's Curiosity rover, which landed in Gale Crater in 2012 and continues exploring today, has traveled more than 23.65 miles (38.07 kilometers).

Perseverance Is Becoming an Even Smarter Driver

Although Perseverance has already surpassed every previous extraterrestrial driving record, NASA engineers are continuing to improve its navigation capabilities.

"The rover that broke this record did so by making its own decisions about the path. Given the autonomy to just go, Perseverance is a better driver than the one that landed, and we're not done," Mark Maimone, deputy team chief for robotic operations on Perseverance, said. "Our newest software teaches Perseverance to steer less and roll more, and to check its own position against orbital maps mid-drive. The next frontier is sunlight itself. Glare in the navigation cameras is why we park by mid-afternoon if we're driving toward the Sun, and if we can teach the rover to see past those artifacts, every rover that follows gets a longer working day."

One challenge is that bright sunlight can interfere with the rover's navigation cameras, making it harder for its software to interpret the terrain. When Perseverance travels toward the Sun, mission planners currently stop driving by mid-afternoon to avoid these problems.

Finding ways to overcome that limitation could allow Perseverance and future Mars rovers to spend more time exploring each day.

The rover's record also extends beyond Mars. Earlier in its journey, Perseverance exceeded two historic driving distances achieved on the Moon.

In 1972, Apollo 17 astronauts Gene Cernan and Harrison Schmitt traveled 22.2 miles (35.7 kilometers) across the lunar surface. The following year, the Soviet Union's robotic Lunokhod 2 rover covered 24.2 miles (39 kilometers). Perseverance has now surpassed both of those distances.

Exploring 4-Billion-Year-Old Rocks for Signs of Ancient Life

For NASA, however, setting mileage records is not the primary objective. The rover's ability to travel farther and faster allows scientists to investigate more geological formations, examine a greater variety of rocks, and piece together Mars' environmental history.

Perseverance has already explored several dramatically different landscapes. Its journey began on an ancient lakebed inside Jezero Crater and continued through the remains of a river system that once carried water and sediment into the basin.

Along that route, the rover encountered a rock called "Cheyava Falls," where scientists identified a potential biosignature. A biosignature is a feature or chemical pattern that could provide evidence of past life, although nonbiological processes can sometimes produce similar clues.

The rover has since climbed toward the crater rim, where scientists are investigating a different type of ancient environment. Instead of the lake and river habitats examined earlier in the mission, researchers are now interested in the possibility that hydrothermal activity once occurred in this region.

Such systems form when water interacts with hot rocks, potentially creating chemical conditions that could support microbial life.

So far, Perseverance has gathered 29 samples of rock and regolith, the loose material that covers the Martian surface. These samples come from several distinct environments, including the crater floor, a fan-shaped deposit left by an ancient river, and the crater rim.

Some rocks in the rim region formed during major impacts approximately 4 billion years ago. They preserve evidence from an extremely early period of planetary history, much of which has been lost on Earth through geological activity.

After completing its investigation of Lac de Charmes, Perseverance is expected to continue traveling along the crater rim before heading south during its extended mission.

One future destination is Rainbow Falls, an enormous piece of ancient Martian crust measuring 328 feet (100-meter-wide). The formation was pushed upward by the powerful impact that originally created Jezero Crater.

As Perseverance continues its journey, each additional mile could bring scientists closer to understanding the ancient environments of Mars and whether the Red Planet once possessed the conditions necessary for life.

#217. Scientists finally uncover why statins can cause muscle pain

Statins are among the most important medications for preventing cardiovascular disease. By lowering cholesterol, they have helped save millions of lives and reduce the likelihood of heart attacks and strokes. However, these benefits can come with unwanted effects. Some patients develop muscle pain or weakness, while a small number experience serious muscle breakdown that can potentially cause kidney failure.

Scientists at the University of British Columbia (UBC), working with colleagues at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, have identified a molecular mechanism that helps explain these complications. Their findings, published in Nature Communications, could guide the development of safer statins that maintain their cholesterol-lowering benefits while minimizing muscle damage.

How Statins Trigger a Harmful Calcium Leak

To investigate what happens inside muscle cells, the researchers used cryo-electron microscopy, an advanced imaging method that allows scientists to examine protein structures in remarkable detail. Their investigation centered on the ryanodine receptor (RyR1), a protein that regulates the movement of calcium within muscle cells.

Under normal conditions, this protein functions as a controlled gateway, releasing calcium when muscles need to contract. The researchers found that statins can interfere with this process by holding the channel open. As a result, calcium escapes continuously into areas where it should be carefully regulated, creating conditions that can injure muscle tissue.

"We were able to see, almost atom by atom, how statins latch onto this channel," said lead author Dr. Steven Molinarolo, a postdoctoral researcher in UBC's department of biochemistry and molecular biology. "That leak of calcium explains why some patients experience muscle pain or, in extreme cases, life-threatening complications."

Three Statin Molecules Reveal the Mechanism Behind Muscle Damage

The researchers concentrated on atorvastatin, a commonly prescribed medication for lowering cholesterol. Although this particular drug was the main focus, their results suggest that similar interactions could occur with other medications in the statin family.

One of the most striking discoveries was the unusual way statin molecules attach to the calcium channel. Rather than acting individually, three molecules occupy a specific pocket within the protein. The first binds while the channel is closed, making it more likely to open. Two additional molecules then enter the pocket and hold the channel open, allowing calcium to continue leaking.

"This is the first time we've had a clear picture of how statins activate this channel," said Dr. Filip Van Petegem, senior author and professor at UBC's Life Sciences Institute. "It's a big step forward because it gives us a roadmap for designing statins that don't interact with muscle tissue."

This detailed understanding could give drug developers a way to address the problem at its source. By modifying the specific features of statin molecules that interfere with muscle proteins, scientists may be able to reduce unwanted effects without compromising the drugs' ability to control cholesterol.

A Potential Path Toward Safer Cholesterol Drugs

More than 200 million people worldwide use statins. Although severe muscle injury occurs in only a small percentage of patients, less serious problems such as soreness, weakness, and fatigue are considerably more widespread. These symptoms can be disruptive enough that some people discontinue their medication, potentially losing important protection against cardiovascular disease.

The discovery could eventually help researchers create treatments that are easier to tolerate, allowing more patients to continue taking medications that reduce their risk of heart attacks and strokes.

Achieving this level of understanding required exceptionally detailed imaging. Working with the high-resolution macromolecular cryo-electron microscopy facility at UBC's faculty of medicine, the team was able to observe precisely how statin molecules interact with the muscle protein. These structural insights connect a longstanding drug safety concern to a specific molecular process, providing valuable information for future medication development.

"Statins have been a cornerstone of cardiovascular care for decades," Dr. Van Petegem said. "Our goal is to make them even safer, so patients can benefit without fear of serious side effects."

Although additional research and drug development will be needed before these findings translate into new treatments, they offer a promising direction. For millions of people who depend on statins to protect their hearts, future medications with fewer muscle-related complications could make treatment more comfortable and improve everyday quality of life.
