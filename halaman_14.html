#314. YouTube starts hiding the dislike button on normal videos

After removing dislike counts years ago, new UI tests at YouTube are making it harder to even using the dislike button at all.

In 2021, YouTube removed the dislike count as a way to “de-weaponize” the button – effort that had been ongoing for years – with YouTube even acknowledging that it was a decision that users might disagree with. Now, following similar changes to Shorts, YouTube appears to be testing further hiding the dislike button.

A report on Twitter/X highlighted by Neowin showcases YouTube hiding the dislike button under an overflow menu, hiding it from view despite the like button remaining front and center. This appears to be on the desktop website, though we couldn’t replicate the same behavior on our end.

It’s not the only case of this happening, though.

Over the past couple of weeks, reports on Reddit have seen the dislike button vanish, with another web user seeing a slightly different UI to the one above, but with the same result of offloading the button to an overflow menu alongside the “Report” button.

YouTube runs tests like this frequently, but given the same change was made to the dislike button on Shorts several months ago, it’s all but certain that the same will happen to standard videos.

#315. NASA reveals 16 stunning galaxies hiding cosmic chaos

Some galaxies glow with sweeping spiral arms, while others are shaped by violent collisions, powerful black holes, and explosive bursts of star formation. A newly released collection of 16 images from NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory and other telescopes reveals the extraordinary diversity of these cosmic structures.

Like precious gemstones, galaxies come in many shapes and sizes, each with characteristics that tell a story about its history. Astronomers generally classify them into three major groups. Spiral galaxies, including our Milky Way, have winding arms extending from their centers. Elliptical galaxies typically contain older stars and are often thought to form through galactic mergers. Irregular galaxies have less orderly structures and can display a wide variety of cosmic activity.

Much as gemstones preserve clues about the geological processes that shaped Earth over billions of years, galaxies hold evidence of the forces that have shaped the universe. Studying these distant systems helps astronomers piece together the Milky Way's history and better understand what may await our galaxy in the distant future.

NASA Combines Chandra, Webb, and Hubble to Reveal Galactic Secrets

The new gallery features 16 images that bring together observations from some of the world's most powerful telescopes. Each includes X-ray measurements gathered by Chandra during its decades of operation in space.

These observations are combined with data from NASA's James Webb and Hubble Space Telescopes, IXPE (Imaging X-ray Polarimetry Explorer), the Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory, NuSTAR (Nuclear Spectroscopic Telescope Array), and additional observatories operating on Earth and in space.

X-ray observations are especially valuable because they reveal extreme activity that other types of light can miss. Chandra can detect enormous clouds of gas heated to millions of degrees by powerful winds from massive stars, material expelled by supermassive black holes, and debris from stellar explosions. These processes also help spread chemical elements through space, including elements that ultimately become part of planets, the air we breathe, and our own bodies.

By combining X-rays with observations at other wavelengths, astronomers can see more of what is happening inside galaxies. The resulting images reveal how these vast systems form stars, interact with their surroundings, and change over billions of years.

Spiral Galaxies Reveal Explosive Star Formation

Several images highlight the remarkable activity taking place inside spiral galaxies. Messier 33 and NGC 3938 are viewed almost directly from above, allowing astronomers to examine their sweeping arms and identify regions containing energetic pairs of stars and the aftermath of stellar explosions.

Other galaxies reveal how their internal structures can help drive the birth of new stars. NGC 1672 and NGC 1385 are barred spiral galaxies, meaning they contain elongated central structures made of stars, gas, and dust. These bars can channel material toward the inner regions, providing fuel for intense episodes of star formation.

NGC 4725 demonstrates another way galaxies can produce new stars. Its observations reveal how an earlier encounter with another galaxy can disturb gas and stimulate star formation.

The collection also includes dramatic side views of NGC 4631 (the Whale Galaxy) and Messier 82 (the Cigar Galaxy). These galaxies feature enormous halos and powerful winds of gas heated to millions of degrees. Driven by intense stellar explosions, these outflows can carry material thousands of light-years into space, spreading important chemical elements into the regions between galaxies.

Supermassive Black Holes Blast Energy Across Galaxies

Some of the most dramatic features in the gallery come from the enormous black holes lurking at the centers of galaxies.

When these black holes actively consume surrounding material, their central regions become extremely energetic. Known as active galactic nuclei, these bright and powerful galactic cores can launch jets of particles and enormous outbursts that influence conditions across their host galaxies.

Centaurus A provides a striking example. Observations from Chandra and IXPE reveal a powerful stream of high-energy particles shooting tens of thousands of light-years into space from the galaxy's central region.

In Messier 106, jets launched from the central supermassive black hole interact with and heat surrounding gas. This activity produces unusual spiral structures that differ from the familiar arms normally associated with spiral galaxies.

The famous Sombrero Galaxy (Messier 104) offers another remarkable view. A supermassive black hole sits within its immense central concentration of stars, while Chandra's X-ray observations reveal a widespread halo of gas heated to millions of degrees and the hot remains of stars. These features surround the galaxy's prominent bands of dust, providing a deeper look at the energetic environment hidden within its elegant appearance.

Violent Galaxy Collisions Offer a Glimpse of the Milky Way's Future

Not every galaxy evolves peacefully. Some undergo enormous transformations as gravity pulls neighboring galaxies together, distorts their structures, or strips away the gas needed to create new stars.

Arp 143 captures the aftermath of a particularly dramatic encounter. A direct galactic collision produced an expanding ring, similar to ripples spreading outward after an impact. The disturbance also triggered waves of new star formation.

Other images show the chaotic consequences of galaxies merging. NGC 3256 and II Zw 096, the latter containing a powerful burst of star formation hidden behind thick dust, provide examples of the violent interactions that were especially common in the early universe.

These collisions also offer clues about the Milky Way's possible future. Our galaxy is expected to merge with the neighboring Andromeda galaxy billions of years from now, and observing other galactic mergers helps scientists understand how such an encounter could unfold.

The gallery includes several other unusual systems. NGC 1569 serves as a nearby example for investigating the intense episodes of star formation that occurred in young galaxies billions of years ago. NGC 660 displays a rare "polar ring" structure, with a ring of stars orbiting around the galaxy's poles rather than following its main plane.

Messier 90 illustrates a different kind of galactic transformation. As it travels through the Virgo Cluster, the galaxy is losing the gas it needs to form new stars. Its movement through the cluster strips away this material, potentially changing how the galaxy evolves.

Together, these 16 cosmic portraits show that galaxies are far from static collections of stars. They are constantly influenced by gravity, stellar explosions, black holes, and interactions with neighboring systems. By revealing these processes in remarkable detail, NASA's Chandra and its partner telescopes are helping astronomers understand the forces that shape galaxies, including our own.

#317. Why AI Is Making Revenue Growth Harder, Not Easier, for Average IT Companies

Have 30-200 Employees? Make $50k-$500k selling your data for AI Training.

Have 30-200 Employees? Make $50k-$500k selling your data for AI Training.

A few months ago, a delivery head at a mid-sized IT services firm told me about a renewal meeting that went sideways. The client had been paying the same rate card for three years. This time, the procurement lead opened with a simple line: “We know your developers use AI tools now. So why does this project still need twelve people?”

There was no good answer. The firm had adopted AI, and its engineers really were faster. But the savings hadn’t shown up on the firm’s side. They had shown up in the client’s expectations.

That meeting captures the problem. For many average IT companies, AI hasn’t unlocked easy growth. It has made the old growth formula stop working.

For decades, the IT services business ran on a clean equation: revenue = people × billable hours × rate.

Want to grow? Hire more engineers, bill them out, repeat. Costs and revenue moved together. Every new customer needed a few more people, and those people came with predictable salaries, predictable utilization, and predictable margins. It wasn’t glamorous, but it was reliable.

This model made headcount the unit of growth. It also made it easy to forecast, easy to explain to investors, and easy to scale if you could keep recruiting.

AI breaks the equation at its most fundamental point: the link between effort and value.

Most people discuss AI’s impact in one direction: “It makes work faster.” That’s true, but it’s only one-third of the story. Three things shifted at once.

1. The effort behind each deliverable dropped

Code assistants write boilerplate in seconds. Test cases get generated automatically. Documentation, bug triage, and first-line support can be partly automated. A task that took a team a week might now take two days.

On paper, that’s wonderful. In a time-and-material contract, it’s a problem, because fewer hours means less revenue for the same outcome.

2. Client expectations moved faster than vendor capabilities

Clients read the same headlines you do. They see demos of AI building an app in minutes. They don’t see the review, security checks, integration work, and edge cases that make production software hard. So they assume the price should fall, and they push for it in every negotiation.

The expectation of a discount arrives immediately. The efficiency gains arrive slowly and unevenly.

3. New costs appeared that nobody budgeted for

AI isn’t free to run. Companies now face costs they didn’t have before:

Usage-based bills for API calls, tokens, and cloud compute that grow with every project

Licensing fees for AI coding tools across hundreds of seats

Data preparation work, which is often the most expensive and least visible part of any AI project

Specialized talent such as ML engineers and prompt and evaluation specialists, who command premium salaries

Governance and security overhead, including legal reviews, client data policies, and compliance work

Constant retraining, because the tools change every few months

So revenue per project is under pressure while the cost of serving each project gets more complicated. That’s the squeeze.

Not every IT firm is suffering. The top tier is doing fine, and so are small specialists with a sharp niche. The ones in trouble are in the middle: capable and reputable, but not especially differentiated.

If you offer the same services as ten other vendors, your main lever in a negotiation is price. When AI lowers the cost of producing work across the whole industry, every vendor feels pressure to cut rates. Whoever blinks first resets the market for everyone.

Firms with unique domain expertise, proprietary tools, or deep client relationships can hold their ground. Generalists usually can’t.

They bought tools without changing the model

This is the most common mistake. A company rolls out AI assistants to all developers, celebrates the productivity boost, and then keeps selling the same way: same contracts, same staffing pyramids, same pricing logic.

The result is odd. Engineers finish work sooner, but the firm still bills by the hour, so efficiency directly cuts revenue. Or the firm keeps the bench full to maintain utilization, so efficiency creates idle capacity. Either way, the savings vanish.

Adopting AI without redesigning how you sell and deliver is like buying a faster truck but still charging by the mile.

IT services have traditionally relied on a wide base of junior engineers doing routine work, supervised by a smaller layer of seniors. AI is very good at routine work. That shrinks the base, which was both a revenue engine and a talent pipeline.

Average firms now face an uncomfortable choice. Keep a big junior base and watch margins erode, or shrink it and struggle to develop the senior engineers they’ll need in five years.

They lack the data and platforms to compete

Large players invest heavily in reusable accelerators, internal platforms, and proprietary datasets. These let them deliver at lower marginal cost with each new client. Average firms often rebuild from scratch on every project. That means their cost of serving each new customer stays high while their larger competitors’ costs fall.

This is the heart of the issue: growth is only valuable if each new customer costs less to serve than the last, or at least no more. For average firms, AI has quietly flipped that into the opposite direction.

Every client wants a custom AI experiment

Clients increasingly ask for pilots, proofs of concept, and “AI-enabled” versions of existing systems. Many of these never reach production. But each one consumes senior time, cloud budget, and management attention. Without discipline, a firm can end up doing lots of low-margin experimental work that looks like growth in the pipeline but behaves like cost in the books.

The firms handling this well aren’t necessarily the biggest. They’ve simply stopped treating AI as a tool and started treating it as a reason to redesign the business.

Instead of charging for effort, they charge for results: a migration completed, a process automated, a defect rate reduced. When AI makes delivery faster, the firm keeps part of the gain, because the client is paying for the outcome rather than the time spent.

This requires confidence in your estimates and a willingness to share risk. But it aligns incentives in a way hourly billing never can.

If you’ve built the same integration five times, it should be a product, or at least a reusable accelerator. Packaged services with fixed scope and pricing turn one-off labor into repeatable revenue. AI makes this easier, since it can handle much of the customization at the edges.

Many average firms track utilization and billing rates but not the true cost of delivering AI-related work. Stronger firms break it down: compute, licenses, rework, senior review time, and failed pilots. They know which accounts are actually profitable after AI costs, and they make decisions accordingly, including walking away from unprofitable work.

Training isn’t treated as an HR expense. It’s a margin strategy. A team that knows how to use AI tools well, review their output critically, and avoid expensive mistakes delivers faster and with fewer errors. A team that uses them poorly generates rework, security risks, and inflated cloud bills.

The gap between a well-trained team and an untrained one with the same tools can be larger than the gap between having the tools and not having them.

When routine coding becomes cheaper, value shifts toward the things AI can’t easily replace: understanding a client’s business, designing architecture, managing risk, integrating messy legacy systems, and taking accountability for results. Firms that invest in consulting depth and domain expertise can charge for judgment rather than keystrokes.

If you run or advise an IT company, you don’t need a grand transformation to start. Try these:

Audit your contracts. Identify which are purely time-and-material and which have room for fixed-price or outcome-based structures. Start by converting one or two with friendly clients.

Calculate true AI cost to serve. Add up tool licenses, compute, and extra review time per project. Many leaders are surprised by the number.

Find your repeatable work. List the five services you deliver most often and ask which could become a packaged offering.

Rethink your staffing pyramid. Decide deliberately how you’ll build junior talent when routine tasks are automated, rather than letting the pipeline dry up by accident.

Invest in structured training. Focus on practical skills: using AI tools responsibly, validating outputs, and understanding cost implications.

Prepare your pricing story. When clients ask for AI discounts, have a clear answer about where value comes from beyond effort: quality, accountability, speed to market, and risk reduction.

Track a small set of metrics. Revenue per employee, gross margin by account, AI spend as a percentage of delivery cost, and rework rates will tell you more than headline revenue growth.

AI hasn’t made IT services less valuable. It has made the old way of measuring and pricing that value outdated. When effort stops being the unit of worth, companies that built their models on selling effort face a real adjustment.

Average firms struggle because they are caught between two forces: clients expecting lower prices and new costs they didn’t plan for. The way out isn’t to resist AI or to adopt it blindly. It’s to rebuild the model around outcomes, reusable assets, skilled people, and honest numbers.

Revenue growth is still possible. But it now belongs to the companies that understand a simple truth: in the AI era, growing revenue is easy to want and hard to earn, and the real prize is growing it without growing your costs at the same pace.

#319. Alaska’s monster tsunami sent water 1,580 feet up a mountain

On the evening of Aug. 9, 2025, passengers on the Hanse Explorer finished taking selfies and videos of the South Sawyer Glacier, and the ship headed back down the fjord. Twelve hours later, a landslide from the adjacent mountain unexpectedly collapsed into the fjord, initiating the second-highest tsunami in recorded history.

We conduct research on earthquakes and tsunamis at the Alaska Earthquake Center, and one of us serves as Alaska state seismologist. In a new study with colleagues, we detail how that landslide sent water and debris 1,580 feet (481 meters) up the other side of the fjord – higher than the top floor of the Taipei 101 skyscraper – and then continued down Tracy Arm. The force of the water stripped the fjord’s walls down to bare rock.

It was just after 5 o’clock in the morning on a dreary day, and fortunately, no ships were nearby. In the months after, some cruise lines started avoiding Tracy Arm. However, the conditions that led to this event are not at all unique to this fjord.

Landslides are common in the coastal mountains of Alaska where rapid uplift, caused by tectonic forces and long-term ice loss, converges with the erosive forces of precipitation and moving glaciers. But a curious pattern has emerged in recent years: Multiple major landslides have occurred precisely at the terminus of a retreating glacier.

Though the mechanics are still poorly understood, these mountains appear to become unstable when the ice disappears. When the landslide hits the water, the momentum of millions of tons of rock is transferred into tsunami waves.

This same phenomenon is playing out from Alaska to Greenland and Norway, sometimes with deadly consequences. Across the Arctic, countries are trying to come to terms with this growing hazard. The options are not attractive: avoid vast swaths of coastline, or live with a poorly understood risk. We believe there is an obvious role for alert systems, but only if scientists have a better understanding of where and when landslides are likely to occur.

The Tracy Arm landslide is a powerful example.

The landslide occurred in August, when warm ocean waters and heavier precipitation favor both glacier retreat and slope failure. The glacier below the landslide area had experienced rapid calving – large chunks of ice breaking off and falling into the water – and it had retreated more than a third of a mile in the two months prior. Heavy rain had been falling. Rain enters fractures in the mountain and pushes them closer to failure by increasing the water pressure in cracks.

Most provocative are the thousands of small seismic tremors that emanated from the area of the slide in the days prior to the mountainside collapsing.

We believe that this combination of signs would have been sufficient to issue progressive alerts to any ships in the vicinity and homes and businesses that could have been harmed by a tsunami at least a day prior to the failure – had a monitoring program existed.

Escalating alerts are used for everything from terrorism and nuclear plant safety to avalanches and volcanic unrest. They don’t remove the risk, but they do make it easier for people to safely coexist with hazards.

For example, though people are still killed in avalanches, alert systems have played an essential role in making winter backcountry travel safer for more people. The collapse at Tracy Arm demonstrates what could be possible for landslides.

We believe that the combination of weather and rapid glacier retreat in early August 2025 was likely sufficient to issue an alert notifying people that the hazard may be temporarily elevated in a general area. On a yellow-orange-red scale, this would be a yellow alert.

In the hours prior to the landslide, the exponential increase in seismic events and telltale transition to what is known as seismic tremor – a continuous “hum” of seismic energy – were sufficient to communicate a time-sensitive warning for a specific region.

These observations, recorded as a byproduct of regional earthquake monitoring, warranted an “orange” alert noting immediate concern. The signs were arguably sufficient to recommend keeping boats and ships out of the fjord.

Our research over the past few years has demonstrated that once a large landslide has started, it is possible to detect and measure the event within a couple of minutes. In this amount of time, seismic waves in the surrounding area can indicate the rough size of the landslide and whether it occurred near open water.

A monitoring program that could quickly communicate this would be able to issue a red alert, signaling an event in progress.

The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration’s tsunami warning program has spent decades fine-tuning rapid message dissemination. A warning system would have offered little help for ships in the immediate vicinity, but it could have provided perhaps 10 minutes of warning for those who rode out the harrowing tsunami farther away.

There is no landslide monitoring system operating yet at this scale in the U.S. Building one will require cooperation across state and federal agencies, and strengthened monitoring and communication networks. Even then, it will not be fail-proof.

Alert systems do not remove the risk entirely, but they are a better option than no warning at all. Over time, they also build awareness as communities and visitors get used to thinking about these hazards.

Many of the most alluring places on Earth come with significant hazards. Arctic fjords are among them. The same processes that create this hazard – glacier retreat, steep terrain, dynamic geology – are also what make these landscapes so compelling. The mix of glaciers, ice-choked waters and steep mountains is exactly what draws people to these places. People will continue to visit and experience them.

The question is not whether these places should be avoided altogether, but how to help people make more informed decisions. We believe that stronger geophysical and meteorological monitoring, coupled with new research and communication channels, is the first step.

On Aug. 9, visitors unknowingly passed through a landscape on the cusp of failure. An alert system might have given tour companies and people in the area the information they needed to make more informed choices and avoid being caught by surprise.

#324. Why some people escape Alzheimer’s despite carrying a high-risk gene

Some people carry one of the strongest genetic risk factors for Alzheimer's disease yet never develop it, remaining mentally sharp well into their 80s. For years, scientists have wondered what protects these individuals despite their increased genetic vulnerability.

Now, researchers at Columbia University have uncovered a biological mechanism that could help explain this remarkable resistance. Their findings point to a rare genetic mutation that protects the brain's blood vessels from damage, potentially opening a new path toward Alzheimer's treatments.

The discovery builds on research the team published two years ago involving APOE ε4, a gene variant strongly associated with Alzheimer's risk. The scientists found that some people carrying APOE ε4 also possess a rare protective mutation in the fibronectin (FN1) gene.

Fibronectin is a protein that helps support the structure of tissues. However, in people with APOE ε4, excessive amounts can accumulate around blood vessels in the brain, potentially compromising a critical protective system known as the blood-brain barrier. The rare FN1 mutation appears to limit this buildup and the damage associated with it.

That earlier discovery suggested an intriguing possibility: What if scientists could develop a treatment that recreates the protection provided by this rare mutation?

A new study published in Nature Aging brings that possibility closer by identifying how excess fibronectin damages the brain's protective barrier and revealing potential ways to interrupt the process.

"This new study takes us from a genetic clue to a disease mechanism," says Caghan Kizil, the study's corresponding author and professor of neurological sciences in the Department of Neurology and the Taub Institute for Research on Alzheimer's Disease and the Aging Brain at Columbia University Vagelos College of Physicians and Surgeons.

"We knew that changes in fibronectin could protect against Alzheimer's, but we didn't know why excess fibronectin was harmful in the first place. This study gives us that detailed mechanism -- and with it, ideas for how to reproduce that natural protection."

How an Alzheimer's Risk Gene Weakens the Blood-Brain Barrier

The blood-brain barrier serves as a protective boundary between the bloodstream and the brain. Made up of tightly connected cells surrounding the brain's blood vessels, it carefully regulates which substances can enter brain tissue while helping maintain conditions necessary for removing potentially harmful waste.

When this barrier becomes damaged or excessively permeable, substances that would normally be kept out can enter the brain more easily. Such disruptions may contribute to inflammation and other processes involved in neurological diseases, including Alzheimer's.

To investigate the role of fibronectin, the Columbia team combined several advanced research approaches. They examined human brain tissue and cerebrospinal fluid, the clear liquid surrounding the brain and spinal cord, using genetic and epigenetic analyses to explore changes in DNA and gene activity.

Their investigation also included single-cell sequencing, which reveals the activity of individual cells, and spatial imaging to examine where biological changes occur within tissues. Additional experiments involved human stem-cell models, three-dimensional cultures of blood vessels, and studies in zebrafish and mice.

Despite the variety of methods, the findings consistently pointed to the same mechanism: APOE ε4 promotes the production and accumulation of fibronectin around the brain's blood vessels.

In mice engineered to carry human APOE ε4, researchers measured nearly twice as much fibronectin in the brain as in mice carrying APOE ε3, another common version of the gene. The greater accumulation was accompanied by evidence that the blood-brain barrier had become leaky.

Scientists Discover How Fibronectin Causes Brain Damage

One experiment provided especially important evidence that fibronectin does more than simply accumulate in damaged blood vessels.

The researchers increased human fibronectin specifically in astrocytes, specialized support cells that help maintain the blood-brain barrier and keep the brain functioning properly.

Increasing fibronectin in these cells was enough to disrupt the barrier, allowing it to become more permeable. Conversely, reducing fibronectin improved the barrier's ability to function normally.

These results provided experimental evidence that excess fibronectin can directly contribute to blood-brain barrier damage rather than merely appearing alongside it.

"The animal experiments allowed us to move beyond association," says co-first author Prabesh Bhattarai, an associate research scientist in the Kizil lab. "When we increased fibronectin in astroglia, the barrier became permeable. When we reduced fibronectin, barrier function improved."

The team then investigated the molecular events responsible for the damage.

They found that excessive fibronectin interferes with communication between cells by activating abnormal signals through integrins, proteins that help cells sense and respond to their surroundings, and an enzyme known as focal adhesion kinase.

These abnormal signals disrupt a network of growth factors called VEGF, HBEGF, and IGF1. Although growth factors are often associated with cell development and repair, these particular signaling molecules also help astrocytes and blood vessel cells coordinate their activities to maintain a healthy blood-brain barrier.

When their communication becomes disrupted, the barrier can lose its integrity, potentially leaving brain tissue more vulnerable to damage.

"Single-cell analysis allowed us to examine this pathway in the different cells that maintain the brain's blood vessels," says co-first author Elanur Yilmaz, an associate research scientist in the Kizil lab. "We observed the same biological pattern across experimental models and human Alzheimer's tissue, making the mechanism especially compelling."

Evidence From Human Brains Strengthens the Alzheimer's Connection

Importantly, the researchers found signs of similar biological disturbances in human samples, suggesting that the process identified in laboratory experiments may also contribute to Alzheimer's disease in people carrying APOE ε4.

Analyses of brain tissue and cerebrospinal fluid showed that elevated fibronectin levels were associated with inflammation in astrocytes.

Genetic and gene-regulation analyses also connected FN1 to blood vessel disease and changes involving VEGFA, a growth factor that plays an important role in maintaining healthy brain blood vessels.

Together, these findings provide multiple lines of evidence linking excess fibronectin to the vascular damage associated with APOE ε4.

"The convergence of these independent human datasets strengthens the case that fibronectin is part of the APOE ε4-related vascular disease process," says Badri Vardarajan, associate professor of neurological science in the Department of Neurology, Gertrude H. Sergievsky Center, and Taub Institute, who collaborated on this discovery of the FN1 mutation.

A Rare Genetic Defense Could Inspire New Alzheimer's Treatments

The potential implications are significant because APOE ε4 is the strongest common genetic risk factor for Alzheimer's disease. Approximately one in five people carries at least one copy of this gene variant.

Although carrying APOE ε4 does not guarantee that someone will develop Alzheimer's, it increases their likelihood of developing the disease. Understanding why certain carriers remain unaffected could reveal new opportunities for prevention and treatment.

The latest findings suggest several possible approaches.

One strategy would be to prevent harmful amounts of fibronectin from accumulating around the brain's blood vessels. Another would involve blocking the abnormal cellular signals triggered by excess fibronectin. A third possibility would be to restore the growth factor signaling that allows astrocytes and blood vessel cells to maintain the blood-brain barrier.

Researchers are already investigating these possibilities through laboratory experiments.

However, translating the discovery into a treatment will require considerable care. Fibronectin performs essential functions throughout the body, including supporting tissue structure and helping damaged tissues heal. Any future therapy would therefore need to target its harmful accumulation or effects without disrupting these beneficial activities.

By identifying the molecular mechanisms involved, scientists now have a more precise framework for designing such treatments. The findings may also help researchers determine when interventions would be most effective and how to measure whether they are successfully protecting the brain.

"By showing how fibronectin contributes to early vascular damage, this study gives us new therapeutic targets and potential ways to measure whether treatments are working," says Richard Mayeux, chair of the Department of Neurology and a collaborator on the project. "Clinically, it connects a major genetic risk factor for Alzheimer's to an early disease process that may be possible to modify."

Although these potential therapies remain experimental, the discovery offers a promising new direction in Alzheimer's research. Rather than focusing only on the damage that develops as the disease progresses, scientists may eventually be able to reproduce a natural genetic defense that helps protect vulnerable brains before significant damage occurs.

#328. Something strange is happening beneath a volcano near 2 million people

A volcano famous for its glowing lava lake is hiding a surprisingly complex system of magma deep underground. Scientists at Penn State have uncovered evidence of two previously unrecognized magma sources beneath Nicaragua's active Masaya volcano, raising new questions about how molten rock moves beneath the surface.

The discovery is particularly important because approximately 2 million people live within 13 miles of the volcano, which is also a popular tourist destination. Understanding its underground activity could help scientists recognize warning signs of future eruptions and better protect nearby communities.

Using six years of satellite observations, researchers tracked subtle changes in the ground around Masaya. They discovered that one part of the volcano initially sank before beginning to rise, possibly indicating that fresh magma was entering a deep reservoir. Meanwhile, the ground around its active Santiago crater and lava lake continued to sink, suggesting that a separate, shallower magma reservoir was behaving differently.

The findings, published in Geophysical Research Letters, reveal that the volcano's underground magma system may be considerably more complicated than previously understood.

"These observations help scientists better understand how magma moves beneath Masaya and may improve monitoring and eruption forecasting in the future," said lead author Lizzie Johnson, who earned her bachelor's degree in geosciences from Penn State in May.

Located near Managua, Nicaragua's capital, Masaya is a geologically complex volcano featuring multiple overlapping craters and calderas. Calderas are large depressions that generally form when the ground collapses after magma is removed from an underground chamber.

Although scientists have studied Masaya for years, the movement of magma through its underground reservoirs and passageways remains poorly understood. These systems can change over time, making it difficult to determine exactly what is happening beneath the volcano.

"Having a thorough understanding of Masaya's volcanic activity and its related hazards is critical as around 2 million people live near Masaya and the caldera is a national park and popular tourist destination," Johnson said. "Although Masaya last produced a major lava flow in 1772, its persistent degassing often produces dangerously high concentrations of sulfur dioxide volcanic gas."

Sulfur dioxide is a potentially harmful volcanic gas released as magma approaches or remains near the surface. Even without a major eruption, elevated concentrations can create dangerous conditions for visitors and surrounding communities.

Young Cheol Kim, who completed his doctorate in geosciences at Penn State this summer, explained that monitoring the volcano continuously is essential for distinguishing normal activity from changes that might signal increasing volcanic hazards.

"Doing continuous monitoring allows us to understand the baseline activity and identify see if things are changing," he said, explaining that certain shifts in volcanic behavior could indicate a period of heightened activity or a possible eruption. "And then if there is a need to warn people, we can do that in a timely manner."

Satellites Reveal Hidden Movements Beneath the Volcano

To investigate Masaya's underground activity, the researchers analyzed measurements collected by the Sentinel-1 satellite, which observes the volcano approximately once every 12 days.

The team used a technique called interferometric synthetic aperture radar (InSAR), which can detect extremely small changes in the elevation of Earth's surface. By comparing satellite measurements taken at different times, scientists can identify areas where the ground is rising or sinking.

The technique combines two important capabilities. Synthetic aperture radar sends microwave signals toward Earth's surface, allowing satellites to collect measurements even when clouds obscure the ground. Interferometry compares the returning radar signals to detect changes in distance between the satellite and the surface.

This is especially valuable in tropical regions, where frequent cloud cover can make conventional satellite imagery less reliable.

"Cloud penetration is important for volcanoes because many of them, like Masaya, are in tropical areas where there is often cloud cover," Kim explained. "And when we look at all the different satellite images across multiple years, we can see how the distance between the satellite and the ground changes, where it is uplifting or subsiding."

By examining these subtle movements over time, researchers can investigate processes happening far below the surface without directly observing the magma itself.

Two Magma Reservoirs Behaving in Different Ways

The satellite measurements revealed an intriguing pattern between 2018 and 2024. During the first part of the study, the ground within Masaya's caldera gradually subsided, meaning it moved downward. Later, the same general area began to rise.

Scientists believe this transition could reflect changes in the amount of magma stored underground. When magma drains away from a reservoir, the overlying ground can sink. When additional magma enters and increases pressure within a reservoir, the surface may rise.

The most surprising finding was that the area surrounding Santiago crater did not follow the same pattern. Instead, it continued to sink throughout the six-year study, even as the broader caldera began rising.

"When it's subsiding, that means that there's probably magma draining away from the volcano, but once the ground starts uplifting again, that can mean that there's potentially new magma moving in," Johnson said. "At the same time, the area directly around the active Santiago crater deflated over the entire study period independently of the transition to inflation of the main reservoir. This indicates that the plumbing system is more complex and could be due to decreasing magma in a second, shallower reservoir."

Together, these observations suggest that Masaya may have at least two distinct parts of its magma storage system undergoing different changes. A deeper source could be supplying additional magma to the main reservoir, while a shallower reservoir near Santiago crater may be losing magma.

This would help explain why different sections of the same volcano moved in opposite directions.

What the Discovery Means for Future Eruptions

Understanding these underground movements is important because changes in the ground can provide clues about where magma is accumulating, where it is moving, and how it might eventually reach the surface.

The researchers emphasized that their observations did not reveal any immediate danger to communities surrounding Masaya. However, identifying the volcano's normal behavior and understanding its magma supply system could make it easier to recognize unusual changes in the future.

Continued satellite observations, combined with more detailed computer models, could also help scientists better understand the connections between underground magma reservoirs and volcanic activity at the surface.

"Further long-term geodetic and modeling studies will provide a more thorough understanding and allow for effective hazard monitoring and mitigation at Masaya and other lava-lake hosting basaltic volcanoes," Johnson said.

The findings may therefore have implications beyond Nicaragua, particularly for other active volcanoes that contain persistent lava lakes and complex underground magma systems.

She completed the research as part of her Schreyer's Honors College thesis, working alongside graduate researchers, including Kim, to analyze satellite measurements and develop her scientific skills.

"I appreciated learning how a research group works," said Johnson, who has recently started a doctoral program in Earth sciences at the University of Hawaii at Manoa, where she will continue studying volcanoes using seismic waves to image below the surface of Kilauea volcano. "I had never had any experience with research, so it was great to be able to join in the group meetings and get to hear about everybody's work. Real applications must also consider memory consumption, cache invalidation, and whether the inputs fully represent the data used by the calculation. Unbounded caches can become a performance problem themselves.

After making changes, developers should repeat the original performance measurements under comparable conditions.

Chrome DevTools Performance: Investigate JavaScript execution, long tasks, layout, and rendering.

Lighthouse: Audit page performance and identify opportunities for improvement.

PageSpeed Insights: Examine field data when available and review lab diagnostics.

The browser Network panel: Inspect resource sizes, loading behavior, and request timing.

For user-facing performance, metrics such as Largest Contentful Paint (LCP), Interaction to Next Paint (INP), and Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS) help evaluate different aspects of the experience. JavaScript optimization can affect these metrics, but none of them measures JavaScript execution speed alone.

Developers should also test on representative devices and network conditions. A change that appears insignificant on a powerful development computer may matter on a lower-powered mobile device.

Effective JavaScript performance optimization starts with understanding how an application behaves under real conditions. Measuring bottlenecks, reducing unnecessary DOM work, controlling frequent events, loading optional modules on demand, and choosing appropriate algorithms can all contribute to more responsive interfaces.

The most important consideration is not whether the code looks shorter or more sophisticated. It is whether the change improves the experience without making the application unnecessarily complex.

By profiling before optimizing and validating every significant change, developers can build web applications that remain responsive, maintainable, and efficient as their features and user demands grow.
