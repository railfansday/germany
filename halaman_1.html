Approximately 40 to 70 new comets are discovered every year, most of which are found by astronomers actively looking for comets or at least new celestial bodies in general. But at least one comet discovered in 2026 doesn’t have that distinction. It was discovered entirely by accident by a telescope that was not actively looking for comets.

Say hello to C/2026 S1 (Euclid), a brand new comet that was just discovered by the European Space Agency’s Euclid space telescope. The comet is located about 1.3 billion kilometers away from Earth, which is roughly the distance between the sun and the orbits of Jupiter and Saturn. It was discovered by Mikko Pöntinen of the University of Helsinki, together with the Solar System Objects Science Working Group, who were using the Euclid telescope at the time of discovery.

What makes this one weird, among the thousands of other comets humans have named, is how they found it. The group was intentionally pointing the telescope away from the elliptical plane, a band in the sky where the solar system’s planets, most of its asteroids and a significant number of its comets reside. The idea was to avoid the cosmic dust and debris left behind by those celestial bodies, allowing the group to take clearer pictures of distant galaxies in deep space.

While doing this, a comet zipped through. It could have gone entirely unnoticed, but Pöntinen had written software to identify moving objects, which flagged images where the comet appeared. Pöntinen was then able to use its speed and trajectory to find it in other images and report it as a new comet.

“I was excited to see my software flag a ‘streak’ smeared across several images,” Pöntinen said in a statement. “Further inspection revealed that it had a fuzzy cloud around it, indicative of an active object like a comet that is shedding dust and gas.”

The unexpected comet was reported to the International Astronomical Union’s Minor Planet Center, where ground-based astronomers confirmed its existence and gave it a name.

The most interesting part of C/2026 S1 (Euclid) is undoubtedly that it was discovered by a telescope that wasn’t designed to find comets on a mission that also wasn’t designed to find comets.

According to Bruno Altieri, archive scientist and member of the Euclid solar system team, this is because of the way the Euclid telescope works. It spends most of its time in a wide survey that will eventually map about one-third of the extragalactic sky, which encompasses all the planets, radiation clouds, debris clouds and other structures outside of the Milky Way galaxy.

It also employs a “step-and-stare” method of mapping the cosmos, which means it “steps” to a spot, stares at the sky for about an hour and then moves on. That means much of what it looks at is only looked at once. Since comets are often found thanks to their movement, it’s hard to identify such an object with this observation method.

Euclid also conducts a deep field survey. These are zoomed-in glimpses of specific parts of space conducted periodically around the schedule of the wide survey. These parts are revisited only a handful of times per year, and that’s the only reason C/2026 S1 (Euclid) was discovered.

“This discovery is indeed a happy coincidence as few comets are expected to be discovered by Euclid,” Altieri said.

Very little is known about C/2026 S1 (Euclid) so far, as it’s only been imaged a few times. There are a few things that astronomers have already gleaned from what they have seen. For starters, this is a long-period comet, which means its orbit around the sun lasts more than 200 years. The exact time hasn’t been calculated, but early estimates put it at thousands of years before it completes a full orbit.

According to Altieri, that means it almost certainly came from the Oort Cloud, which is a shell of icy bodies, rocky planetesimals and other debris that surrounds the entire solar system. Comets from the Oort Cloud are referred to as “nearly isotropic” comets and orbit the sun from all sorts of zany angles. C/2026 S1 (Euclid) is no different.

Space doesn’t have an up or down, but if it did, C/2026 S1 (Euclid) would be straight up from the sun as viewed from Earth. Its sweeping orbit takes it far above the solar system before cutting between Saturn and Jupiter on its way back down. It’s expected to reach perihelion — the point where it’s closest to the sun — in 2027. After that, it won’t be back there for thousands of years.

Your sandboxed agents just got a team. The official BAND AI kit for Docker Sandboxes is now available at docker.io/bandhq/band-python-kit:latest.

Docker Sandbox kits package everything an agent needs to run in a Docker Sandbox, so a single sbx create command gives you a ready-to-use agent environment. But an isolated agent is only half the story: real work means talking to people and to other agents. The BAND AI kit connects a sandboxed agent to the world. The agent runs on your machine with access to your repository, and joins a BAND room where it collaborates with humans and with agents running anywhere: locally, in the cloud, or on your own infrastructure. The same kit also connects sandboxes to each other, so multiple agents can work together in one room while each keeps its own isolated environment. In this post, we’ll show how a sandboxed agent connects to the world through BAND, using a local agent called Repo Doctor.

BAND provides the collaboration layer for these agents. With BAND, developers can give agents persistent identities, bring them together in shared rooms, route work through @mentions, maintain message history, and surface tool execution events in the room. Each agent brings its own model, framework, tools, workspace, and infrastructure, and joins the room as a participant, wherever it runs.

Docker Sandboxes give that agent an isolated microVM with its own kernel, filesystem, Docker daemon, and network. Inside it, the agent works with local source files, runs commands and tests, and makes changes to a mounted workspace. The BAND kit connects the two with one sbx create command.

All connectivity is outbound. The agent opens a WebSocket to BAND and posts its replies through the BAND API, so nothing needs to reach into the sandbox. From the room’s point of view, it behaves like any other participant.

#4. Let’s Use the Emergent CSS random() Function in all the Browsers

The creator of the TV show The Good Place wrote a tie-in book about moral philosophy which includes a chapter called “The Luck of the Draw,” discussing how the myth of meritocracy leads people to “underestimate the role that luck has played in their lives.” Given how God seems to play dice with the universe, there is something compelling in the way art imitates life when websites embrace controlled chaos in their designs. The jury is out on whether extreme versions of this nondeterminism such as generative UI are a helpful usage of unpredictable UX. Indeed, when I see the YouTube comments reacting to Google’s upcoming usage of GenUI in search, maybe it’s taking the idea too far down a bad path. But there is still something about the idea of a webpage that exists in a state of subtle flux each time you land on it, the same way you can’t step into the same river twice.

I’m a consultant who often works on short-term, greenfield projects, which provide me with a window into the zeitgeist and the trends companies think are the future. It’s no coincidence that the idea of randomness permeated one of my recent projects. That’s epitomized by a burst of confetti to give the user a sense of excitement when they run a random draw they configured. And like many a UI feature in the corporate world, the simple idea of confetti was subject to several revisions to make every randomized particle align with the client’s brand.

In fact, the requirements became custom enough that we ended up ditching the JavaScript plugin we were using and rolled our own confetti implementation! This illustrates the tension between the conflicting needs for chaos and control in UX, even in a fun feature like random confetti.

Wouldn’t it be nice if we could wield controlled presentational randomness in the presentation layer without leaving CSS?

If unpredictable user experiences are having a moment, it follows that CSS will do its part to make randomized layouts easy to implement. The creators of CSS have always been on a mission to harvest common UI patterns into declarative CSS standards. In keeping with that spirit, we see that in late 2025, Safari became the first browser to support the CSS random() spec, as part of an update that emphasized “letting you solve common use cases with HTML and CSS alone, paving the cowpaths, and reducing the need for JavaScript or third-party frameworks.”

Since then, cool demos and discussions of random() keep popping up. For instance, Schalk Neethling showed us how CSS random() can give us fine-grained control over the infamous confetti effect, and Alvaro Montoro made a strong argument that CSS turns out to be the most suitable language for such tasks. He points out this approach is in line with the Rule of Least Power, which encourages “solving a problem using the least powerful language capable of expressing and solving it.”

Now the bad news: half a year after Safari introduced CSS random(), there isn’t clarity on when it will land in the other browsers. At time of writing, there are signs of life that both Chrome and Firefox have been working on it, but no guarantees about when we will be able to use it outside of the Apple world, even behind a browser flag.

So, it seems currently I can only try the online demos of CSS random() on my work MacBook and not on my PC where I do my personal projects. I am tempted to write my own implementation, but the syntax is surprisingly intricate, mostly because of elaborate random caching and keying semantics, combined with the options for base values and intervals. Even if I could manage to get all those details correct, CSS random() is part of an editor’s draft spec that’s in the “early exploration phase” and “major breaking changes are expected.”

On top of that, from my dive into CSS polyfills in my article on ::nth-letter, we know the whole idea of a CSS polyfill can be a minefield.

With all these obstacles in mind, a person would have to be a special breed of crazy to attempt to polyfill CSS random().

One of the commenters on a neat YouTube demo of the feature marvelled that it’s a “feature that works ONLY IN SAFARI?!? Did the Earth get flipped upside down?” Indeed, I am more accustomed to getting my first opportunity to experience emergent features in Chrome, which means my friends on iPhones often can’t run my experiments.

And yet, in the case of random(), it’s darkly poetic that a feature based on chance appears in an unexpected place where many of us can’t use it. In fact, even Safari users may benefit from my css-random-polyfill package, because Safari updates are tied to the OS, meaning not everyone can upgrade to the latest version of the browser. Besides, we know how much Apple loves it when you hack their stuff to improve compatibility.

Jokes aside, Apple seems serious about the “hackability” and transparency of everything about the open source WebKit engine that powers Safari, and most of the demos I’ve used to test my polyfill are forks of demos from the WebKit blog, in which the Apple Safari team showed off the possibilities for CSS random() back when it was in Safari preview.

Here’s my cross-browser version of the first demo from the Safari team’s article. It’s a randomly scattered field of stars fading in and out at random intervals. The larger, four-pointed stars all tilt at the same randomly selected angle. All stars have subtle, randomly hued shadows around them.

To migrate the Safari-only original to a version that works in Chrome and Firefox, we need to change the HTML to reference my polyfill script and add the randomized marker class to all elements that we want to polyfill.

As for the CSS, unlike my :nth-letter polyfill which uses a nonstandard selector that has to be translated into valid CSS at runtime — and introduces drawbacks in the process — this time we need to support a new function in CSS instead of a new selector. It turns out the CSS we can use in this situation is technically valid, even in browsers that have never heard of CSS random(). More later on why it is valid, but for now, just notice that anywhere we want a random value, we store it in an intermediate custom property, and we always have to follow the convention that the property name starts with the prefix --random.

The spec that includes random() makes it clear that this kind of function “can be used in place of any part of any property’s value,” just like calc() or min(). So, by requiring extra ceremony and conventions, the polyfill is supporting a subset of what we will get with native random(). To see the glass half-full, it means the CSS stays compatible with the native implementation: we could delete the script reference to the polyfill once native support goes baseline and our code will still work, like it does today when it detects native support in Safari. in this case the polyfill does not process random() calls at all and it lets Safari do all the work. This is a compromise I can live with, especially if the alternative is to press our noses against the glass of Safari-only demos on YouTube and make comments such as one viewer did: “Can’t wait to use this in prod in 4 years.”

Chris Coyier said of the original starfield demo from Apple that he found it “pretty darn compelling!” I agree, and when I was testing my polyfill, that demo was fun to watch randomly twinkling, refresh and see the stars scatter differently using an emergent, declarative CSS standard. By contrast, I can’t say I have ever sat around wishing I could create a 100×100 CSS grid with randomly multicolored cells, so this example from the Safari team feels a bit like a contrived excuse to randomize something. I don’t feel like complicating the polyfill to support a CSS syntax that isn’t implemented in any browser — evidently I only give myself permission to do that once a year. The implementation of my generic --item custom CSS function takes an --index argument followed by 10 optional arguments. These could be increased to any number of arguments you think will be the realistic maximum size of a collection you would need. Each of the optional arguments is made optional by defaulting it to an empty value, so the caller of the function only needs to pass in the arguments it needs to index. Lastly, the function maps the --index to the argument at that index, because CSS custom functions do not support variable length collections of arguments the way JavaScript functions do.

Sidenote: This generic helper function is interesting, because Temani Afif has demonstrated cool use cases for being able to choose from a list of colors using an --index variable, but the solution he created was specific to the color data type and he freely admits it’s “more of a hack than a CSS feature. #5. Powering the Agent Development Lifecycle with any framework, from ADK to LangChain and beyond

End to end walkthrough of the new Agents CLI Extension System, with a worked LangChain example and a custom deploy policy

When we launched Agents CLI at Google Cloud Next '26, we built it around one strong opinion: going from an idea to a production agent on Google Cloud should take a single unified command surface. The agent framework landscape has grown more diverse in the months since. In addition to ADK, developers today pick between LangChain and LangGraph, specialized newcomers like Deep Agents, or in-house frameworks tailored to a specific business domain. Different teams prefer different frameworks, and the right choice for a greenfield prototype is rarely the right choice for a production migration. Enterprise admins may also have governance policies they want to enforce across the ADLC for all of their development teams, such as: a compliance scan, an SSO gate, a Model Risk Management hop.

Can a team benefit from the deployment, evaluation, and observability work in Agents CLI without being forced to adopt ADK, or without being forced to accept the default behavior of every command? Starting with version 1.5.0, the answer is yes. Agents CLI now ships with an Extension System that lets developers override or add commands without forking the tool. Version 1.8.0 ships the first-party LangChain extension, which replaces the framework-coupled commands with their LangChain equivalents and leaves the rest of the lifecycle untouched.

Most agent development toolchains available today share a limitation: they are opinionated about one specific framework, and the surrounding capabilities (deployment targets, CI/CD pipelines, evaluation harnesses, observability wiring) assume the developer has adopted that framework. When your framework matches the tool's assumption, the opinionated path works. When it does not, you're left with three bad options.

The first is to fork the CLI, maintaining a divergent copy and re-syncing it on every upstream release. You pay a permanent maintenance tax, and every upstream improvement has to be hand-merged into the fork. The second is to wrap the CLI behind custom scripts, building a parallel command surface that invokes the underlying tool. The problem is that developers (and their coding assistants) forget about the wrapper and call the underlying tool directly, bypassing the intended abstraction. The third is to walk away and build everything from scratch, which can consume several engineer-months of work that the team had not planned for, and which rarely produces tooling as polished as the upstream project.

The problem shows up in two scenarios. One is enterprise customers adopting Agents CLI: in regulated industries, a central platform team often requires that every deploy invocation be wrapped with a compliance check, that login route through corporate single sign-on, or that evaluations flow through a Model Risk Management pipeline. Each of these requirements, implemented today, means either forking the CLI or building a wrapper layer. The other is teams that use ADK most of the time but need to run a non-ADK framework on a specific project. Within the Agents CLI command surface, six commands assume the ADK runtime: playground, run, and four eval subcommands. The other eighteen or so (scaffold, infra, deploy, publish, login, and friends) are framework-agnostic. Without extensions, choosing a non-ADK framework for a single project meant losing access to all twenty-four.

The Extension System handles both with one mechanism.

An Agents CLI extension is a directory in a Git repository that contains a single file called agents-cli-extension.yaml. The file declares which built-in commands the extension overrides and which new commands it adds. From the official documentation:

Extensions take two shapes, described in the Using Extensions page. A command override replaces the behavior of a built-in like playground, deploy, or eval generate. The overridden command receives the user's arguments verbatim, so the user experience stays consistent with the native command. A new command exposes a workflow the CLI does not currently provide as agents-cli . Both shapes can coexist in the same extension, and most non-trivial extensions use a combination of the two.

Extensions are distributed through Git repositories rather than through a dedicated package registry. The design draws from the extension model used by the gemini-cli project. There is no central index to publish to, no package format to maintain, and no ownership roster to apply for. Any directory inside any Git repository can serve as an extension, and Git handles the versioning, pinning, and distribution. For enterprise platform teams, this means creating an internal platform-extensions repository and pointing their developers at it, with no external registry to coordinate with.

The # syntax lets a single Git repository hold multiple extensions, with the fragment identifier selecting one of them. Enterprise platform repositories often bundle together a family of related extensions (one for deploy, one for login, one for scaffold create) under a single repo with shared ownership and release cadence.

Extensions install at two scopes. The default is the project scope, where the extension and its lockfile entry are committed to the project's own repository. Teammates and CI pipelines pick it up on checkout. The alternative is the user scope, enabled via the --global flag, where the extension is stored in the user's home configuration directory and applies to every project on the machine. The user scope is the one you reach for when an extension needs to be available before any project exists, such as a framework bootstrap extension that configures agents-cli create itself.

When both scopes define the same command, the project scope wins, and agents-cli info surfaces the source of each active command. Scoping is deliberately explicit: the Extension System will not silently merge or override extensions, and any conflict surfaces to the developer at install time or in the extension list output.

The Extension System follows the discipline of package managers like uv or npm: it resolves a Git reference to an exact commit SHA at install time, records the resolved SHA in a lockfile, and uses the lockfile to re-materialize the exact reviewed code on every fresh checkout. The agents-cli install command restores the vendored working copy from the pinned SHA without advancing the pin, which guarantees that CI and local development run bit-for-bit identical code. To advance a pin to the latest commit on the tracked reference, the developer runs agents-cli extension update, which re-resolves the reference and re-prompts for third-party trust. The complete lifecycle is in the Using Extensions → Lifecycle section.

Every frontend developer will eventually stare at a CORS error in the browser console, watching an API call fail for reasons that seem opaque. A CORS error fix requires understanding what the browser is actually telling you, matching that specific message to its root cause, and applying a targeted, security-conscious server configuration.

The scheme, host, and port together define an "origin." https://example.com and http://example.com are different origins (different schemes), as are https://example.com and https://api.example.com (different hosts). https://example.com and https://example.com:443 are the same origin because port 443 is the default for HTTPS, and browsers omit it in origin comparisons. The browser enforces the same-origin policy on client-side JavaScript reading responses, not on sending requests. This distinction trips up many developers: the server may still receive and fully process the request; the browser simply refuses to expose the response to the calling code. The request lands, the database row gets written, and the browser throws an error anyway. The policy exists to prevent a malicious page from silently reading sensitive data from another origin using the user's ambient credentials.

Cross-Origin Resource Sharing (CORS) is a controlled opt-in mechanism via HTTP response headers. The server signals to the browser that a specific foreign origin is permitted to read its responses. Four headers control the mechanism: Access-Control-Allow-Origin specifies which origin is allowed, and Access-Control-Allow-Methods declares which HTTP methods are permitted. Access-Control-Allow-Headers lists which custom or non-simple headers the client may send, while Access-Control-Allow-Credentials indicates whether the browser should expose the response to credentialed requests.

The browser does not treat all cross-origin requests equally. "Simple" requests use GET, HEAD, or POST with a Content-Type of application/x-www-form-urlencoded, multipart/form-data, or text/plain, and no custom headers. A POST with Content-Type: application/json is not a simple request and triggers a preflight.

Simple requests go directly to the server, and the browser evaluates the CORS headers on the response. Anything else triggers a preflight request: an OPTIONS call the browser sends automatically before the actual request, asking the server whether the real request would be acceptable. If the OPTIONS response lacks the proper CORS headers, the browser never sends the real request at all.

A typical Chrome DevTools error reads: Access to fetch at 'https://api.example.com/data' from origin 'https://app.example.com' has been blocked by CORS policy: No 'Access-Control-Allow-Origin' header is present on the requested resource.

Two origins appear in this message. The URL after "fetch at" is the target server, the resource the code tried to reach. The value after "from origin" is the page's own origin, the one making the request. Chrome is stating that the target server's response did not include an Access-Control-Allow-Origin header that matches the requesting origin, so the browser blocked the response from reaching JavaScript.

The most common variant is the missing header: No 'Access-Control-Allow-Origin' header is present on the requested resource. The server simply did not include the header at all.

When the console says Response to preflight request doesn't pass access control check, the server either did not respond to the OPTIONS request correctly or returned a non-2xx status code for it.

Redirect variant: Redirect is not allowed for a preflight request. Developers commonly misdiagnose this one. It occurs when the URL triggers a redirect before reaching the application handler. Typical culprits include HTTP-to-HTTPS upgrades, trailing-slash normalization (e.g., /api/data redirecting to /api/data/), or a load balancer rewriting URLs. The browser's preflight OPTIONS request follows the redirect, and the Fetch specification (§4.8) forbids following redirects on preflight requests, so the entire flow fails. Fix the URL so no redirect occurs.

Credential variant: The value of the 'Access-Control-Allow-Origin' header must not be the wildcard '*' when the request's credentials mode is 'include'. When fetch is called with credentials: 'include' or XMLHttpRequest sets withCredentials = true, the server must respond with the exact requesting origin, not *. The browser will block JavaScript access to the response if a wildcard is used with credentials.

The fix is straightforward: ensure trailing slashes match between location and proxy_pass, and place any redirect rules after or outside the API location block so they do not intercept OPTIONS requests.

Include Vary: Origin on every response where Access-Control-Allow-Origin is set conditionally. Without it, CDN or proxy caches may serve a CORS response cached for one origin to a different origin, causing intermittent failures.

#13. Unifying Edge AI: How Coral NPU’s open standard brings portable, energy-viable intelligence from model to silicon

Some of the most compelling use cases for AI are happening directly on the devices around us. You see it first in personal devices like wearables that need to understand context in real time, and increasingly in enterprise and industrial systems like autonomous robots and smart sensors. These experiences require low latency, strict privacy, and all-day reliability that cannot depend on a constant round-trip to the cloud.

Edge AI complements the cloud to bring the best AI experiences. These rely on a continuous flow of context and intelligence across both, working as a model cascade where information is captured and processed in the right place at the right time. Ultra-low-power models on the edge can continuously sense and respond in real time, while seamlessly handing off to larger on-device or cloud models when deeper reasoning is needed.

Still, building for the edge is much harder than it should be. While cloud infrastructure has converged around a few standard software stacks, the edge is split across hundreds of proprietary chip architectures and incompatible toolchains. For ML developers, this creates a heavy fragmentation tax, where you end up rewriting kernels, re-quantizing models, and debugging vendor-specific compilers for every chip you target.

Last year, Google Research introduced Coral NPU with a simple idea: an open hardware and software standard is the only way to unify the edge and unlock developer productivity and portability. Our standards based, ML-first RISC-V architecture paired with a reliable open-source toolchain gives developers confidence that their models will compile and run, letting teams optimize a model once and deploy it across different SoC vendors, devices, and power envelopes.

Today, we are reaching three major milestones in the Coral effort:

Fixing fragmentation at the edge takes more than publishing an instruction set. Historically, many edge accelerators were designed to hit high peak-compute numbers on spec sheets, leaving compiler teams to figure out software support later. In practice, unsupported neural network layers fall back to a general-purpose CPU, which can hurt latency and drain the battery.

We designed the Coral NPU architecture and its compiler together from the start. Instead of chasing peak metrics, our focus is on energy-viable compute: helping to deliver the right performance at the right power envelope for your application.

Over the past year, we released the first three milestones of the 32-bit Coral NPU design: a C-programmable RISC-V scalar frontend for control flow (Milestone 1), an RVV v1.0 vector co-processor for SIMD operations (Milestone 2), and a tightly coupled memory subsystem with floating point support (Milestone 3). With this month's release of the Matrix Execution Unit (Milestone 4), the 32-bit architecture is complete.

Scalar and vector units are great for general control logic and signal processing, but modern ML workloads—from vision encoders to transformer attention layers—spend most of their time on 2D matrix-matrix (GEMM) and matrix-vector (GEMV) multiplication. Running 2D matrix math on a 1D vector engine forces the chip to repeatedly load weights and activations from memory, which can mean spending more energy moving data than doing math.

Our Matrix Execution Unit leverages the RISC-V VME specification (formally part of the Zvt family) to address this need and uses a dedicated outer-product multiply-accumulate (MAC) engine. Because an outer-product engine computes a full 2D tile of operations in a single cycle (256 MACs per cycle), it reuses weights and activations directly inside local registers. This brings quadratic throughput scaling to the core, configurable for up to 1 TOPS.

Having a dedicated matrix engine in a sub-10 mW envelope changes what developers can build on a small battery. Rather than stopping at simple keyword spotting or basic motion detection, devices can run continuous, multimodal workloads, including compact transformer models like Gemma, real-time voice translation on wearables, spatial audio filtering on hearables, and low-latency visual perception on robots.

To help silicon teams bring this open design straight into commercial production, the complete 32-bit RTL is available today on GitHub.

To make the hardware easy to target without writing custom assembly, we are also releasing the v1.0 foundation of our open-source MLIR/IREE compiler toolchain.

With this stack, you can bring models from JAX, PyTorch, or TensorFlow through standard representations like StableHLO and TOSA. The compiler partitions workloads between the host CPU and the Coral NPU core, handles operator fusion, tiles matrices to fit local SRAM and matrix accumulators, and progressively lowers workloads directly to Coral NPU scalar, RVV v1.0 vector, and VME matrix instructions. Alongside our open-source MPACT simulator and reference FPGA design, hardware and software teams can benchmark models and tune silicon configurations before a chip ever goes to fabrication.
