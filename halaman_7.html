Forms are among the most important components of modern websites. They allow users to register accounts, submit contact requests, search for information, complete purchases, and interact with web applications.

Despite their apparent simplicity, forms can introduce significant usability and development challenges. Poorly labeled fields, unclear validation messages, inaccessible controls, and unreliable submission behavior can prevent users from completing even basic tasks.

Modern HTML provides many built-in features that help developers create more reliable forms with less JavaScript. When combined with semantic markup, thoughtful CSS, and carefully designed validation, these features can improve both usability and maintainability.

This article explores practical techniques for building modern web forms that are accessible, responsive, and easier to maintain.

A well-structured form begins with the correct HTML elements. Native controls provide built-in browser behavior and communicate their purpose to assistive technologies.

Each control has an explicitly associated label. The name attributes identify the fields when form data is submitted, while autocomplete helps browsers recognize common information types.

The required attribute indicates that a field must be completed before a normal browser submission can proceed.

These native features reduce the need for custom code and provide a more consistent starting point across browsers.

HTML provides several input types designed for specific kinds of information.

For example, type="email" enables browser validation for email-shaped input, while type="tel" identifies a telephone number field and can prompt mobile browsers to display a suitable keyboard.

Selecting appropriate input types improves the user experience, especially on mobile devices.

However, native validation has limits. An email input does not establish that an address exists or belongs to the person entering it. Similarly, a URL input does not guarantee that a submitted address is safe to access.

Client-side validation should therefore be treated as a usability feature, not as a substitute for server-side validation.

Browser controls can look different across platforms. A small set of reusable CSS rules can make a form feel more consistent while preserving native functionality.

The box-sizing: border-box declaration makes width calculations more predictable because padding and borders are included in the declared width.

Using font: inherit helps form controls remain consistent with the surrounding typography.

The visible focus outline is equally important. Keyboard users need to identify which control currently receives focus, so removing browser outlines without an accessible replacement should be avoided.

For operations that must not be repeated, server-side idempotency controls may be necessary. Disabling a button alone cannot guarantee that a request is processed only once.

Forms frequently collect personal or business information, so security must be part of their design.

Validate all submitted data on the server.

Apply appropriate authorization checks to sensitive operations.

Use CSRF protection where required by the application's authentication architecture.

Escape or safely render user-provided content to reduce injection risks.

Avoid placing passwords, tokens, or sensitive personal information in URLs.

Apply appropriate rate limits to public-facing endpoints.

HTML validation helps users correct mistakes, but it does not establish trust in submitted data.

For example, a hidden input containing a user's role must never be treated as authoritative proof of that role. A malicious client can change the value before submission.

Security decisions belong on the server, where the application can verify permissions and enforce its rules.

Developers should test forms at narrow widths and with browser zoom enabled. Long labels, validation messages, and translated content should be allowed to wrap naturally.

It is also worth checking that touch targets are comfortable to use and that the form does not depend on hover effects to communicate important information.

A form that works when every field contains valid data may still fail under realistic conditions.

Verifying that the server rejects invalid or unauthorized requests.

Confirming that successful submissions provide clear feedback.

Automated browser tests can cover common validation and submission scenarios. Manual testing remains valuable for checking whether error messages are understandable and whether the form feels usable to people with different accessibility needs.

The most reliable forms are designed to handle failure clearly, not just to demonstrate a successful submission.

Modern HTML provides a strong foundation for building accessible and maintainable forms. Semantic elements, appropriate input types, native validation, and reusable CSS can reduce unnecessary JavaScript while improving the user experience.

When custom validation or asynchronous submission is required, developers should add it carefully and preserve clear feedback, keyboard support, and server-side security controls.

A well-designed form does more than collect information. It guides users through a task, helps them recover from mistakes, and communicates what happens next. By treating usability, accessibility, and security as connected parts of the same design process, developers can build forms that are more reliable and easier to maintain.

Pennsylvania businesses and nonprofits have reached the deadline to remove skill-game terminals after the state Supreme Court classified the machines as slot machines under state law and lawmakers failed to agree on a regulatory system.

The court’s June ruling said the games are unlawful unless licensed. It paused enforcement for 120 days to give lawmakers time to act, but businesses were required to remove the machines by Tuesday, October 13; enforcement was set to begin Wednesday, October 14, according to CDC Gaming.

The Supreme Court declined requests to extend the deadline, CDC Gaming reported. With the legislative window closed, an estimated 70,000 terminals across Pennsylvania were set to become officially illegal after October 13.

Law-enforcement agencies have said they will crack down on businesses hosting the machines beyond the deadline. The Pennsylvania State Police warned that terminals still operating on October 14 could be seized immediately and that business owners may face criminal prosecution.

The deadline follows roughly a decade in which skill-game terminals operated without a comprehensive statewide regulatory system. The 120-day stay offered lawmakers time to establish one, but they did not reach an agreement before the court’s deadline. ReadWrite has previously reported on the Supreme Court’s classification of skill games as slot machines and the consequences for operators.

Pace-O-Matic, which develops skill-game software and terminals, said it is complying with the law and has urged operators and locations to make sure the games are no longer available to play. The company also warned that lost machine revenue would affect small businesses and organizations including American Legions, VFWs, volunteer fire companies and emergency-service groups.

Joe Rocca, owner of Pittsburgh-based Rite Touch Distribution, said removing the terminals threatens his business. He expects to have 1,000 machines left after removals are complete; he said almost all of his company’s business involved leasing terminals in Pennsylvania or selling them to distributors who leased them to local businesses and nonprofits.

Penn Entertainment welcomed the end of what it characterized as an illegal gaming industry, citing concerns about responsible-gaming practices, consumer protections and the absence of tax revenue for the state. Casinos and the American Gaming Association had argued that the unregulated machines sidestepped legal and regulatory requirements.

Supporters of skill games, by contrast, say the terminals provide an important source of additional income for small businesses, bars, taverns and nonprofits. Rocca supported regulation but objected to machines being placed in settings such as hair salons and mini-casinos.

The Independent Fiscal Office estimates that each terminal can generate $47,000 a year, underlining the potential scale of the revenue businesses and community groups stand to lose. Some may turn to pull-tab machines or seek to become Pennsylvania Lottery retailers. Lottery spokeswoman Ewa Swope said the lottery has more than 9,900 retailers statewide and expects interest from players and businesses as skill games leave the market.

Two stalled measures in Harrisburg, House Bill 2213 and Senate Bill 1079, would set a $500 monthly fee for each terminal and cap the statewide total at 50,000. Their proposed framework was estimated to generate $300 million in annual state tax revenue.

A separate proposal in Democratic Gov. Josh Shapiro’s budget would have allowed licensed establishments to host up to five machines, taxed gross revenue at 52% and raised an estimated $487 million in fiscal year 2026-2027, according to the Independent Fiscal Office. That plan also failed to secure agreement. The proposals remain a possible legislative route, but no replacement regulatory system was in place when enforcement began; lawmakers’ proposed skill-game rules have not resolved the immediate operating question for businesses with machines still on site.

#142. The Best Hearing Aids I’ve Tested (2026): Jabra, Resound, Apple, and More

Hearing-challenged individuals have long had one option for dealing with this widespread issue: an expensive, bulky hearing aid prescribed by a doctor. That changed in 2022 when the US Food and Drug Administration approved over-the-counter hearing aid devices, giving consumers access to a vast and growing array of alternatives. Today, there are more hearing aids than ever, and they come in different shapes, sizes, and most importantly, prices. How does a $100 hearing aid compare to a $4,000 prescription device for treating mild to moderate hearing loss? I’ve been testing products for the past four years to answer that question.

I’ve performed hands-on (ears-on?) testing of more than 65 hearing aid models around my home, outside, and in noisy environments. I’ve found plenty to like on the market today—and even more aids to avoid. The Jabra Enhance Select M3 holds the crown as the best over-the-counter hearing aid, while those seeking a prescription device should seek out a provider to fit them with a pair of Resound Sensia hearing aids. For those just getting started with hearing tech, I almost universally recommend giving Apple’s AirPods Pro 3 earbuds a try. They’re an intuitive way to see how your hearing can improve without breaking the bank.

Below is my complete roundup of the best prescription and over-the-counter hearing aids I’ve tested and approved.

Updated October 2026: I've updated my picks with the Jabra Enhance Select M3, Resound Sensia 9, Oticon Zeal, and Legato Harmony Glasses.

Every person is unique, and so are their hearing loss needs. Consider these factors to find a device that suits your needs and lifestyle. For more tips, check out our How to Buy a Hearing Aid guide.

Hearing aids shouldn’t bankrupt you. With such a steep price and minimal insurance coverage, it’s no wonder that so many people put off hearing loss treatment. Between audiologist visits, hearing aid orders, custom fittings, and maintenance, it’s justifiable that you might postpone treatment rather than fork over your credit card. It’s vital you know your budget before you start browsing for hearing aids. Many brands offer trial periods where you can test out the devices for a set number of days and, if they're a bad fit, return them free of charge.

It doesn’t have to be obvious that you’re wearing a hearing aid. Despite cartoonish media depictions of hunched-over seniors screaming “WHAT?!” at every person who tries to talk to them, hearing loss doesn’t discriminate by age. With nearly 10 million Americans under the age of 60 affected, more should be taking advantage of the sleekly designed hearing aids on the market. Similar to the stigmas that keep people from seeking mental health treatment, the hearing-loss community is plagued by self-consciousness when it comes to wearing these devices. Modern in-the-ear (ITE) hearing aids have come a long way in subtlety.

Opt for a professional hearing test over an online one. While online and app-based tests are a good way to dip your toes in and see whether you have hearing loss, a detailed audiogram that only an in-office visit can provide is a far better option. A hearing professional can use this audiogram to calibrate hearing aids to your specific needs.

What Are the Different Types of Hearing Aids?

Before you choose which hearing aid is right for you, it’s best to familiarize yourself with the different styles on the market. While you may see multiple design styles, hearing aids essentially boil down to two main varieties: behind-the-ear and in-the-ear. Completely in-canal (CIC) devices also exist, but none are available over the counter. Here are common terms you’ll see:

Behind-the-ear (BTE) hearing aids, also known as over-the-ear, are probably the most traditional style. For these devices, an often bulky plastic case rests on the back of your ear while a tube delivers sound to an ear mold. Because they’re bigger, they tend to have the most technology and can cater to higher degrees of hearing loss. That said, they also tend to stand out more, which is a drawback for those looking for subtlety. Receiver-in-the-ear (RITE) hearing aids are essentially the same as behind-the-ear devices, with a receiver component that sits in the ear canal instead of being built into the case. Though the industry likes to tout them as a sleeker, less noticeable counterpart, in actuality, they’re not much smaller than BTEs and function identically.

In-the-ear (ITE) hearing aids, also known as in-the-canal (ITC), are more comparable to wireless earbuds from a design standpoint. These devices allow for directional mics but also tend to collect moisture and wax.

Completely in-canal (CIC) hearing aids are the most “invisible” hearing aids on the market. This is because, as the name suggests, they’re completely submerged in your ear canal with nothing sticking out (besides a tiny string for removal). They typically have to be inserted by a professional and left in the ear for longer periods, and as noted, they are not available over the counter.

Traditional hearing aids must be “fitted”—not just physically fitted to your ear anatomy but acoustically fitted to line up with the particulars of your hearing loss. This is because for most people, hearing loss is not unilateral. Some may experience trouble hearing low frequencies, while others may struggle with higher ones. (Mine has a dip somewhere in the middle.) By tuning the hearing aid to boost the frequency bands where your hearing is at its weakest, a precision hearing aid can improve your overall hearing quality without blowing out the frequencies where your ears are already working well.

With self-fitting hearing aids, you do this tuning yourself, at home. Typically, an app will walk you through a simple hearing test, and the results of that test are used to tune, or fit, the frequencies as discussed above. This is basically the same process a doctor will undertake in fitting a prescription hearing aid, only in a simpler and more automated manner.

Conversely, preset hearing aids can’t be tuned in this fashion. These considerably less expensive aids tend to boost all frequencies universally, though most will at least include a slider letting you prefer bass versus treble or vice versa. As a result, the improvement offered by a preset hearing aid will necessarily be less nuanced than that provided by a self-fitted aid, though if you have relatively mild hearing loss, the difference may be less marked than you think.

In the beginning, replaceable (or disposable) batteries were the only option for hearing aids. These batteries are tiny cells, smaller than a pencil eraser, which you replace with a new one after they die. Hearing aid batteries can last for 70 hours or more, so you'd typically replace them once every week or two, depending on use. The longer lifespan means that if you are often away from a power outlet (or forget to charge your aids), you can worry less about running out of juice. (However, you will have to remember to carry spare batteries with you.) Hearing aids with replaceable batteries can also be (but aren’t always) smaller and cheaper, but the batteries are difficult to work with. Even if you have steady hands, it can be difficult to get a tiny battery in and out. These batteries are easy to lose and represent a serious health hazard if children or pets eat them. There’s also a small cost consideration: spare batteries aren’t free.

Rechargeable hearing aids use lithium-ion batteries and recharge via a case, much like standard Bluetooth earbuds. Battery life can vary widely, from over 24 hours to less than eight. That said, for most users, if the case is kept plugged in and the hearing aids are dropped into the case anytime they aren’t in use, they should never have to worry about a dead battery. The case itself also has a battery; it can supply an extra three to seven full recharges to the hearing aids before the case battery dies. Again, this varies widely. Rechargeable aids are much more commonplace today and reflect the most up-to-date technology. You’ll have far more options if you go for a rechargeable model.

Jabra revamped its entire hearing aid lineup in 2026, which means that none of our previous picks are available on the market any more. No worry, as Jabra has replaced the line with a trio of new devices, dubbed M1, M2, and M3. Each is more sophisticated than the last, and more expensive too.

All three are excellent products, and users who need simple assistance around the house or while doing errands will get along fine with either the M1 ($1,195) or M2 ($1,695). If you find yourself regularly struggling with hearing in crowded or noisy environments, the M3 should be your pick. This 2.66-gram device features Jabra’s most advanced hearing improvement algorithms (all part of its SoundScape technology suite), including tech that is designed to “lock on” to a speaker, even when they’re in motion. The system works well, and it can make a real difference when you’re trying to understand what people are saying at a busy cocktail party or conference.

If you need immersive streaming features, Jabra’s hearing aids won’t get the job done, though they can at least do double duty for streaming the occasional phone call or podcast when you’re on the go. Battery life of 26 hours is outstanding, and Jabra’s (massive) case offers three full top-ups.

There’s a fairly open secret in the hearing aid world: Almost every prescription hearing aid producer also makes an over-the-counter model (or licenses it to another brand, as is the case with producers like Sony). GN Hearing produces the hearing technology that Jabra sells. In prescription form, you’ll find its hearing products marketed as Resound.

The just-released Sensia is stuffed with technology that Jabra doesn’t get, including a four-microphone directional beam former designed to better process incoming audio from every direction and algorithmic updates that analyze the environment in real time to better handle noise regardless of the environment. That all sounds like a lot of hokum, but I was able to compare the Sensia hearing aids to Jabra’s M3 side by side, and the difference was surprisingly obvious. With the Sensia hearing aids, I discovered what was perhaps the crispiest and most vibrant audio experience I’ve encountered to date, with absolutely no hiss, even at the devices’ highest volume level. With the Jabra M3, some level of hiss starts to creep in at as low as a 35 percent volume level.

Of course, the hearing aids don’t exactly shake things up from a form factor standpoint. They are functionally identical in appearance to the Jabra M3, though they do shave one-twentieth of a gram off the weight, bringing it down to 2.61 grams per unit—though this comes at the cost of some iffy Bluetooth stability. Battery life at around 20 hours (based on my testing) is no surprise; the devices even use the same case as the Jabra hearing aids, with three recharges specified.

Tragically, Resound is one of the least transparent manufacturers in the industry when it comes to pricing. The company actually referred me to “online research” to determine an estimated cost, which can be anywhere from $2,500 to $7,000. Still, I can’t argue with Sensia’s performance. My simple advice: Shop around until you find a reseller who charges something on the low end of that range.

When Sony discontinued its hearing aid partnership with WS Audiology earlier this year, it left a gaping hole in the market for high-quality, completely in-the-ear hearing aids. At present, while there are plenty of invisible hearing aids available over the counter, there are none that I can comfortably recommend. The good news is that a few excellent prescription models remain available. The bad news is that they may well be unattainably expensive.

At the head of the class is Oticon’s Zeal, a hearing aid model so small that each miniature earbud weighs just 1.68 grams, the smallest and lightest prescription hearing aid I’ve ever tested—and it’s not even close. The units’ oddball design is largely responsible for their minuscule size, featuring a snake-like neck that worms its way close to the eardrum. A lengthy wire that sticks out the backside of each device does triple duty as a retrieval wire, a retention lock, and an antenna to help the hearing aids communicate better with the Oticon app on your phone.

The unit takes some tuning, so plan to spend quite a while in your audiologist’s office, but the results speak for themselves. My test Zeals offered just the right level of audio boost where I needed it—with no frequencies blown out and no hiss to speak of, no matter what environment I found myself in. Oticon’s app is also idiot-proof, which is good, because the units themselves have no physical controls (for obvious reasons).

Pricing is a whole other consideration. Oticon quotes $5,000 to $8,000 per pair for these units, which puts them near or at the very top of the market. That alone may well have you rethinking how important your vanity is when you can buy a solid behind-the-ear alternative for a quarter of the price.

Apple’s 2024 entry to the hearing aid world had an amazing impact on democratizing access to hearing aid technology, and for the past few years, I’ve recommended to just about everyone that the best way to get started thinking about hearing health was to pick up a pair of AirPods Pro 3. Apple’s improvements to its hearing aid tech may not be the main event, but the upgrades that surround its hearing aid features are more than enough to make this offering the new king of the hill in the hearing aid/earbuds combo space, especially at this price.

Little has changed with the way hearing improvement works: Take the hearing aid test built into iOS or upload a professional audiogram—you can import your previous settings if you already have a pair of AirPods Pro 2. Hearing aid features are designed for use in Transparency mode, but you can also activate the Media Assist feature to have your hearing aid settings applied to whatever you’re streaming in other listening modes.

While Apple has clearly upgraded the way audio is processed to eliminate the background hiss present in the AirPods Pro 2, that’s possibly less impactful than its improvements to active noise cancellation (ANC), which is now one of the best on the market. When streaming media with ANC turned on while in a noisy gym, I could barely detect anything happening in the world around me, and with Media Assist active, music never sounded so immersive and alive, even while getting my cardio in. The battery life boost to 8 to 10 hours is also hugely meaningful to hearing aid users. It’s not “all day” battery life, but it’s much better than the 6 hours the AirPods Pro 2 offered.

EssilorLuxottica created this category with the launch of its Nuance Audio Glasses last year. Now it has competition, and while Nuance was a solid first-gen product, Legato has one-upped the competition with this eponymous offering, available in three styles ($999 to $1,099, without prescription lenses).

The concept behind hearing glasses remains the same: These are for people who need a gentle level of hearing assistance and who, for whatever reason, can’t or won’t wear hearing aids. That’s not a small market: 80 percent of adults over 55 who need hearing aids do not wear them. And while most of those people will never be convinced to shove something in their ear canal so they can hear a little better, many are likely to be swayed by an extra set of open-air speakers hovering at the sides of their head.

Legato’s hearing glasses are minimally tunable—there are only two simple hearing profiles available, though you can slightly tweak the volume of your own voice—but they are surprisingly effective at boosting sound, particularly conversation, a task for which they are specifically tuned. Hardware controls are easy to use, and Legato’s app couldn’t be simpler, either. Best of all is how Legato has dramatically redesigned its frames. While Nuance’s frames feature a wire running from one arm to another, through the hinges and across the bridge, Legato embeds its technology in two separate pieces, one in each arm, with nothing up front. This cuts a solid 5 grams off the weight of the glasses, and more importantly, shifts the weight backwards and off the nose. As a result, they’re far more comfortable for long-term wear.

JLab has been a player in the earbud space for years, and with the Hear (8/10, WIRED Recommends), it launches its first hearing aid product—a pair of earbuds that do double duty for both improving your listening experience and streaming media from your mobile device. The earbuds are available in black, white, or beige, and while the 4.94-gram weight of each unit isn’t insignificant, they are reasonably comfortable even for moderate-term wear.

You can’t upload an audiogram to JLab’s app, but four operational modes and additional options like noise cancellation provide a quick way to tune your experience based on your environment. Naturally, the aids particularly shine when it comes to Bluetooth streaming, and they even feature an equalizer that can help you improve things further, though why the noise cancellation feature isn’t available while listening to music is a huge mystery. At this price, though, the Hear over-delivers considerably.

#144. A massive asteroid slammed into the North Sea and unleashed a 330-foot tsunami

A massive asteroid slammed into the North Sea roughly 43 to 46 million years ago, triggering a tsunami more than 100 meters (330 feet) high. Now, after more than two decades of scientific disagreement, researchers have uncovered compelling evidence confirming the ancient collision.

The impact created what is now known as the Silverpit Crater, a massive structure buried deep beneath the seafloor. Although scientists first discovered it in 2002, its origins remained fiercely debated, with some researchers arguing that geological processes, rather than an object from space, were responsible.

A research team led by Dr. Uisdean Nicholson of Heriot-Watt University in Edinburgh has now provided the strongest evidence yet that Silverpit was formed by an asteroid or comet. By combining advanced seismic imaging, microscopic examination of rock samples, and computer simulations, the scientists have reconstructed the violent event and identified minerals that could only have formed under extraordinary pressure.

The findings, published in Nature Communications, establish Silverpit as one of the relatively few confirmed impact craters on Earth.

A Mysterious Crater Beneath the North Sea

The Silverpit Crater lies approximately 700 meters beneath the seabed in the southern North Sea, about 80 miles off the coast of Yorkshire, England. The crater itself measures roughly three kilometers across and is surrounded by a much larger, 20-kilometer-wide region of circular faults, which are fractures in the rock where movement has occurred.

When scientists first identified the formation in 2002, several features suggested that it had been created by a high-speed collision with an asteroid or comet.

Its nearly circular outline, elevated central peak, and surrounding rings of faults resembled structures found at other impact sites. Central peaks can develop when rocks compressed by a powerful collision rebound upward, somewhat like the way water rises in the center after an object splashes into a pool.

Some geologists proposed that the unusual formation resulted from the movement of underground salt deposits, which can deform the rock layers above them. Others suggested that volcanic activity had caused part of the seabed to collapse, producing a structure that only appeared to be an impact crater.

The disagreement persisted for years. In 2009, geologists even voted on the competing explanations, as documented in Geoscientist magazine. Most participants rejected the asteroid impact hypothesis.

The latest findings show that the impact explanation was correct.

Scientists Find the Smoking Gun for an Ancient Asteroid Impact

The breakthrough came from combining newly available seismic images with microscopic evidence recovered from beneath the seabed.

Seismic imaging allows geologists to investigate structures hidden underground by analyzing how sound waves travel through different layers of rock. This technique can reveal buried faults, craters, and other formations that cannot be observed directly.

Using the improved data, Nicholson and his colleagues were able to examine Silverpit's internal structure in unprecedented detail.

But the most decisive evidence came from tiny mineral crystals discovered in rock cuttings collected from an oil well near the crater.

Dr. Uisdean Nicholson, a sedimentologist in Heriot-Watt University's School of Energy, Geoscience, Infrastructure and Society, said: "New seismic imaging has given us an unprecedented look at the crater.

"Samples from an oil well in the area also revealed rare 'shocked' quartz and feldspar crystals at the same depth as the crater floor.

"We were exceptionally lucky to find these -- a real 'needle-in-a-haystack' effort. These prove the impact crater hypothesis beyond doubt, because they have a fabric that can only be created by extreme shock pressures."

Shocked minerals are particularly important because their internal crystal structures have been altered by immense pressure. Such changes can occur when an asteroid strikes at tremendous speed, generating powerful shock waves that pass through the surrounding rock.

Ordinary geological processes generally cannot produce these distinctive microscopic features. Their discovery at the same depth as the crater floor provides strong physical evidence that an extremely violent impact occurred.

A 160-Meter Asteroid Unleashed a Monster Tsunami

With the crater's origins established, researchers used numerical models, or computer simulations based on physical laws, to reconstruct what happened during the collision.

Their results suggest that an asteroid approximately 160 meters across approached from the west and struck the seabed at a shallow angle.

Although much smaller than the asteroid associated with the extinction of the dinosaurs, an object of this size could still release an enormous amount of energy when traveling at cosmic speeds.

According to the simulations, the impact violently displaced vast quantities of rock and seawater, producing a towering curtain of debris before the material collapsed back into the ocean.

Dr. Nicholson said: "Our evidence shows that a 160-meter-wide asteroid hit the seabed at a low angle from the west.

"Within minutes, it created a 1.5-kilometer-high curtain of rock and water that then collapsed into the sea, creating a tsunami over 100 meters high."

That means the collision sent material nearly a mile into the air before it fell back into the sea, generating a tsunami taller than a 30-story building.

Unlike ordinary wind-driven waves, tsunamis involve the movement of enormous volumes of water. They are typically generated when a sudden disturbance, such as an earthquake, underwater landslide, or asteroid impact, rapidly displaces the water above it.

The estimated height of Silverpit's tsunami comes from computer modeling of the ancient impact rather than direct observation. Nevertheless, the reconstruction illustrates just how destructive even a relatively modest asteroid can be when it strikes the ocean.

A Scientific Debate Finally Settled After Decades

For Professor Gareth Collins of Imperial College London, the discovery brings a satisfying conclusion to a controversy he witnessed firsthand.

Collins participated in the 2009 debate over Silverpit's origins and contributed the numerical simulations used in the new research.

Professor Collins said: "I always thought that the impact hypothesis was the simplest explanation and most consistent with the observations.

"It is very rewarding to have finally found the silver bullet. We can now get on with the exciting job of using the amazing new data to learn more about how impacts shape planets below the surface, which is really hard to do on other planets. "

The findings do more than settle an old argument. They also give scientists a valuable opportunity to study the underground damage caused by high-speed asteroid impacts.

Because much of Silverpit's structure remains buried and well preserved, researchers can investigate how the collision fractured, displaced, and reshaped the surrounding geological layers.

These observations could help scientists interpret impact structures on other worlds, where the subsurface effects of collisions are often much more difficult to examine.

Earth has experienced countless asteroid and comet impacts throughout its history, but relatively few recognizable craters remain.

Weathering, erosion, sediment deposition, and plate tectonics continually reshape the planet's surface. Over millions of years, these processes can wear down, bury, or completely destroy evidence of ancient collisions.

As a result, exceptionally preserved impact sites like Silverpit are scientifically valuable.

Dr. Nicholson said, "Silverpit is a rare and exceptionally preserved hypervelocity impact crater.

"These are rare because the Earth is such a dynamic planet -- plate tectonics and erosion destroy almost all traces of most of these events.

"Around 200 confirmed impact craters exist on land, and only about 33 have been identified beneath the ocean.

"We can use these findings to understand how asteroid impacts shaped our planet throughout history, as well as predict what could happen should we have an asteroid collision in future."

Hypervelocity impacts occur when objects strike at extremely high speeds, typically several kilometers per second. At those velocities, even an asteroid measuring only a few hundred meters across can deliver a devastating amount of energy.

With its origins now confirmed, Silverpit joins a small group of important underwater and buried impact structures, including the Chicxulub Crater in Mexico, associated with the mass extinction that wiped out the nonavian dinosaurs, and the Nadir Crater off the coast of West Africa, another recently confirmed asteroid impact site.

Together, these craters provide evidence of the powerful role cosmic collisions have played in shaping Earth's geological and biological history.

By studying Silverpit's remarkably preserved features, scientists hope to better understand what happens when an asteroid strikes the ocean, how the surrounding rock responds, and what such an event might mean for Earth in the future.

Every website is at its best the day it ships. The final branch merges, the site goes live exactly as designed, and it is briefly perfect. It will never be this good again.

Not because anything breaks. The site keeps working. But the market moves, the messaging shifts, a competitor launches something, and the careful thing you built slowly stops matching the company it represents. A year later, it is a period piece. Not broken, just behind. Every team knows this decay, and almost everyone treats it as a law of nature.

It doesn’t have to be that way. A website could keep improving after launch instead of drifting away from its best day, quietly optimizing itself while the team that built it works on something else.

Picture it working: while you sleep, an agent catches that last week’s design-system change never propagated to the pricing page, and fixes it. Another finds a set of images shipped uncompressed in a rushed release and optimizes them. A third flags an accessibility regression a new component introduced, and either fixes it or leaves it for you to check. You wake up to a site that is measurably better than the one you left, and a short list of the few decisions the agents wanted your eyes on. That is the version worth wanting.

And the moment you take that promise seriously, you run into a problem that has nothing to do with the technology:

The obvious move, once you have capable agents, is to hand them the whole site. Let them write, edit, optimize, and publish, and get out of the way. It sounds like the natural endpoint, and it is the first thing most people picture when they hear “autonomous website.”

It is also the thing almost nobody wants once it is real in front of them.

We learned this the way you learn most things worth knowing: by building the opposite first. Building Fimo, an autonomous website platform, we set out to make websites fully autonomous, assumed that was the goal, and then watched what people actually did with it. What they did was hesitate. Not because they distrusted the agents, but because a website has no single owner. Different parts belong to different people, and each one wants a different amount of autonomy. So the question was never whether to trust the agents. It was where to draw the line, and for whom.

Once you ask it that way, the work splits cleanly into three kinds.

Start with the largest pile, because it is bigger than people expect. Most of what keeps a website healthy is rule-bound, repetitive, and completely joyless. Keeping accessibility compliant as pages change. Propagating a design-system update once a token moves. Catching a broken meta tag, an unoptimized image, a link that rotted when a URL changed three sprints ago.

None of this is where anyone’s talent lives. Nobody was hired because of their gift for spotting a missing alt attribute. This is the work you are actively relieved to hand off, and it is the work agents are best at, because it is defined by rules rather than taste. An agent that quietly keeps this layer correct across a whole site, unattended, is not a threat to anyone’s job. It is the tedious eighty percent finally taken care of.

Naming how much of the maintenance load actually lives in this pile is what makes the whole idea of an autonomous site feel less like a leap. You are not handing over judgment. You are handing over chores.

At the other end sits the work you would not delegate at any price. It is a small pile, but it is the reason you exist.

An agent can check a new page against every rule you have given it. It can confirm the contrast passes, the heading order is right, the tokens are correct, the copy matches the style guide. What it cannot do is decide what the page should feel like, or whether the thing you are shipping is, in the taste sense, good. That judgment is exactly what you were hired for, and no amount of capability moves it off your desk.

This is the part people reach for first when they resist autonomy, and they are right to protect it. The mistake is thinking the whole site is made of this kind of work. Almost none of it is. But that small part matters more than all the rest, which is why automating everything feels so wrong.

Between the chores and the untouchable sits the part no product can settle for you, because the line runs through different places for different people.

Notice what is happening there. It isn’t that one of them is cautious and the other reckless. It is that the same task carries different amounts of judgment for each of them. For one person it is a decision; for the other it is a chore. There is no default a product could ship that would be right for both, because “right” is a function of where your value sits, not of the task itself.

This is why the control has to be per-task and per-person, and why we stopped trying to find the setting that would work for everyone. There isn’t one. There is only the line each person draws, and the tools to draw it precisely.

Once you accept that the line is personal, a toggle between “approve” and “delegate” stops being enough. Where the line sits depends on what the agent is actually doing, so the real unit of control is the agent itself.

This is where it stopped being a settings problem, and it is the shape Fimo took. You don’t pick from a fixed menu of behaviors. You compose the agents, deciding what each one is even allowed to touch. You can build one from scratch, or take one close to your needs and shape it to your own line: an accessibility agent you trust to run unattended, a content agent you keep close to anything brand-facing.

And they don’t stay fixed. They learn from their tasks and from what you teach them, so the boundary you set last month isn’t the one you’re stuck with. What you had to approve then, you can delegate now, not because you lowered your guard but because the agent earned it. The line is not a setting you configure once. It moves as trust is earned, in the direction of less work for you.

None of this means flipping a site to autonomous on day one. In practice it goes the other way. You delegate a little, watch how it does, and loosen.

And you can actually watch. Every agent’s runs, its history, its logs, and a before-and-after of what it changed are there to inspect. Trust doesn’t grow because you got used to the idea; it grows because you can see what happened and compare. The first time an agent quietly fixes something you would have missed, and you can see exactly what it did, the next delegation gets easier.

Deadlines keep you from becoming the bottleneck on what you have already handed over. If you don’t weigh in, the agent proceeds. You set the terms once, and you stop being the thing the whole site waits on.

The worry people voice first is that an agent will change something on their site without them. Turn it around: the real risk is a site that never changes at all. A frozen site doesn’t stay safe. It just falls behind, slowly, in a way nobody notices until it represents a company that no longer exists.

The point of autonomy was never to remove you from your website. It was to remove the decay.

The point of autonomy is to keep the launch-day version from being the best version, and to let you spend your judgment on the handful of things that actually deserve it, while the rest takes care of itself. Draw the line where your value is. Let the agents hold everything on the other side of it. And let the line move as they prove they can.

#151. Chasing China’s Monazite Monopoly



One of the singular features of Baotou, an industrial city of 2 million people in Inner Mongolia, is a 10-square-kilometer (4-square-mile) lake that is among the most polluted in the world. The artificial lake, referred to by the name of the dam associated with it, Weikuang Dam (or Baogang Tailings Dam), has for decades collected the effluent from minerals processing for China’s globally dominant iron, steel, and rare-earths industries.

Built in the late 1950s, the lake contains a highly toxic stew of industrial chemicals with substantial amounts of radioactive thorium, the latter resulting primarily from the processing of monazite ores for rare-earths. China has since greatly tightened its environmental regulations and restrictions on mining and minerals processing. For example, largely because of the environmental disaster that is Weikuang Dam, the country doesn’t permit the development or operation of stand-alone monazite mines—that is, mines where nothing is mined except monazite ore.

Now, with industrialized nations in North America, Asia, and Europe spending billions to shore up their rare-earths supply chains, and counting on monazite to help them do it, China’s history and experiences with monazite offer important lessons.

Monazite ore is a pillar of China’s rare-earths industry because it is a prime source of some less common but extremely important rare-earth elements, notably dysprosium, terbium, yttrium, and erbium. The country mines and processes vast quantities of monazite ore; it also imports huge amounts of monazite ore for processing.

Most of the domestically mined monazite ore comes from a single mine: the mammoth Bayan Obo complex, 150 kilometers north of Baotou, in Inner Mongolia. Bayan Obo, which is primarily an iron-ore mine, also happens to contain the largest known rare-earth deposit in the world. It’s the source of nearly half of all the rare-earth ore mined on the planet in a typical year. Most of the rare-earth ore mined at Bayan Obo is a type called bastnaesite; it’s the mining of that bastnaesite that makes it legal to mine monazite ore at Bayan Obo as well.

All told, ore from Bayan Obo was used to produce 170,000 tonnes of rare-earth metal last year, according to David Merriman, research director at the minerals intelligence firm Project Blue. Roughly 30 percent to 40 percent of the rare-earth ore mined at Bayan Obo is monazite. That amount alone would be enough to make China the world’s largest processor of monazite for rare-earths.

China’s 2025 Monazite Imports Topped 70,000 Tonnes

But a more detailed picture of China’s interest in monazite emerges when you consider the country’s imports of the ore. Last year, they amounted to more than 70,000 tonnes, says Thomas Kruemmer, a Singapore-based consultant on minerals and metals. That figure becomes more impressive when you consider the unique challenges of shipping monazite by sea. Because of its radioactivity, concentrated monazite ore is classified as Class 7 dangerous cargo under the International Maritime Dangerous Goods Code. That means, Kruemmer notes, that the ore can be imported through only four ports in China.

In addition, importation of some kinds of monazite into China is supposed to go through a joint venture consisting of the China National Nuclear Corp. and Shenghe Resources, which are at least partly state-owned. But lots of imported monazite doesn’t go through those organizations, notes Merriman. That’s because heavy mineral sands, which may contain roughly 2 percent or 3 percent monazite by weight, are not subject to the same import controls as concentrated monazite ore. So companies import the heavy mineral sands; separate out various profitable concentrates, including the monazite mineral concentrate; and then process the monazite concentrate to recover the rare-earths.

China has lately stepped up its efforts to secure rare-earth ore around the world. One reason, says Kruemmer, is simply to make up for recently lost supplies. In 2025, the U.S. government invested US $400 million in MP Materials, which operates the Mountain Pass mine and processing facility in California. The deal put an end to long-standing contracts under which MP Materials had been selling tens of thousands of tonnes of rare-earth concentrate annually to a trading affiliate of Shenghe Resources.

But other Westerners speculate that China has strategic reasons for stepping up its efforts to acquire heavy mineral sands or other rare-earth assets abroad. By locking down rights to deposits or projects abroad, Chinese organizations are making them unavailable to non-Chinese developers, according to this reasoning.

Merriman points out that your view of these activities largely depends on where you sit. The U.S. government recently made a conditional loan commitment of up to $725 million to U.S. monazite processor Energy Fuels, which has been aggressively securing substantial monazite supplies in Australia, Brazil, and Madagascar. To a Chinese observer, those moves probably look rather strategic.

China, meanwhile, is doing what it has been doing for decades. “China’s playing its game,” says Merriman.

Now, on her first mission as surveillance lead, she’s losing her cool because of a phone snafu? Furiously tapping the exit icon, she accidentally opens WhatsApp. “What the fuck!” she shouts. At first, Lili’s confused. Then she opens the chat and sees a history of carnal correspondence between this woman and Luis. Her heart drops. She pulls over.

The DEA has assembled a massive unit to shut down a high-powered narco money launderer, and she is on the side of the road, reading flirty texts from her husband’s mistress. She’s losing the target. But all Lili can think about is betrayal. The messages are endless. It’s not the first time. The image she’s had of herself and Luis, the eager greenhorn agent and hotshot husband, is coming apart in real time. What about their 3-year-old daughter?

She remembers the mission. The target they’re tailing is surely armed. A key rule of leadership is full faculties. One mistake and someone could get killed. She’s clearly lost tactical command. Just as they all expected, she thinks. Failing when it counts.

Lili knows this could hurt her career. What she doesn’t know, in this moment, is just how far her career will eventually go; that this gut punch will catalyze her emergence as an unlikely pioneer in an entirely new kind of criminal investigation. That she will help seize millions of dollars in illicit cryptocurrency, arrest some of the world’s most treacherous criminals, and help write the playbook still used in digital money-laundering investigations. Her colleagues will know her as a principled, dogged, ruthless, sometimes frustrating but always inventive woman waging a highly personal crusade. And when she helps take down the largest and most sophisticated dark-web marketplace in history, her targets will place a $4 million bounty on her head.

“Infante!” she hears over the radio. “Where are you?”

Lili Infante became a DEA agent by accident.

It was spring 2007, Lili’s sophomore year at Columbia University. She was a serious and studious economics major who cut a notable figure on campus, a tall Russian émigré who rarely socialized. Having grown up in the chaos of post-Soviet Moscow, in an unstable household, Lili sought structure, discipline, and excellence. She planned to outwork her peers and land a coveted internship on Wall Street her first summer.

Lili at 19 with her grandmother Nina in the Russian countryside.

Among the few diversions Lili permitted herself were weekly Skype calls with her grandmother Nina, back in Russia. Lili had a complicated relationship with her mom, a divorced drinker with a string of boyfriends, but Nina was her closest confidante, the only person she truly trusted. Lili would stay up late to tell Nina about life in America, her inability to integrate into campus culture, and her ballroom dance club, which was her other recreational indulgence. Lili didn’t like “fun,” per se, but she liked dancing for the same reason she liked school—it’s orderly, choreographed, predictable. Critically, it’s also nonverbal. Conversation doesn’t come easily to Lili. All her life, she’s felt like an outsider, an amateur anthropologist peering in on humanity rather than participating in it. A room full of twisting, twirling, silent strangers is one of the few places Lili feels ordinary.

As the spring semester drew to a close, Lili set her eye on summer jobs. She’d applied for a financial analyst position at Citi’s wealth management division and another position, described only as “Russian language analyst,” which she’d stumbled across on a career board. One day, she received offers from both jobs, minutes apart. She could report to duty at Citi Wealth, fulfilling a longtime goal. Or she could accept the analyst position, which mysteriously listed no employer, only an address: 99 10th Avenue. This turned out to be the Manhattan headquarters of the Drug Enforcement Administration. She was intrigued. As a child in Moscow, Lili had been obsessed with American police procedurals. She was drawn to their clean message of good versus evil and the inevitability of justice, which was the opposite of the lawless kleptocracy she knew. During her childhood, Moscow was ruled by gangs. The police often worked for bribes. Here, the police came when you called. She admired them. But should she begin her career on Wall Street or realize her childhood dream of fighting crime?

The following Monday, Lili showed up at DEA headquarters, a giant industrial box in Chelsea just south of the neighborhood’s blue-chip art galleries. A receptionist guided her through a warren of fluorescent-tube-lit hallways and deposited her in a room with a huge bulletin board, classically arranged with mug shots, question marks, thumb tacks, and byzantine constellations of yarn. An agent straight out of central casting—bald, no-nonsense, sturdy—introduced himself and showed her around. Lili couldn’t believe it. The agent told her what she’d be working on, a RICO investigation into Russian gangsters based in Brighton Beach. She would operate a wiretap and translate in real time the conversations of drug runners, money mules, foot soldiers, and hit men.

“Think you can handle that?” the agent asked.

Lili was stunned. “Yes,” she said, after collecting herself. “Hell yes.”

“Good,” he  said. He handed her a set of headphones. “Welcome to the DEA.”

That summer, Lili learned that she was built for the life of an investigator.

The moment she tuned into the wire and heard unfamiliar voices, she was hooked. Lili has always loved the puzzle-solving aspect of surveillance. Growing up, she often eavesdropped on her mother’s phone calls, trying to decipher the mechanics of adults and their relationships. The wire provided a new outlet for that voyeuristic impulse. Spying felt right. She happily pulled triple shifts.

Lili was aiding an investigation into a gang led by a man named Boris Nayfeld. He came to power by seizing control of the Bratva—the Russian mob—in Brighton Beach after an alleged assassination. His gang had hooks in every racket imaginable: drugs, diamond smuggling, human trafficking, Medicaid fraud, murder for hire. Lili’s primary targets were two Ukrainian brothers, Nikolai and Arthur Dozortsev, who were linked to importing ecstasy tabs by the million from Southeast Asia and laundering the profits through shell companies and luxury items. They were rumored to own a hilltop villa in the French Riviera, a Riva Vertigo yacht, and an Aston Martin Vanquish coupe. They were high-profile fixers—and very scary men—who turned out to have very ordinary conversations with family, friends, girlfriends, dentists. Lili was  fascinated by the mobsters’ quotidian chatter. She wondered: Was everyone capable of evil? Lili is a dogmatically ethical person. Dishonesty, betrayal, and violence confound her. And here were stone-cold criminals, leading otherwise normal lives.

As Lili translated, agents worked in the field, acting on the intel she gathered—raiding stash houses, making arrests. Others supervised her surveillance. One of those was a  brash, handsome 32-year-old lead case agent named Luis Infante.

Surveillance is like war: mostly boring. Between crucial moments, there’s a lot of time spent lounging, spinning around in cheap swivel chairs. Eventually, Luis and Lili got to talking. Luis was half Puerto Rican, half Cuban, born in Yonkers; he’d become a special agent after four years with the NYPD and the state troopers. His was not such a strange path. But how did an Ivy League sophomore, Luis wondered, end up in a basement operating a wiretap?

Lili started at the beginning. She was born Lilita Shabazian in 1987, in Moscow. Her mother, Katya, had aspired to be an actress but got pregnant at 19. Unwilling to sacrifice her own youth, Katya spent nights partying. Alone at home, an 8-year-old Lili made her own dinner and sat in front of the TV, sipping her mother’s booze and watching reruns like Walker, Texas Ranger. Katya maintained their meager household by selling clothes in the local market. At times, Katya traveled, leaving Lili in the countryside with her grandmother, Nina, a strict and independent woman. Nina grew her own food and made her own elaborate dresses. She had high hopes for Lili and would sit with her granddaughter and read the classics: War and Peace, Crime and Punishment, and Lili’s favorite, The Three Musketeers, with its themes of loyalty and courage in the face of corruption. Nina was a bright spot in a bleak childhood, much of which Lili spent wandering the streets of Moscow with other aimless children. “For us,” Lili told Luis, “there were no piano lessons.”

Lili as a toddler in Moscow, with her mother, Katya, behind her.

One night, Lili told Luis a story about how, when she was a child, a local gang threatened her mother. In Moscow at the time, anyone who sold goods had to pay for protection; Katya paid a gang that came cheap and was low in the local hierarchy, which left her open to intimidation. One day a bigger gang decided to target Katya's business partner, kidnapping him, beating him, and leaving him for dead in the forest. Then they set their sights on Katya and demanded $50,000, threatening to abduct Lili and send her back in the mail, piece by piece. Lili recalled seeing her mother in tears on the phone, begging for mercy. No one came for Lili, but Katya blamed herself for the predicament. She started drinking heavily to dull the regret. That was when Katya started disappearing, eventually for days at a time—and when Lili's obsession with American cops and robbers took on new meaning. On Law & Order, the law wins. For Lili, justice became a fantasy.

Now in America, at the DEA, Lili could live that dream. By midsummer, she was anxious for a breakthrough in the case. She wanted Nayfeld and his goons—Russian gangsters just like the men who tormented her mother—behind bars. Luis explained that RICO investigations could take years. Law enforcement is often about patience, he said. Lili had a hard time with that. They listened for hours, waiting for Nayfeld’s henchmen to make evidentiary mistakes. What they heard was mobsters running errands around Brighton Beach.

The long shifts spent listening meant more late-night chats. Luis asked how Lili came to the States. She thought she was doomed to live and die in Russia, she said, but one day, when she was 11, the phone rang. “Hello, Lili,” a man said. “This is your father.”

It was the first time Lili could remember crying. Her father had disappeared when she was a toddler. She had never known his voice. A few days later he was at the door, a burly man with dark brows, fitted into an expensive suit. He didn’t explain why he’d left, or now returned. In the US, he had become successful. Something about selling tires. Later Lili would come to realize his business was “connected,” but as a poor girl in Russia, she was amazed as he introduced her to a world of ease and excess. Lili had grown up with a single pair of pants and no winter coat, and now her father waltzed into Moscow’s elite boutiques and gave her free run of the stores. He took her to Moscow’s finest restaurants. Then he said he was bringing her to America. Lili didn’t want to abandon her family, but her mother and grandmother urged otherwise. She should leave, they said, and have a life. The other children in their neighborhood had already turned to crime. Some kids she knew were drug dealers and prostitutes. In Moscow, they said, there was nothing for her.

America was as different from post-Soviet Moscow as Lili imagined, but she was still mostly on her own. Her father remained wildly generous at times but could also turn manic or sadistic; he’d shower her with gifts, then beat her for any perceived imperfection. He sent Lili to the Windsor School in Queens and then to Loomis Chaffee, an elite private academy in Connecticut. It took Lili only a few months to become fluent in English, but she didn’t make many friends. “I didn’t know how to act,” Lili told Luis. “I was like a hood rat.” And so she studied and studied, outpaced her peers, skipped a grade, and found herself 17 years old and enrolled at Columbia University in New York.

Before Luis, Lili had shared her story with very few people. But something about him made her feel comfortable. Luis was charming, funny, and incredibly handsome. He had a wide smile, dark hair, and bright green eyes. He was married, though, so Lili hid the feelings she was starting to have for him. Toward the end of the summer, Luis came clean: He was falling for her too. He was separated from his wife, he said. He begged Lili to be with him. She demurred. She admitted her own attraction, but an affair went against her ethics. His marriage was already through, he said. She remained resolute.

Now it was awkward. They were still up late together in the wiretap room. Alone, side by side, listening to the mobsters chat—or worse, have phone sex with their girlfriends—Lili and Luis batted romantic repartee at each other. He loved her, he said. Midlife crisis, she shot back. I’m only 32, he said. I’m not yet 20, she replied. Lili stood her ground.

Come Autumn, Lili returned to school and passed the wiretap to a new analyst. Luis and his wife entered couples therapy. As the leaves turned and Lili settled into her sophomore classes, she moved on.

“I left her. It’s over. I need to see you.”

Lili and Luis stepped off the plane into the heavy San Salvadorian air. Katya trailed behind, thickly made up, stumbling beneath a blanket of blown-out hair. Lili wondered whether she had made a terrible mistake by inviting her mother to come here and live with them. It was 2012. Just three years after graduating from Columbia, Lili was already a mother herself. She held her baby, Lia, in her arms.

After Luis left his wife, Lili dated him through college and fell madly in love. After class, she met him downtown in pubs and dives, where Luis talked about the day’s dangers and shared stories from his time at the NYPD: chasing a dealer down a Bronx alleyway; wrestling a gun from a man’s hand in a high-rise hallway. Then they went back to his place. With each date, Lili’s world of exams and problem sets seemed increasingly frivolous. By the time she graduated, in 2009, the idea of Wall Street was long gone and she was set on becoming a federal agent.

But it was the Great Recession, and the DEA didn’t seem to be accepting applications. So Lili and Luis married, moved in together, and had a daughter. Lili loved being a mother but quickly understood that she did not want to be only a mother.

Lili roamed their apartment cradling Lia. During nap time, she called her grandmother. Once, when Lili was lamenting the challenges of motherhood, Nina offered a startling idea: Why not bring Katya to live with them and help raise Lia? Lili was reluctant. Chaos followed Katya everywhere, plus she was still drinking. But Nina said that she just needed something to live for. Otherwise, it was only a matter of time before she overdosed in her apartment or passed out in a snowbank.

Nina had never steered Lili wrong. Lili booked her mother a flight.

Katya made a strange addition to their home. She couldn’t drive and spoke almost no English. Her Russian was stilted and aristocratic, an affectation she had picked up while nannying for a Russian oligarch in Dubai after Lili left for America. Her typical uniform was a fur coat, silk pantsuit, and 5-inch stilettos. Katya is something of a post-Soviet Blanche DuBois, an aging beauty obsessed with status and regret, always complaining, or boasting about her greatest achievement: almost being cast, at 17, as the lead in a 1980s Soviet film.

With Katya available to watch Lia, Lili contemplated the most prestigious way to fill her time while she waited out the dismal job market. On a whim, she applied to Harvard Extension School, got accepted, and began taking classes. She loved being back in the world, learning, striving. But then Luis accepted a plum but dangerous post in El Salvador, where he would be investigating high-level drug trafficking operations and acting as a diplomatic liaison. Lili, Katya, and baby Lia joined him, and the Infantes moved into a palatial embassy house, complete with a cook and a maid, whom Katya relished ordering around.

Theatrics aside, Katya improved once she was surrounded by family. She quit drinking and threw herself into caring for her granddaughter, just as Nina had predicted. She was a doting and careful (if theatrical) babushka, seemingly intent on making up for the ways she had neglected her own daughter. For Lili, it was healing to watch.

But she was still restless. Determined to finish her degree, Lili started flying back and forth to Cambridge weekly—a five-hour flight, during which she crammed case studies. At home in El Salvador, she finished problem sets while rocking Lia to sleep. After two years, she graduated from Harvard with honors and submitted her application to the DEA.

At the DEA academy in Quantico, Virginia, Lili considered the question, which she assumed was rhetorical. It wasn’t, as she discovered when her instructor, an older, bulging, terrifying woman, repeated it, much louder.

“WRONG!” the instructor said. “As a woman, to survive in this agency, you’ll have to become either a bitch or a slut. Take your pick.” (The DEA declined to comment on the record for this story.)

It was 2014. Lili was the only woman in her class. Her peers were very large men accustomed to violence: former police looking to up the stakes and Afghanistan vets in search of a new thrill. Lili dominated many of them academically, answering questions first or correcting their errors. In athletic training, she was ferocious, pouncing on her training partners in boxing, wrestling, and jiu-jitsu, even though she was half their size. She wanted to dispel any suspicions about female timidity. She succeeded. No one at Quantico had ever met anyone—man or woman—quite like her. “Instead of a bitch or a slut, I chose a third option,” Lili says. “I was annoying.”

After 20 weeks of training, Lili graduated near the top of her class. She would be stationed in Miami, where Luis had already been reassigned after his stint in El Salvador.

Ever since the cocaine explosion of the 1980s, Miami had been America’s central depot for the drug. Geography made it inevitable. The city leans into the Atlantic, pointing toward the Caribbean—the source of endless contraband, smuggled in by imaginative cartels. In the heyday of the cocaine trade, powerboats rocketed across Biscayne Bay, their hulls stuffed with powder. Cessnas drifted in low over the skyline, dodging radar to land on airstrips in the Everglades. Cartel divers attached magnetic parcels to cargo ship hulls. Homemade narco subs slipped unseen beneath the surface.

For the Miami DEA office, this still meant constant action and late nights on dangerous cases—exactly what Lili was looking for. Compared to the dingy New York office, the Miami DEA was a gleaming palace of spotless glass and marble. Agents drove fast cars and hit the extensive first-floor gym before their shift, setting personal records on the bench or running combinations on punching bags before heading upstairs. This was the office that had brought down Noriega, rooted out a Hezbollah money-laundering ring, and once intercepted the sale of a $35 million Soviet submarine to cartel leaders in Colombia. Lili wanted in.

Instead, she was given trash detail. On one of her first days on the job, Lili stood alone in a remote warehouse, tossing ziplocks full of drugs into an incinerator, marking the destroyed evidence on a clipboard. She was also pulling stints on the Miami DEA’s most dreaded post: the Duty Box, a closet-sized post where her two jobs were to answer the anonymous tip line and buzz visitors into the boss’s office.

Lili was one of four women in her unit. The DEA culture was dated—the veterans who dominated the agency were called Jurassic narcs—and her office had a hard-partying style. After work, agents hit the bars and clubs together. But Lili didn’t drink. Having watched her mother’s undoing, Lili likes to say she “got sober at 12 years old,” when she left Russia. Instead, she stayed late at the office, filling out busywork reports with dizzying thoroughness. On weekends there were backyard barbecues, but Lili was rarely invited. Instead she went to Yuca 105, a Cuban restaurant in South Beach where she danced salsa. Yuca had serious dancers, the kind who don’t party, or make small talk, or do anything to compromise their focus and coordination. Lili fit right in. She’d drive home drenched in sweat, a momentary bliss.

Meanwhile, the assholes back at work called Lili “Sochi,” after the Russian city that was hosting the upcoming winter Olympics. On a team-building trip to the International Spy Museum, they pointed at placards on Russian agents through history: “Friends of yours, Sochi?”

“Don’t take it personally,” Luis said. He wanted to support Lili, but he was stationed in a different office, across town. He knew Lili would be a great agent, but he also knew that her social graces, or lack thereof, might be holding her back. She tended to ruffle feathers. She was bad at reading the cues of an institutional culture. Lili always admired how easily Luis navigated social situations, how he could charm anyone. “I know it’s silly,” he said, “but maybe try smiling? Make small talk. The men are going to look down on you, especially since you’re smarter than them. Act impressed. Flattery helps.” Lili wasn’t interested in flattery, but she knew Luis was right. “You’re gonna have to make some friends,” he said.

If there was one friend to make in Lili’s office, it was Jose Irizarry, a rising star in the Miami DEA whose desk was just across from Lili’s. Irizarry was a top agent in Group 4, an international money-laundering unit and a linchpin division: The entire narco economy is built around clandestine finance. The members of Group 4 were like the DEA’s SEALs, running deep-cover operations, posing as mules, or cultivating informants to infiltrate outfits in Cartagena, Madrid, Amsterdam, and throughout the Caribbean. Theirs was a game of trust; for some, making friends with criminals meant partying alongside them. For years, Jose and his team had been working a cartel smuggler named Diego Marin. The “Contraband King” allegedly laundered dope money with nested shell companies offshore, misvalued imported goods, and engaged in other astonishingly inventive misdirections. To run elaborate stings on their targets, Jose’s team used what’s called Attorney General Exempt Operations, which gave DEA agents authority to act as actual money launderers for cartels to understand the narco traffickers’ financial pathways. Group 4 was creative, restless, and reckless. Lili gazed with envy across the office as they planned their missions.

Soon she made a habit of sneaking over to buttonhole Jose about his division, peppering him with questions about mirror trading and tax havens, hawaladars and mules. She told him how badly she wanted to do the kind of work he was doing. Sweeping, sophisticated, international cases with real consequences. “Be patient,” he said. “You’ll catch your break.”

She was on her way to meet a few men. Businessmen, you could say. The kind who were high enough up the criminal chain that they would fit right in at a Dade County Chamber of Commerce meeting. Olga parked and stepped out in her heels and miniskirt—Gucci and Louis Vuitton, head to toe. She introduced herself to her clients. They ordered and got down to business. Olga was a money-laundering wunderkind, here to advise them. Except Olga was not a money launderer. Olga was Lili, wired for sound.

It had been six months since Lili joined the DEA, and she’d distinguished herself quickly. She was now deep into the most dangerous casework available: undercover operations. What made her unusual at the DEA—being a young, pretty, Russian émigré—was ideal for cover. She developed an alter ego, Olga: a cunning, mysterious, seductive money mule.

As Olga, she dined alongside some of Miami’s scariest, most powerful men. She always arrived a little late, so that targets and layout could be confirmed by physical surveillance agents posing as diners. Her provocative but elegant dress came courtesy of the DEA’s drug seizure money. Lili dressed plainly in real life, but Olga felt powerful strutting down Collins Avenue, turning heads.

Undercover bios should be just different enough to minimize the need for fabrication. So Olga was born in Moscow and moved to New York when she was 12. There, she got involved with the Russian mob out in Brighton Beach. Just like Lili’s old friend Boris Nayfeld, Olga knew how to wash even the dirtiest money by filtering it through a variety of real businesses and shell companies. She’d present her services, and then, with the  cash in her care, the DEA would arrest or flip the unwitting target.

Olga called her services “business development”: luring in prospective clients for a subsequent bust. They talked shop, made plans, and toasted to the future. Olga drove home, up Collins Avenue with the glassy intracoastal on the left, full of  meandering pleasure craft, and then on the right a line of deco palaces that eventually gave way to the dunes of the Atlantic. The most beautiful drive in the world, as far as Lili was concerned, and one she took often, heading home after undercover nights to look in on sleeping Lia and unwind with Luis, trading stories about targets, networks, informants.

As Olga, Lili was thrust into the byzantine realm of cartel cat and mouse, where the DEA cultivated an array of CIs, or confidential informants. These were mostly unsavory characters, either pressured with prison time or, worse in Lili’s mind, attracted to the power and the financial rewards. In the criminal underworld, the key currencies were cocaine, cash, and information, and the market for the latter was extremely gray. Informants were either paid for their efforts or given a cut of DEA hauls, or they were sociopaths allowed to trade testimony for a light sentence.

One of Lili’s primary sources was a chubby, charismatic Columbian businessman, codenamed Igor. Jose, Lili’s mentor, had cultivated Igor over many years and entrusted him to Lili after she showed her prowess in the field. Igor owned a used-car dealership and a series of electronics stores. Since mobsters are always looking for beater cars and burner phones, his outlets attracted prime targets for the DEA. Lili, Jose, and Igor always met in public, sometimes at swank spots, sometimes at Dunkin Donuts. Jose and Igor acted like old pals, laughing and reminiscing about past stings. For the arrests Igor helped arrange, he would often take a 20 percent cut of the DEA’s seized proceeds. By the time Lili met Igor, in 2014, he was rumored to have racked up millions.

Igor was useful. He introduced Olga to his clientele and had his ear to the rail in the underworld. But Lili was growing uneasy. It didn’t sit right with her that a venal huckster could get rich by working both ends of the drug war. “This is just how it goes,” Jose would explain to her. The people who have information on criminals are, by definition, mixed up with criminals. But Lili liked her ethical lines tidy—and it felt strange to be handing Igor envelopes full of cash. Igor is a small fish, Jose said. And you need small fish to catch the big ones.

Lili followed Jose’s lead. He was the pro, after all. Luis advised similar. When Lili would catch her grandmother over Skype, Nina was only proud. “You are succeeding in America,” she said. “What else is there?”

Around the office, Lili was finally getting some respect. She set her sights on doing consequential work just like Group 4, Jose’s elite squad. Their beat was international. The big show. To get there, she had to prove herself in domestic money laundering. And she needed to show tactical capability on a bust. This was how she found herself chasing a money mule through the streets of suburban Florida, with a chopper overhead. How she ended up pulled over on the highway, looking at her husband’s phone, and realizing that not only was her target getting away, but Luis, the man of her dreams, was cheating on her.

Lili sat at home, in shock. She’d radioed HQ and pulled out of the mission, surprising everyone. “Family emergency,” she’d said. The irony, she thought, stepping over Lia’s toys on the living room floor. The house was empty. Her mother was out of town, and Lia was at school. She waited in silence for Luis to come home. Out of curiosity, she typed a flirty message to the mistress on his phone and got an immediate response in kind. Her heart shattered anew.

Lili had suspected that Luis had strayed at least once before, when she was pregnant. He insisted nothing ever got physical, but he still had to beg for forgiveness. He followed her to Russia, where she’d sought refuge with family, and stood outside her window, crying inconsolably. “All men are liars, Lili,” Nina had told her back then. “But at least this one seems to have a conscience.” She advised Lili to take Luis back. But this time, Lili couldn’t forgive. When Luis walked in with Lia, laughing, Lili called him into the bedroom and dropped the phone at his feet. On cue, he fell to his knees.

“You don’t have to lie anymore, Luis,” she said. “I did the reconnaissance.”

After booting Luis from the house and putting Lia to bed, Lili called Nina. Her grandmother was comforting but blunt, as always. “You are a survivor, Lili. You can survive on your own, like I have.”

Lili filed for divorce, and they split custody of Lia. To shut out her grief, Lili threw herself further into work. She still wanted to make a name in money laundering, but her first field lead had been a fiasco. In the DEA, you’re only as good as your last bust. She felt like she needed to prove herself. One night, alone in her newly empty bed, her mind raced. She remembered an episode of The Good Wife she’d seen, where the plot revolved around a new, anonymous, decentralized cryptocurrency called bitcoin. A clever technology, she thought, and likely also a perfect tool for cartels. Rather than smuggle duffel bags full of cash across the border, they could make the same transfer online.

The next morning, Lili scoured the DEA case management system for investigations involving crypto. If she had the idea that criminals could use bitcoin to launder money, likely they had too. But she found only one major case: the Silk Road, the world’s first dark-web marketplace, where users traded bitcoin for drugs, guns, and counterfeit documents. Federal agents had shut down the site and arrested its founder, Ross Ulbricht, a year earlier.

Surely, Lili thought, Silk Road was just the beginning. She asked around the office. Most had never heard of the Silk Road investigation. Others were uninterested. But Lili was certain this was the future of crime. Alone at night, she started plumbing the depths of the dark web and discovered that dozens of sites had filled the Silk Road vacuum. There was an entire illicit economy operating right under the nose of the DEA, a new narco trafficking paradigm fueled by a revolutionary transactional system. In a way, this was the ultimate money laundering case. With cryptocurrency, the money didn’t even need to be laundered.

Lili had marched into her supervisor’s office unannounced, as usual. It was late 2014. She wanted authorization to spend crypto on the dark web. Sitting behind his desk, surrounded by his awards and challenge coins, her supervisor asked, “Who’s bitcoin?”

Lili had been up all night, ruminating on the endless criminal possibilities of cryptocurrency, and she rattled off an impassioned crash course on the history of dark-web marketplaces.

The dark web, she explained, relied upon two innovations: bitcoin, with its anonymous transactions, and an encrypted network called Tor, which routed web traffic through an enormous number of servers worldwide, hiding a cybercriminal’s IP address among a sea of decoys. The combination of bitcoin and Tor supposedly rendered dark-web drug transactions completely untraceable, and so dealers were migrating online in droves. Why stand exposed on a street corner and risk your life defending territory when you could sit in your home, wait for customers to send you bitcoin, and then mail them your product? The previous year, US police had made 1.5 million arrests for narcotics-related crimes, but the drug epidemic was getting worse. The cocaine crisis had been replaced by the even more destructive opioid crisis. And synthetic opioids, which could be manufactured anywhere, were hitting the streets. Now, with digital marketplaces, they were available like mail-order retail. The drug war was changing, and they had to get ahead of it. Lili, of course, was the woman for the job.

Her supervisor, nonplussed, allotted some money in deference to Lili’s dissertation, if only to get rid of her. It would be a proof of concept, Lili said. If the drugs show up in the mail, we know the problem is real. He filled out a form and released $200.

Lili used a marketplace called Blue Sky to purchase cocaine from a vendor named HonestCocaine. When other agents caught wind of what Lili was up to, they gave her a hard time. “Stick to your casework, Sochi,” they said. “Don’t get distracted by internet funny money.”

A week later, a package arrived at a DEA post office box. The white powder went to the lab, and HonestCocaine lived up to their name: The delivery was real, and high quality. Lili marched back into her boss’s office. There are dozens of sites, she said, and thousands of dealers. “We have work to do,” Lili said.

To take down dark-web marketplaces, Lili needed to know how they worked. She’d been attending local cryptocurrency meetups in Miami: oddball gatherings of techies, gambling addicts, and libertarians who met at local Cuban spots to sip café con leche. Her presence as the only young woman, yet again, caused a stir at first—the crypto demographic at the time was decidedly male and nerdy—but they were impressed by her focus and interest. While the crypto acolytes waxed poetic about freedom from fiat currencies and the beauty of the blockchain, Lili was enticed by the opposite implication. The fact that every bitcoin transaction was indelibly recorded meant it was perhaps not anonymous. Once she figured out how to trace it, following the money would be easier than ever.

Dark-web investigations paired well with single motherhood. No longer able to stay late at the office, Lili was home by 6 every night, in mom mode, playing with Lia and talking about her day at school. After putting her to bed, Lili cracked open her laptop and clocked back in, working late, sometimes chatting with her grandmother as she scrolled through listings of drugs, guns, and stolen passports.

After a few weeks, Lili was ready to select a target. She felt like “a kid in a candy store” deciding who to go after first. One vendor, username Owlcity, stood out for his potent inventory: Alpha-PVP, aka bath salts, and fentanyl.

The DEA had no playbook for how to pursue a dark-web case. She began by purchasing some product from Owlcity, 2.5 grams of Alpha-PVP. The package came a week later and clearly incriminated the dealer. But who was Owlcity? A dark-web case, Lili realized, is the exact reverse of a typical narcotics investigation. Here, the crime is visible for all to see—the drug arrives at your doorstep—but you can’t prove who sent it.

Lili examined the package. Owlcity had been careful. Fake name and return address. A generic stamp, untraceable. She dusted the cardboard for fingerprints, but the exterior had been handled many times, by couriers and warehouse workers. Changing tack, Lili attempted to penetrate Tor and pin down Owlcity’s IP address. She taught herself to analyze the blockchain. As with English, she picked it up quickly. But every effort was a dead end. Her target remained a disembodied string of characters, a ghost.

Finally, in February 2016, Lili had a flash of inspiration. She messaged Owlcity and politely explained that a package she had ordered from him earlier had never arrived. Because the dealer seemed to pride himself on good customer service—he had a 98 percent positive rating—she had a hunch the dealer would respond. He did, and she asked him to track the package, hoping that when the user went to USPS.com, they wouldn’t think to use Tor. Which they didn’t. Owlcity had no idea that the DEA could access the post office web-traffic tracking logs, which led Lili to an apartment only a few miles away, in the suburb of Miramar, and a name: Chrissano Leslie.

A week later, Lili surveilled Leslie, a young Jamaican man, driving beneath the palms to his local post office, where he dropped off a package with fentanyl in it. A few months after that, Leslie awoke to the sound of his front door splintering off its hinges. Shooting out of bed, he grabbed his stash, ran half naked to the bathroom, and shook a cascade of pills and powders into the toilet. Close behind was Lili’s Clan Lab team—a special unit trained to handle lethal substances like Fentanyl: 10 guys in hazmat suits, armed. “Freeze!” He flushed the toilet, but the pills clogged the drain. He was trying to flush again as agents tackled him. Leslie’s apartment was brimming with home-pressed pills, and underneath his bed they found a stash of weapons: handguns and an Uzi. A serious dealer, just sitting in the suburbs, with no visibility to police or DEA. Just as Lili figured. As the agents got cuffs on Leslie, the toilet overflowed, pushing a cloudy fountain of fentanyl-laced water across the tile.

The Owlcity arrest made the papers, and Lili earned some stripes. She moved full-time to cybercrime, and her supervisor even suggested she lead a team. But she says she had a tough time recruiting in her own office; even worse than a know-it-all rookie was a know-it-all rookie with a promotion. The Jurassic narcs didn’t want to be her subordinate.

So Lili widened her approach. Throughout the Owlcity investigation, she’d enlisted outside federal agencies to help her when the DEA’s resources fell short of the demands of a complex, drawn-out cybercrime investigation. To access banking records, she’d used IRS investigative tools; to track Leslie’s packages, she’d employed Postal Inspections; to kick down his door, she’d called in FBI SWAT. To bring each of these agencies onboard, she’d visited them in person and pitched a PowerPoint-enhanced presentation on the looming dangers of dark-web drug dealing. Her renegade enthusiasm was contagious, and at each agency she convinced a few agents to help her out.

Now, Lili had a new pitch: What if these agents stayed with her and formed an official task force? She found takers and even added agents from Homeland Security. Almost unintentionally, Lili had formed one of the first interagency cybercrime task forces. She was 28.

Lili, 28, in the Miami DEA office with guns that were seized in her first big dark-market bust—the Owlcity case.

Later that year, Lili was at a crypto conference when she met Tigran Gambaryan, already a legend in cybercrime for developing the capability to decipher the blockchain. Tigran had been an investigative state auditor before picking up a badge and gun to join the IRS’s elite Criminal Investigations Division, the law enforcement arm of the federal tax authority, where agents used specialized techniques to bring down criminals in the field.

Lili watched Tigran present his investigation into the corrupt DEA agent Carl Force, who had skimmed more than $700,000 in bitcoin while investigating Silk Road. Force was tempted by his belief that bitcoin was untraceable, but Tigran linked the theft back to him with ingenious innovations in blockchain analytics. It was the first time blockchain evidence was introduced in court. Nearly awestruck, Lili took notes and then introduced herself afterward.

They had a lot to talk about. Tigran stood 5'5" and wore thick-rimmed glasses and, usually, a tight shirt around conspicuous biceps; like Lili, he was a nerd with an adrenaline dependency. She was surprised to learn that Tigran, too, grew up poor in Moscow, even in the same district as Lili. This was why he also hated corrupt cops. They exchanged numbers and decided to keep in touch. They might need each other down the line.

With the Silk Road gone, a dark-web marketplace known as Dream Market was now the largest in the US—the new emporium for drugs, counterfeit documents, and stolen data. And Dream, Lili decided, would be the target of her newly minted Counternarcotics Cyber Task Force. Lili codenamed her investigation Operation Tornado, after Tor. Historically, drug trafficking investigations are looking to catch the leadership in conspiracy cases. But dark-web drug vendors and administrators rarely communicate directly and never learn each other’s real names. It’s like a cartel but with the operational security of a terror cell. Lili figured she should start with some vendors, then move on to the leadership—OxyMonster and Kitt3n, or the “Dream Team,” as they called themselves.

Of Dream’s many vendors, one stood out to Lili: UStoUS, who peddled some of the most experimental products on the dark web. His signature item was called the “God Pill,” a gumbo of cocaine, amphetamines, and fentanyl. Via an undercover buyer account, Lili ordered the God Pill to Miami. Using the technique she learned in the Owlcity case, she traced the IP address of the computer used to buy the postage, which led her to a smoke shop called Heady Warez in Cape Girardeau, Missouri, on the edge of the Ozarks. An excellent lead. But the question remained, who was UStoUS: the owner, an employee, or simply a customer checking on his shipments while waiting in line?

Operation Tornado caught a break when local police in Cape Girardeau called in to report a tip. A woman, fresh out of rehab, walked in and told detectives that her boyfriend was trying to kill her. She was addicted to opioids, and he was a dealer who sold on the internet. He knew that she was trying to get clean but deliberately tempted her with his product, leaving pills out where he knew she would find them. His name was Joshua Kelly, and he owned Heady Warez smoke shop.

A week later, two task force agents lounged in an unmarked car outside of Kelly’s shop. Kelly was a skeletal young man whom local law enforcement suspected was linked to a string of recent overdoses. This was a novel threat of drugs delivered through the mail, Lili realized: People often OD’d alone in their homes, and word of a tainted batch was slow to spread. Unlike a corner dealer, a dark-web trafficker is cut off from his customers and might have no idea that he's poisoning them.

It wasn’t until Lili had her agents search Kelly’s trash that they found damning evidence: the same labels he used when he shipped out his God Pills. On May 9, 2017, Lili and a SWAT team broke down Kelly’s door.

The entire house was a factory for pill production. While searching the rooms, an agent accidentally knocked over a vacuum cleaner. A cloud of fentanyl mushroomed out and settled onto the carpet. Lili found Kelly slumped over in a chair. Up close, he was disturbing to behold. Bone-thin and covered in open wounds, he was heavily addicted to his own supply.

When Lili questioned Kelly outside, he had only one thing to say.

Lili was haunted by what she’d seen. The takedown was satisfying, but the tragedy stayed with her. The opioid epidemic sweeping the nation was leaving broken, desperate people in its wake. On another bust, soon after, Lili found herself face-to-face with a dealer who used his own infant as a human shield. She had to talk him into setting the child down before she could cuff him. It reminded her of the desolate, hopeless flood of opiate addiction that took hold in Russia in the 1990s, which she’d fled because of the related crime and poverty. That addiction wave only continued; Nina had recently told Lili that her cousin Dennis, only 16, was starting to use and sell drugs, hang around gangsters, and disappear for days at a time. She was worried. Nina told Lili it felt like raising Katya all over again.

Alone, Lili took care of Lia and tunneled into work. At night, she’d sometimes call Luis. As angry as she was, he remained a sympathetic ear. He was proud, and not surprised that she’d already surpassed him professionally. And he was useful for helping Lili navigate prosecutorial personalities and the intricacies of building indictments.

Her next move, she told him, was the Dream Market management, starting with OxyMonster. Using tricks she’d learned from Tigran and her own research, Lili discovered OxyMonster’s identity by tracing his bitcoin wallet address, which he’d optimistically and foolishly made public so he could receive tips. The address was linked to a private server, which, oddly enough, also hosted websites related to competitive beard growing, like Beardsociety.com and Mybeard.org. This led her to a rising star in the competitive beard-growing world, a Frenchman named Gal Vallerius.

On August 31, 2017, at 1:05 pm, Gal Vallerius arrived at Atlanta International Airport, accompanied by his wife, Yasmin, and his incredible beard—30 centimeters of resplendent vermillion. Lili had been researching Vallerius for months. He was a homebody from a small Breton village who liked to take walks with his wife and tend to his pet rabbits. He was also a man of international distinction, as both an elite beard competitor and a global drug kingpin.

Vallerius and his wife were, in fact, en route to the World Beard and Mustache Championships in Austin, Texas, where Vallerius hoped to clinch the title. Homeland Security records indicated that Vallerius had never visited America before, and France is reluctant to extradite its citizens, so Lili seized the opportunity to catch him on US soil. She waited at customs with officials from the Transportation Security Administration and Customs and Border Protection, who pulled the couple aside and asked for their electronics for a routine search.

“What are you in town for?” an agent asked.

“A facial hair competition!” Vallerius replied. “In Texas.”

“Oh,” Lili said, feigning shock. She appraised his beard. “Well, you’ll definitely win!”

They all laughed. Then Lili took the electronics aside. Within seconds, she spotted Tor, a bitcoin wallet, and a text file that contained the schedules for Dream administrators.

At home that night, Lili examined Vallerius’ computer. To seize his bitcoin, she had to get into his wallet, which was password protected. But she’d stalked Vallerius so thoroughly that she knew enough personal details to hack his wallet the old-fashioned way: by guessing. She made a list of important dates (birthdays, anniversaries), sentimental names (pets, his wife), hobbies (beards, rabbits, the Bible), and then settled into an adrenalized fugue state in which she tried every permutation of every term until it opened. There it was: half a million in bitcoin.

Soon, Vallerius’ bearded face appeared on the pages of the Miami Herald, the Daily Mail, Vice, and The Guardian. With her signature on indictments and her name in the media, Lili was quickly gaining a reputation in the nascent world of cyber policing. She was invited to speak at conferences for bitcoin fanatics and cybercrime experts alike. She gave an interview to Bloomberg about the changing scope of criminal crypto transactions and was named law enforcement officer of the year by the US Attorney’s office in the Southern District of Florida.

But she could still fly under the radar on her home turf in Miami. She resurrected her Olga character with the express purpose of luring cartels into crypto. Now that she could trace web transactions, crypto would be the ultimate medium for the DEA’s money-laundering stings. Back out in the field, she set meetings with Sinaloa Cartel accountants, pitching a brand-new package of instantaneous digital transactions.

Lili at 16 with her grandmother Nina in the Russian countryside.

Career ascendent, Lili was still raising Lia solo, occupying herself with work, and relishing late-night chats with her grandmother. One day, she came home and found Katya distraught. “It’s Grandma,” she told Lili. “She’s sick.” Lili's grandmother had collapsed suddenly, and doctors eventually found an aggressive tumor in her brain: glioblastoma.

Lili sent her mother to Moscow to help Nina recover. Her brain was badly swollen from an emergency operation, but she was awake. Each morning, she seemed to be getting stronger, more alert. But then one night, on the phone, Katya told Lili that Nina had slipped away. It was a rare moment when Lili stopped in her tracks. Nina had been the strongest person she knew. And now, gone.

“Lili,” Katya said quietly, “there’s something else.” Late one night, Katya said, Nina had made a confession. Two years earlier, Dennis had gotten into trouble with a local gang. He’d sold drugs on consignment but then never paid the gang back. Men started coming around, threatening to kill him. To save him, Nina paid up.

“How?” Lili asked. She knew Nina had no money.

“She sold drugs,” her mother said. “On a dark-web marketplace called Hydra.”

Hydra was the largest darknet marketplace and among the most sophisticated in the world, a pseudo-corporate behemoth with 17 million users and thousands of vendors. It not only sold drugs but also accounted for about 80 percent of the darknet’s crypto transactions. For years, Lili had known about the market, often discussing it with Gambaryan, her fellow cybercrime crusader. Since Hydra operated only in Russia, Lili never thought it would cross her desk, much less sweep up her family.

She was gobsmacked. How in the world had a 72-year-old woman in the countryside, who had never committed a crime in her life, become a dark-web heavyweight seller? Nina was the most honest person Lili had ever known. When everyone else in Lili’s world had taught her to cheat and steal, Nina had modeled integrity. In silence, Lili listened as Katya relayed Nina’s confession. “She felt like there was no other way out,” Katya said.

Nina and Dennis hatched a plan to grow and sell marijuana to raise the payoff money. In Russia, dealing any amount of weed carries a mandatory four-year sentence—up to life, for large quantities—so prices are steep and profits can be significant for those willing to take the risk. Nina was a lifelong gardener. So she sourced an industrial quantity of marijuana seeds on Hydra, which was already dominant in Russia.

Much like Lili, Nina taught herself Tor, VPNs, and bitcoin, all while wrapped in a quilt in her country cottage. When harvest time came, Nina set up a vendor profile on Hydra, and Dennis became their grandmother’s delivery system. Hydra operated differently from other dark-web drug marketplaces; because the Russian post office often inspects packages for contraband, mail order was too risky. Instead, Hydra couriers stashed parcels in public places and sent buyers coordinates to recover them. A geocached delivery was known as a “hidden treasure”—klad—and couriers were called kladsmen.

As Nina’s kladsman, Dennis transported their product and hid it throughout Moscow—in gutters and bushes, buried in parks and snowbanks, stuck to magnets under benches. It was dangerous work. He dodged police, thieves known as “seagulls,” and vigilantes trying to interfere with Hydra. Hydra, meanwhile, had its own legion of enforcers, known as “sportsmen,” who punished seagulls and negligent kladsmen by drenching them in “brilliant green,” an antiseptic dye that’s nearly impossible to wash off, beating them with pipes, setting their hair on fire, or nailing their hands to trees. In Russia, the dark web wasn’t a hidden shadow economy. It was a war raging in the streets.

Nina and Dennis did well for themselves. It helped that Dennis, unbeknownst to Nina, was dealing harder stuff on the side. In a few months, Dennis’ debt was paid off—but the two kept going. Katya only realized the scale of her mother’s operation when she inquired about her medical costs. Did she need help? Lili could send some money. Nina told Katya not to worry. “You know,” she said. “I’m a very rich woman.”

When Katya was finished, Lili hung up without saying a word. Stunned, she thought about all her chats with her grandmother, sharing drug-busting tales, or the woes of her complicated family, and receiving advice and kind words in return. In the tumult of so many disappointments and betrayals, Nina had been Lili’s unshakable, centering constant. Now this? She decided then and there: Her next target was Hydra.

Around the DEA office, Lili’s new obsession was swatted away. She felt like a rookie again—here comes Sochi, spouting off another wild idea. She told her bosses that she felt compelled to take on this criminal powerhouse, that Hydra was consolidating power. Hydra administrators were advertising on Telegram and posting ads on YouTube. But she was firmly reminded that a crime needs to be committed against American citizens. Neither Lili nor the DEA had jurisdiction.

Then, Tigran Gambaryan called with news: He was serving on a team which had secured “extraterritorial jurisdiction” to take on Hydra and had found a federal attorney in San Francisco willing to take on the case. He knew Lili might have an interest in Hydra. He offered her a spot on the team.

Gambaryan brought Lili up to speed: The team had infiltrated Hydra’s finances and mapped its internal hierarchy. The market, it turned out, employed a diverse roster of salaried professionals: marketers, lawyers, IT people, an HR department, purity testers, and doctors who provided telehealth services to buyers. Highly educated, capable people. Hydra’s efficiency was unprecedented, and unsettling.

They had tracked down the market’s central bitcoin wallet. Meticulous blockchain analytics had led them to a Hetzner data center in Germany. German law enforcement took over from there, and after a long wait, the US team learned that the server housed all of Hydra’s funds.

But they still needed a foolproof argument for jurisdiction, so Gambarayan turned to Lili’s undercover skills. By sending in photos of the DEA’s own laboratory, Lili posed as a chemist and got herself hired by Hydra as a purity tester, yielding further revelations about the inner workings of the market.

Next, she turned her attention to the owner of the server housed in the Hetzner data center, a man named Dmitry Pavlov. Lili mined Pavlov’s social media, dating profiles, and forum posts to stitch together a picture of a 32-year-old web host from Cherepovets, a northwestern steel town, who drove a suspiciously expensive Audi, taught music lessons, and had a girlfriend named Dima. Pavlov, she learned, had allegedly hosted several other Russian darknet markets. She cracked into Pavlov’s private correspondence and got a clearer picture of Hydra’s origin. An anonymous figure, moniker No Name, had merged about a hundred Russian darknet sites into Hydra in 2015. Pavlov came aboard as Hydra’s IT administrator, joining a cadre of operators: There was Zog, the site’s main programmer; Fat Cat, its admin; Handsome Jack and Fatality, the customer dispute moderators; GlavRed, marketer extraordinaire; and Hydra’s two leaders, Satoshi Nakamoto (named for the mysterious inventor of bitcoin) and the Observer.

In 2016, Pavlov found himself in the middle of a digital turf war. Hydra was being targeted by another site, RAMP, Russia’s leading drug marketplace at the time. Hydra was luring RAMP’s vendors away with better advertising, wider distribution, stronger connections to Chinese precursor manufacturers, and a pioneering, utopian vision: a darknet marketplace run like a tech startup, staffed by talented programmers and finance whizzes as opposed to RAMP’s team, whom they called “simple addicts.” In their ads, blasted out on WhatsApp, Telegram, and YouTube, Hydra called itself “the internet” whereas RAMP was just “television.” RAMP fired back by poaching Hydra’s chemists, and Hydra retaliated by overwhelming its competitor’s site, temporarily shutting it down. RAMP was rumored to have hired armed thugs to go after turncoat vendors. Hydra doxed RAMP’s admins, eroding its users’ trust. Then came the leak that won the war: screenshots of a 20-year-old RAMP admin arguing with his mother over text. The site shut down soon after.

Hydra grew unchecked, amassing 1 million users, then 2 million, then 17 million, in more than a thousand towns, expanding past Russian borders into Kazakhstan, Armenia, Ukraine, and Belarus. Hydra's money trail ran through Russia's crypto underworld, much of it flowing into exchanges headquartered in a razor-shaped Moscow skyscraper, Federation Tower, then home to Garantex, Suex, and Chatex, three crypto exchanges later sanctioned by the US Treasury for money laundering.

Keeping pace with Hydra’s expansion, Pavlov rented more servers each year, eventually up to 50, to house its operations. Sourcing cheap synthetic precursors from India and China, Hydra vendors were able to serve provincial villages previously untapped by darknet markets. Rural unemployment made the life of a kladsman attractive to disaffected young people. Less agile Russians—even grandmothers—signed on as vendors. Hydra’s presence became ubiquitous, normalized, the logical end point of all darknet marketplaces before it. In an ad depicting the entire history of human drug use, Pavlov and company claimed they were bringing about “the onset of a new level in the spiritual development of humanity.”

Tigran and the rest of the team weren’t terribly concerned with Pavlov. They were more interested in Hydra itself—in seizing its funds and crippling its infrastructure. Lili shared their goal but wanted more. She wanted justice: real people behind bars, punished for their crimes. It became an obsession. A personal quest couched within the greater investigation.

Linking Pavlov to Hydra was the easy part. Arresting him would be more complicated. Lili couldn’t arrest him on Russian soil, and, unlike OxyMonster, he didn’t seem to have any international competitions on the calendar.

Then war broke out. In February 2022, the case suddenly accelerated when Russia invaded Ukraine and NATO levied economic sanctions. Hydra’s crypto exchange stood to allow blackhat hacker cells, arms merchants, and private militaries to move money into Russia, skirting sanctions. Lili’s dark-web target was in the midst of an international crisis. Alliances and animosities had been awakened. If Pavlov is smart, Lili thought, he’ll move the site onto a Russian server, where it—and he—would be safe from adversarial governments. Out of Lili’s reach forever.

As Russian tanks battled Ukrainian guerillas, drones clashed in the sky, and the rest of Europe looked on in terror, Lili sprinted to assemble enough evidence against Pavlov. On April 5, 2022, the team made its move. German police descended on the narrow, labyrinthine halls of the Hetzner data center, pulling servers from the walls. Meanwhile, at the Phillip Burton Federal Building in San Francisco, Lili stood before a federal grand jury convened in secret and carefully laid out the meticulous digital trail from IPs to wallets to Hydra for Pavlov’s indictment.

With each server removal, Hydra lost functionality. Finally, the site went dark.

Without leaving the United States, Lili had helped topple the largest dark-web marketplace in the world. Pavlov was still free, which exasperated her. But she had a plan. One that relied, bizarrely, on the services of an organization which had never once helped her, or her family: Russian law enforcement. Lili was angling to force their hand. Once the Hydra news broke, with Pavlov’s name attached in her indictment, it would be a national embarrassment. Russia’s Federal Security Service, she was betting, would have to cuff him.

Her gamble paid off. On April 11, 2022, Pavlov was reportedly arrested while trying to flee his home province of Vologda. The Germans, meanwhile, seized the Hydra bitcoin wallet, flush with $25 million.

That evening, the Miami DEA emptied out, and Lili was the last to leave the office. As she crossed the parking lot alone, a little melancholy stirred. For the first time in years, there was nothing to chase. She’d proven herself, outlasted the skeptics, and somehow, from a desk in Miami, taken revenge on the same kind of crooks who had haunted her childhood. But she wanted to share it with someone who really understood what it meant to her. She called Luis. When he picked up, she said quietly, “I did it.”

The following year, 2023, Lili received an award for Outstanding Investigative Accomplishments in Cybercrime from Homeland Security. The ceremony was in DC, a ballroom full of badges and banquet tables. Lili was handed a small Lucite desktop ornament with her achievement engraved into it.

It was strange to receive such an honor, as Lili was no longer a federal agent. The Hydra case had made her an even bigger star—at a money-laundering investigations conference in Bangkok, Lili was told by a former intelligence agent that surviving Hydra admins had placed a $4 million bounty on her—but she quit the DEA a few months later.

Lili was eager for something new. Plus it was a good time to get out. Lili’s former mentor, Jose Irizarry—the swaggering superstar who’d taught her the ropes—had been arrested a couple of years before Lili’s exit and quietly left his post. Then, in 2022, Irizarry granted the Associated Press a bombshell interview. He had been skimming millions from undercover ops and, alongside other agents, had partied his way through Madrid, Cartagena, and the Caribbean with the very traffickers he was meant to catch. There were yachts, Hublot watches, Tiffany rings, reported WhatsApp threads full of drunk agents trading porn and arranging sex tours on the government dime. Irizarry was not posing as a money launderer to catch the cartels; he was just laundering money for the cartels. Diego Marin, the alleged “Contraband Kingpin,” was not an informant but a collaborator. By the time Irizarry confessed, $9 million had vanished into thin air. It remains the most consequential arrest of a corrupt DEA agent. “The drug war’s a game,” Irizarry shrugged to an AP video crew. “A very fun game that we were playing.”

Lili had been shaken. Irizarry’s corruption was staggering. And he damaged the clever, deep-cover casework she’d been developing outside of Hydra, where Lili-as-Olga had been successfully enticing criminal organizations into the trap of crypto laundering—until that entire operation was compromised by Irizarry’s bust, since all those introductions had been made by Igor. As one of Irizarry’s confidential informants, he was now forever tainted. “You can’t win an unwinnable war,” Irizarry had told the Associated Press. Fuck you, Lili thought. It was one of several corruption scandals that tarnished the entire DEA. And now Lili was onstage representing that agency, in a dark suit, hair pulled tightly, smiling for photos.

Back in Miami, Lili wondered what was next. She was 32 years old, and for the first time she felt the uncertainty of that youth. Her hard edges were softening. The road ahead wasn’t a clear line of duty or a strict moral code. She’d spent her whole life trying to impose order on the chaos of her childhood. It gave her a world of binaries. The law provides justice. People love you or they betray you. But this belief set the stage for a lifetime of disappointments. She’d spent all these years seeing everyone’s flaws, but not her own.

She’d become deep friends with Luis. She reflected on the ways she hadn’t always been the easiest partner either, leaving him for Harvard with an infant while he was undercover. And as much trouble as Katya had always been, she was still Lili’s mother. Every time Katya seemed to fall off the wagon, Lili took her in. It felt liberating, the idea of mercy. In this light, she could even understand her grandmother and her inability to abandon her grandson, no matter his misdeeds. You can’t erase pain with structure, she learned. Or purity. Or anything. You can’t outthink heartbreak.

So what can you do? You can recognize that the world is messy; that people fail you, and you fail them. You make peace with ambiguity. You realize that love and imperfection can coexist. You see that knowing this is the real safety net. Institutions may not be the answer, but people are. You forgive your mother. You forgive your grandmother. You look in the rearview mirror and see that your daughter in the back seat is likable and chatty, a sociable spitfire, like her father. You raise her the best you can, the way your mother could not do for you, to see the world for all its possibilities.

For older adults, being able to move comfortably and perform everyday activities is essential for maintaining quality of life. Simple tasks such as standing up from a chair, climbing stairs, and walking can become more difficult as strength and balance decline.

According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, unintentional injuries, including those caused by falls, are among the leading causes of death in adults aged 65 and older.

Despite the importance of physical fitness, many older adults believe that meaningful improvements require long, demanding workouts. Christopher Sciamanna, professor of medicine and public health at Penn State College of Medicine and lead author of the study, says the new findings challenge that assumption. Even a brief daily routine can improve important measures of mobility and physical function.

"The human body is designed to improve very quickly," Sciamanna said. "And just a few repetitions of an exercise performed regularly can lead to huge improvements. Exercise is about forward thinking -- think about what you want to be able to do and train for it."

Why Shorter Workouts Could Make Exercise Easier

Strength training, also known as resistance training, involves making muscles work against some form of resistance, such as body weight or elastic bands. Research has shown that it can substantially improve muscle strength within a few months.

However, fewer than one in five older adults meet the recommendation to perform muscle strengthening activities at least twice a week. Common obstacles include discomfort, limited time, and the physical demands of conventional exercise programs.

Another challenge is that planning an effective workout can feel overwhelming, particularly for people who are unfamiliar with exercise.

"Exercise is actually really complicated, because you have to decide how many repetitions, how far, how many sets, how much rest, and how many times per week," said co-author Smita Dandekar, associate professor of pediatrics at Penn State College of Medicine. "It's hard work, so there's huge problems with people wanting to do exercise. If we can make it short, we're part way there."

The researchers had already seen encouraging results from an earlier experiment called FAST (Functional Activity Strength Training)-1. In that smaller study, 24 older adults performed just 30 seconds of push-ups and squats each day. After six months, their ability to perform squats had improved.

These findings led Sciamanna and his colleagues to investigate whether an especially brief exercise program could produce measurable improvements in older adults.

Improving performance does not always require a complete redesign or a complicated optimization framework. Many improvements come from understanding how browsers load resources, reducing unnecessary work, optimizing images, and delivering only the code a page actually needs.

The first step in performance optimization is understanding how a website behaves under real conditions.

A page may feel fast on a powerful development computer but perform poorly on a mobile device or a slower network. Measuring performance helps developers identify the actual source of the problem instead of making changes based on assumptions.

Three important Core Web Vitals help evaluate the user experience:

Largest Contentful Paint (LCP): Measures when the largest relevant content element becomes visible.

Interaction to Next Paint (INP): Measures responsiveness across user interactions.

Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS): Measures unexpected visual movement during page loading and use.

And yet, many rare-earth experts see a way forward, and it depends on a specific kind of ore, called monazite. The ore contains huge amounts of valuable rare earths, including neodymium, praseodymium, and dysprosium.

“The future is monazite,” declares Jack Lifton, an analyst, consultant, and author. Lifton, who has been studying the rare earths industry since 1962, has seen countless trends, companies, and technologies come and go, and he isn’t given to sweeping statements. But on the subject of monazites, he’s bullish. “Right now, in my opinion, monazite is the most important rare-earth ore,” says Lifton, who is also cochairman of the Critical Minerals Institute, an organization that’s promoting the creation of a North American supply chain.

That path forward isn’t going to be smooth or straight, Lifton and others are quick to acknowledge. Monazite typically contains radioactive thorium and uranium, which means its handling, storage, and processing are tightly regulated by government agencies. It can be processed only with special techniques and procedures, and the mildly radioactive wastes generated by processing must be stored in isolation indefinitely.

Nevertheless, major investment is now flowing to monazite mines and processing technologies. In the United States, Energy Fuels, which operates the country’s only conventional uranium mill, has developed its own extraction process and is producing mixed oxides of neodymium and praseodymium—and also pilot-scale quantities of dysprosium and terbium oxides, key heavy rare earths that are otherwise produced almost exclusively in China.

Energy Fuels is processing monazite into rare-earth oxides at its White Mesa Mill in southeastern Utah. The body of water at left in the image is a tailings impoundment, where radioactive waste from processing is stored.Energy Fuels

The company has spent more than US $200 million to secure monazite assets in Brazil and Madagascar, and plans to scale its rare-earth production to higher commercial levels, at an estimated cost of $410 million. Energy Fuels’ rare-earth ambitions received a significant boost in June, when the Trump administration announced that it had signed a $725 million conditional loan commitment with the company.

Meanwhile, though, a government-backed project in Saskatchewan, Canada, stands as a cautionary tale for monazite backers. After six years of construction and expenditures of more than CA $100 million (US $72 million), a processing plant there has produced only minuscule quantities of rare earths. The processing plant, built by the Saskatchewan Research Council, was supposed to be producing 40 tonnes of rare earths per month by the end of 2024. That didn’t happen. “Full operation,” with production of 400 tonnes of metal per year was planned for “early 2025.” That didn’t happen, either. The organization is now targeting late 2027—by which time the project will have cost more than CA $216 million, according to the Saskatchewan Research Council’s CEO, Mike Crabtree.

Outside of North America, major monazite projects are also planned by Astron Limited, Hastings Technology Metals, Iluka Resources, Mkango Resources, Sovereign Metals, Steenkampskraal Holdings, and Tronox, among others. Perth, Australia–based Lynas Rare Earths, the largest rare-earths company outside of China, is expanding its monazite mining and processing operations. Most notable is the Lynas mine at Mount Weld, Australia, one of the world’s top monazite resources; the ore is processed at a sprawling facility in Malaysia.

Of course, for countries outside of China, all this surging activity doesn’t guarantee a route to rare earths independence. It’s not even close. So the vital questions now are, What else has to happen to broaden and diversify the rare-earths supply chains? And can all of this be done safely?

Planet Earth has enormous quantities of monazite ore, in two different forms. In Australia, Canada, China, and South Africa, massive deposits of monazite take the form of veins of hard rock that run through mountains and calderas. Much of the new interest in monazite, though, is focused on a second type, called heavy mineral sands. The sands are the result of tens of millions of years of exposure to wind, water, and gravity crumbling mineral-rich rocks into grains, which then accumulated in coastal areas or alongside riverbeds.

A particularly desirable form of monazite ore is found in heavy mineral sands, such as these on a beach in Brazil. Travel Pix/Alamy

Within those grains, the monazite is relatively hard and chemically inert. The monazite might be just a few percent by weight, but it typically contains between 45 and 70 percent rare earths by weight, including, in many cases, all of the necessary rare earths for magnets: neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium, and terbium.

“The best distribution of rare-earth elements that you can find anywhere is in monazites,” says Constantine Karayannopoulos, former CEO of Neo Performance Materials and now a director of or adviser to several rare-earths firms. “The real money is in those four elements.” Dysprosium and terbium, both examples of heavy rare earths, are particularly sought after and are usually not present in the most commonly mined ore for rare earths, called bastnaesite.

There are vast deposits of monazite-bearing heavy mineral sands in Australia, Brazil, India, Madagascar, Mozambique, and South Africa, with smaller ones in China, Idaho, Indonesia, Malaysia, Sri Lanka, Thailand, the southeastern U.S., and Vietnam.

A geologist points out dark veins of hard-rock monazite ore in the Steenkampskraal mine in South Africa [top]. Ore samples from the mine are collected for analysis [above].Rodger Bosch/AFP/Getty Images

What’s more, collecting the ore simply involves dredging or strip-mining the sands, as opposed to the blasting, drilling, and heavy earth moving required to get at hard-rock ores. Then, too, the sands are already in granular form, so they don’t need to be crushed or ground, like rock ores do. Separating out the heavy minerals from the sands can usually be done at the mining site, using relatively cheap magnetic or gravity-based techniques, leaving a relatively high grade of concentrate for further processing.

And now the bad news about heavy mineral sands. “The problem is they tend to have a lot of thorium,” which is weakly radioactive, says James Kennedy, president of ThREE consulting, a rare-earths advisory firm based in Missouri. “The thorium is the single biggest problem” with using the sands, he says. “If you were mining and processing monazites for rare earths, you would end up with hundreds and eventually thousands of tons of thorium-containing radioactive residues” per processing facility. The only markets for thorium are a couple of tiny ones for welding rods and some optics, he says, so “you have to store it.” And under the rules for nuclear material set by the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission and the International Atomic Energy Agency, any stored radioactive material that doesn’t have a planned use or disposal pathway is considered nuclear waste. “So the [radioactive] liability suddenly far exceeds the value of anything you’ve mined and sold,” Kennedy says.

It wasn’t always this way. Up until the 1950s, monazite was the main ore mined for rare earths. Companies processed heavy mineral sands from Australia, Brazil, and India, and hard rock from South Africa, to produce rare earths for tracer ammunition, steel alloys, cigarette-lighter flints, and, later, color-TV phosphors.

The Mountain Pass mine and processing plants, in California, are the only large-scale rare-earth production facilities in the United States.David McNew/Getty Images

Several developments combined to shift rare-earths production from monazite to bastnaesite. In 1949, a vast deposit of bastnaesite ore was discovered at Mountain Pass in California. By the early 1960s, bastnaesite had eclipsed monazite as the primary rare-earth ore. In the 1990s, with rare-earth production shifting to China, the move toward bastnaesite continued with the exploitation of massive deposits in that country, such as those at Maoniuping, that were either entirely or mostly bastnaesite.

Now, though, any resurgence of rare-earth production outside of China may hinge on a monazite renaissance, and some observers are taking a hard look at the strict regulations that control the handling of thorium and other by-products of monazite processing. Those regulations, Kennedy insists, will throttle the rare-earth ambitions of the world outside China, unless governments take action now.

“You rationalize the regulations around thorium and you will end the rare-earth crisis in the U.S.,” he declares. “You’ll turn rare earths from something that’s always scarce to something that is common and just naturally flows out of other mining.”

A prime example of Kennedy’s vision already exists at Energy Fuels, based in Lakewood, Colo. It’s the only company in the U.S. licensed to process, handle, and store compounds of uranium, and among the very few licensed to store radioactive materials of any kind. Those permits give Energy Fuels the distinction of being the only U.S. entity allowed to process monazite ore for its rare earths.

“Supersacks” containing a tonne of neodymium-praseodymium oxides were loaded on pallets at Energy Fuels’ White Mesa Mill.Energy Fuels

The company is moving aggressively to capitalize on that distinction. Energy Fuels’ main business is still uranium—last year it produced nearly 500 tonnes of it. But it has already started selling rare-earth oxides, following a modest $22 million investment in a small rare-earth processing facility at White Mesa Mill in Utah, where it also processes uranium. In September 2025, the company announced that it had sold 1.2 tonnes of neodymium-praseodymium oxide to South Korea–based POSCO International Corp., a leading maker of permanent-magnet assemblies for EV motors. Around the same time, Energy Fuels disclosed that it had raised a further $700 million.

In December 2025, the company announced it had produced small quantities of dysprosium oxide. Tiny amounts of dysprosium are needed for motor magnets to ensure they keep functioning at high temperatures. Today, essentially all of the world’s dysprosium comes from China.

Energy Fuels says it can produce 1,000 tonnes of rare-earth oxide a year at White Mesa Mill. But its rare-earth production there currently shares facilities with the uranium operations, so they cannot both be produced at the same time. Its monazite comes from the chemical company Chemours, which mines heavy mineral sands in Florida and Georgia to produce titanium and zirconium. The monazite ore sold to Energy Fuels is what remains after Chemours strips out the minerals it needs, namely ilmenite and zircon.

The chemical company Chemours mines heavy mineral sands at sites in the U.S. states of Georgia [seen here] and Florida. Monazite extracted from the sands is sold to Lakewood, Colo.-based Energy Fuels, which processes the ore into rare-earth oxides.Chemours

“Effectively, monazites are waste,” Karayannopoulos points out. “So you don’t have to dig another mine” to produce them.

Chemours and Energy Fuels have agreements under which Chemours ships 500 tonnes of monazite annually to White Mesa Mill. Energy Fuels is also negotiating or already has deals that would give it access to enormous reserves of monazite ore in Australia, Brazil, and Madagascar. These will be essential for its ambitions to become a world-class supplier of rare-earth oxides.

Mark Chalmers, former CEO of Energy Fuels, led the uranium company’s entrance into the rare-earths business.Jesse Rieser/The New York Times/Redux

“We’re playing a long game,” says Mark Chalmers, who recently retired as Energy Fuels’ CEO and is now a consultant to the company. “We’re doing the engineering right now on a larger facility that will have the capacity to produce up to 6,000 tonnes per year” of rare-earth oxide, he said in an interview. For comparison, Lynas Rare Earths produced around 12,000 tonnes of rare-earth oxide in its most recent fiscal year.

In announcing the $725 million conditional loan commitment with Energy Fuels, the U.S. Defense Department’s Office of Strategic Capital said the deal would help Energy Fuels scale up its output of rare-earth oxides and also help realize the company’s plans to turn the oxides into metals, a key step preceding the fabrication of magnets.

To see what can go wrong with a monazite project, consider the project in Saskatoon, Canada, where the Saskatchewan Research Council (SRC) has built and demonstrated a pilot plant to produce rare-earth oxides from monazite ore. It’s the only rare-earth extraction and separation plant in the country. In an email, a spokesperson said the plant “is scheduled to be commissioned by year’s end and after a production ramp-up phase, operational within the calendar year 2027.” When in “full operation,” at a time not specified by the spokesperson, the plant will have a capacity somewhere between 400 and 600 tonnes of rare-earth metal per year.

But even that relatively modest output will depend on a reliable supply or “feedstock” of monazite. In an industry small enough that many of the top people are on a first-name basis, SRC’s inability to secure such a supply is an open secret. “Their problem is they haven’t got enough feedstock to run on a continuous basis, so they’re making sample runs,” a well-connected industry observer told IEEE Spectrum. (SRC declined to make an official available for this story.)

In April 2024, SRC announced it had reached agreements with a Vietnamese organization, the Hung Thinh Group, for 3,000 tonnes of rare-earth carbonate per year for five years. It’s unclear how much of that feedstock has made it to the SRC facility. Vietnamese government restrictions on the export of monazite and bastnaesite have been in effect since 2022 and became law this past January.

But the real twist here is that one of the largest, highest-grade monazite deposits in the world is just 800 kilometers from Saskatoon, near Alces Lake in Saskatchewan. It’s likely that the SRC facility was conceived and built with that monazite deposit in mind. After all, as a government-owned entity, SRC says its mission is “to help the people of Saskatchewan strengthen the economy with quality jobs and a secure environment.”

The Alces Lake deposit is owned by Appia Rare Earths & Uranium Corp. In an interview, Appia’s CEO, Tom Drivas, talked up the possibility of a link between SRC and Appia. “SRC would be our logical option because, you know, it’s in the same area, same province. They need the monazite. And if we can supply the monazite, that’s sort of a natural, right?”

It probably won’t happen, though. The logic of rare-earth mining is utterly different from the logic of mining a commodity like, say, copper. Multiple experts contacted by Spectrum suggested that the cost of developing a monazite mine at Alces Lake, including such necessary infrastructure as roadways, electricity, and water, would be in the hundreds of millions of dollars. Such costs, and the mercurial exigencies of the rare-earths market, make it unlikely that the government of Saskatchewan would commit that kind of money, even with help from the Canadian federal government. These factors help explain why Canada, which has some of the most attractive deposits of rare-earth ores on the planet, along with impressive mining companies and resources, has no operating mines for rare earths.

Meanwhile, SRC is pursuing other options. Under a deal announced in May with REalloys, in Euclid, Ohio, REalloys will spend $20.6 million for upgrades to SRC’s Saskatoon facility, while SRC develops technology to produce rare-earth metals from rare-earth oxides. The deal also gives REalloys the right to purchase up to 80 percent of the SRC plant’s output. Where will the feedstock come from? Hard to say, but REalloys also struck a deal with U.S. Critical Materials Corp. for 10 percent of the hypothetical output of a proposed mine at Sheep Creek, in Montana. Sheep Creek reportedly has substantial amounts of hard-rock monazite that’s unusually low in thorium. But it’s far from certain that those deposits will ever be mined. The proposal for developing a mine there, in a breathtakingly scenic area near the headwaters of the Bitterroot River, has attracted fierce opposition from environmental groups, outdoor enthusiasts, and local officials.

Taken together, SRC’s recent moves have left industry veterans nonplussed. “I’m not sure what their business model is,” said one. “They say, ‘we’re a research institute. Our mandate is to help industry.’ The reality is, they’re competing with industry.”

SRC has also drawn scrutiny for some questionable business practices. In 2022, SRC board member Dennis Fitzpatrick was fired after raising concerns about the qualifications of an engineer hired to manage and direct operations at SRC’s Saskatoon plant. The president and CEO of SRC at the time, Mike Crabtree, was responsible for the hire. A subsequent investigation of Fitzpatrick’s claims by the Canadian Broadcasting Corp. detailed a troubling web of relationships and practices surrounding the SRC’s rare-earths initiative. (Fitzpatrick did not respond to multiple emails.) For example, past business associates of Crabtree’s were reportedly appointed to positions of authority without any other candidates being considered and without their connections to Crabtree being made public.

In one case, in 2021, Crabtree disclosed that he was offering a venture capital firm, Westbridge Capital, an opportunity to invest in the rare-earths plant. But according to Fitzpatrick, Crabtree never mentioned that the VC firm was run by an SRC board member who also happened to be one of Crabtree’s business associates—or that Crabtree himself was a trustee of Westbridge and an investor in the company, as well as a paid advisor.

Putting aside SRC’s missteps, the organization does have one advantage: It holds multiple licenses from the Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission to handle, process, and manage radioactive materials. And that’s huge.

Most of the radioactive burden associated with processing monazite ore comes from substances produced during processing. In one of the early steps, pulverized, powdery ore is dried and then leached in strong acids—a process called cracking—to produce a rare-earth concentrate. Waste products of this step include a slurry containing thorium hydroxide and much smaller amounts of uranium hydroxide. The rare-earth concentrate then undergoes an extraction process to isolate specific rare earths, such as neodymium or dysprosium. More thorium and also radium are produced during these steps.

Waste barrels were photographed in 2024 at Energy Fuels’ White Mesa Mill, where ores containing uranium and rare-earths are processed and refined.Jesse Rieser/The New York Times/Redux

The thorium hydroxide slurry, meanwhile, is typically deposited in heavily regulated waste cells, called tailings impoundments or tailings ponds. The thorium hydroxide, either in the form of a slurry or a powder, must be stored in isolation from the environment indefinitely. Energy Fuels stores waste from its longstanding uranium production operations, as well as its new rare-earth operations, in tailings impoundments at its White Mesa Mill facility. The impoundments are just 10 kilometers from the community center of the Ute Mountain Ute Tribe, a group of Indigenous people, which has vigorously opposed operations at White Mesa Mill for more than 20 years.

Virtually all of the thorium is the isotope thorium-232, which is only weakly radioactive. Its radiation is alpha particles, which cannot penetrate human skin. Nevertheless, improper storage can have serious consequences, particularly if the thorium is inhaled or ingested.

“Have a look at the gravitational separation of HMS [heavy mineral sands] in Nigeria,” Thomas Kruemmer, a rare-earth consultant and analyst, wrote in an email. “There is lots of dust in the Nigerian processing sites, which the workers inevitably breathe in. And inside your respiratory system there isn’t any protective skin, so alpha radiation develops its full potential. This is something many people do not consider.”

In 2012, protestors in Malaysia [top] rallied against the Lynas rare earths processing facility [above], then under construction near Kuantan. Left: AFP/Getty Images; Right: Goh Seng Chong/Bloomberg/Getty Images

Moreover, adds Kruemmer, “it is not thorium itself. It is the decay isotopes of thorium that are highly radioactive.” One decay product of thorium is polonium, he points out, “the preferred poison for [killing] Russian dissidents.”

In 1982, a company called Asia Rare Earth, a subsidiary of Mitsubishi Chemical, began extracting yttrium from monazite ore in Perak, Malaysia. Local residents soon began complaining of various illnesses. With the consent of the Malaysian government, the company had built a waste channel to a nearby disposal site, where the waste was stored in open steel barrels and in an unlined waste pond. The waste overflowed from the pond whenever it rained heavily. In 1984, a Japanese professor measured radiation levels at and near the pond and found them to be, in the most extreme cases, hundreds of times higher than internationally accepted maximums. A subsequent investigation found extremely high levels of birth defects and eight cases of leukemia in the small community near the waste dump.

In 2011, at age 69, Lai Kwan prepared to bathe her 29-year-old son, Cheah Kok Leong [top]. Cheah Kok was born with severe mental disabilities that Lai Kwan believed were caused by exposure to radiation that she received while working at a rare earths processing plant in Bukit Merah, Malaysia [bottom, photographed during its disassembly].Rahman Roslan/The New York Times/Redux

A protracted legal fight among local residents and Asia Rare Earth and the Malaysian government culminated in the closure of the plant in 1992. By that time, so much radioactive material had been carelessly dumped that cleanup took more than two decades and cost Mitsubishi an estimated $100 million. The episode still haunts the rare-earths industry in Malaysia, where Lynas now processes monazite concentrate at the largest rare earths processing facility in the world. That plant, in Kuantan in the state of Pahang, has been the subject of organized and active local opposition that began even before its commissioning in 2012. The Malaysian government has sporadically tightened its regulations on the plant, stipulating that, for example, radioactive wastes be shipped out of Malaysia or that the processing stages that create radioactive residues be accomplished outside of Malaysia.

Kennedy notes that if monazite ore is to become a major source of rare earths, much larger quantities of thorium will need to be disposed, probably more than could be economically accommodated in lined and isolated tailings ponds.

One of the very few options for using such large amounts of thorium involves nuclear power. Years ago, countries including the U.S., Canada, Norway, and Germany had thorium-reactor research programs, and India has had a slow-moving program for decades. But today, the only operating thorium power reactor is in China. The Shanghai Institute of Applied Physics of the Chinese Academy of Sciences has been operating, since 2023, a 2-megawatt molten-salt-cooled reactor that is thorium-fueled.

The world’s only operating thorium reactor is a 2-megawatt unit at the Shanghai Institute of Applied Physics of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. In October 2025, technicians used mechanical arms to analyze samples of the reactor’s molten-salt fuel.Jin Liwang/Xinhua/Getty Images

A couple of Western startups are keeping the thorium reactor dream alive. A Franco-Dutch company called Thorizon says it has designed a reactor that would be fueled by a mixture of thorium and spent nuclear fuel. Startup Copenhagen Atomics is working on a 1-MW reactor that’s a prototype for a 100-MW, mass-manufacturable, molten-salt reactor.

Processing of monazite ore could provide fuel for an enormous number of thorium reactors far into the future. Consider a single large facility producing 5,000 tonnes of rare-earth oxide a year. If the monazite contains 7 percent thorium, the plant would also be producing roughly 700 tonnes of thorium a year. According to Rusty Towell, a professor at Abilene Christian University in Texas who directs an advanced-reactor project there, a tonne of thorium would be sufficient to generate about 350 MW (electric) for a year. So the large processing facility alone would be throwing off enough thorium annually to keep 2,450 100-MW reactors operating for a year.

That amount of power—245 gigawatts—is about half of all the generating capacity growth forecast for the United States over the next decade, a period during which analysts expect accelerating electricity growth due to increasing numbers of data centers and electric vehicles.

Virtually all rare-earths experts foresee a growing role for monazite. But given the problems with thorium, local opposition to some projects, and political instabilities connected to others, some have muted expectations. “The sole and single monazite hope rests with Iluka’s expensive project in Australia, IMHO,” writes Kruemmer. Iluka Resources, based in Perth, is now building a massive rare-earths refining facility at Eneabba, 275 km north of Perth. It will process a million-tonne monazite stockpile that accumulated over decades from the company’s other mining operations. The cost of the project is estimated at AU $1.8 billion (about US $1.3 billion).

In a US $1.3 billion project, Iluka Resources is building a massive facility in Eneabba, Australia, to extract and separate rare earths from a million-tonne stockpile of monazite, visible in the background of this photo.Iluka Resources

For other experts, though, the biggest challenges facing monazite projects isn’t thorium, or radiation, or regulations. It’s the blend of quirks, misconceptions, and self-dealing that dog the entire rare-earths industry.

“The rare-earth mining business is the Afghanistan of mining,” quips Kennedy. It’s a place where people and companies go with grand ideas, only to become ensnared in quagmires.

One fundamental problem is the highly speculative nature of almost every aspect of the rare-earths business, which lends itself to backroom deals. In particular, the industry is plagued by speculators who buy stakes in a project with no intention of staying with the project through completion; they cash out as soon as they can profit from any future favorable news—a technique known as “pump and dump.”

Consider the Trump administration’s decision to take a 10 percent stake in USA Rare Earth in exchange for $277 million and to provide a $1.3 billion secured loan to the company. USA Rare Earth has the rights to a deposit called Round Top near Sierra Blanca, Texas. The company hired the Wall Street firm Cantor Fitzgerald to raise $1 billion in equity financing for the deal. Cantor Fitzgerald, as it happens, is run by the sons of Howard Lutnick, Donald Trump’s Commerce Secretary.

Meanwhile, there is no rare-earths mine at Round Top, and it’s a distinct possibility that there never will be.

“The current administration consistently fails to seek expert advice and is recklessly pouring billions of taxpayer dollars into speculative [rare-earth element] processes and projects, like Round Top, that have little chance of commercial success,” says Terry McNulty, a mining consultant based in Tucson.

Geoff Atkins, vice president of business development of the Canadian rare-earths firm Ucore Rare Metals, had advice for his colleagues at a convention of the Critical Minerals Institute in 2022. “Instead of focusing on your speculative value,” he said, “instead of being able to say we’ve got the biggest project in the world, instead of, ‘we’ve got a [net present value] of $3 billion or $4 billion,’ how about you say, ‘we’ve got a project which is going to get into operation, and this operation is going to provide the greatest value to our shareholders in the long term, not just in the short term when we can pump this up and, you know, the [early] investors can sell out.

“Because unless there is that shift,” he warned, “we’re not going to have any hope of getting the volumes we need to give the certainty to the downstream supply chain.”

In short, the rare-earths industry outside of China needs to grow up. And sooner rather than later.
